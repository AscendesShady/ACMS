1.1  What is machine learning

There are many definitions of machine learning or machine learning
algorithms. A widely used definition is as follows (→Mitchell, 1997): “A
computer program is said to learn from experience E with respect to
some class of tasks T and performance measure P, if its performance at
tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E.” The task is
generally one that cannot be performed or is difficult to perform with a
program with a specific and fixed set of instructions. For example, in
transportation engineering, it could be the task of identifying traffic
zones that are more prone to accidents, given the fact that the zones
represent widely varying conditions of climate, pavement, design,
vehicles, and speed.

  Traditionally, solutions to many transportation-related problems have
been attempted with the use of statistics. However, machine learning
enables us to tackle complex problems, especially nonlinear problems
and those with many variables, without having to meet many of the
criteria/assumptions for the validity of the statistical models. One of the
key differences between the traditional statistical and machine learning
approaches is that the former tries to achieve an explanation of the
problem using established rules and assumptions, whereas the latter
tries to perform the task of prediction more accurately, for the specific
problem at hand. This approach of machine learning is more similar to
the application of human intelligence for solving any problem.

  One alternative way to define machine learning is the field of
developing and applying algorithms that are applied to datasets from
different fields, with different numbers of variables (features) and
observations, learning from the data, and then performing the required
task on the test data (→Figure 1.1).
Figure 1.1: Concept of the application of machine learning (ML).

The performance P is generally measured in terms of accuracy, which has
different forms of mathematical representation, depending on the
specific addressed problem. For example, in a case where the objective is
to classify objects into different groups, the accuracy could be the
proportion of correct or incorrect (error) classification, whereas for a
numerical prediction problem, it could be the coefficient of
determination. However, the design of the performance measure (and,
specifically, what to measure) must be made with a consideration of the
problem and the seriousness or consequence of making an error.

  The experience, E, refers to learning from the data, which comes from
a dataset. The dataset can be presented in different formats; a common
way is to represent features in columns and observations in rows. The
features are the properties of the observed data, which are measured in
some quantitative or qualitative scale. The properties may or may not be
linked to a specific label or target – which can help formulate the
problem as “supervised” or “unsupervised,” respectively. If the dataset Z
is represented by 100 observations with 3 features, Z can be represented
as Z ∈ R100X3 .

1.2  Types of machine learning algorithms

Machine learning can be broadly classified into supervised and
unsupervised learning.

  Supervised learning: In supervised learning, the features or
attributes are associated with a target or label. For example, the widths
of different types of cracks are associated with the level of cracking in
pavements. In this type of learning, predictive models are built that relate
a set of input variables to a response variable, which could be discrete
(classification model) or continuous (numerical prediction model). In this
process, the models are trained to either classify or predict numerical
values. Methods include Naïve Bayes algorithm, support vector machines
(SVMs) and artificial neural networks (ANN).

  Unsupervised learning: In this type of learning, models are built to
cluster a group of observations on the basis of the structure of the
dataset. The K-means unsupervised algorithm is used to group or
“cluster” data points around their centroids. The objective is to group or
cluster them in such a way that the total variance within the clusters is
minimized (objective function). The process is started by assigning
random centroids, assigning the data to these centroids, and forming the
clusters. The algorithm determines the new centroids for these clusters
and then iterates the above process until cluster assignments of the data
do not change.

  In the case of unsupervised learning, the objective is to understand
the structure of the dataset and/or to separate the data into different
groups based on the similarity of the features. Supervised learning has
two primary applications such as regression and classification, whereas
unsupervised learning is used for clustering the data.

1.3  Core concepts in machine learning

1.3.1  Classification

k-Nearest neighbor (KNN): This method utilizes the concept of “similarity”
or “closeness” in the classification of samples. It does not require the
assumption of any specific type of underlying distribution of the samples.
The principle of this method is that when we have a new observation or
sample, we look for the majority class of the NN of this new sample, and
then label it as part of that class (→Figure 1.2). The nearest neighbor is
found based on distance metrics such as Euclidean distance. The

Naïve Bayes: This is a classification method (→Figure 1.3) in which the
predictors within each class are assumed to be independent of each
other. This simplifies the estimation of the probability. In this case,
separate distributions can be considered for each class, and the
observations from each class are considered to be samples from the
probability distribution of that class. Bayes’ theorem of conditional
probability helps us to estimate the probability of an observation coming
from a given class, given its location. Then based on the highest
probability (of co-occurring in a specific group or cluster), the new
observation is classified in that group. One advantage of using the
distributions rather than the individual observations (as in KNN) in
making the classification is that the process is relatively less sensitive to
noise in the training data.

  In the Bayes’ classifier approach, the probability of a specific output
(or target) is calculated from the input data with several features, and
then for a test case with the given features, the target label is assigned to
that, which shows the highest probability in the input data. This approach
becomes problematic when there is a relatively large number of features,
and the probability for a specific test case that matches a certain
combination of all features (i.e., present within the input data) may end
up being zero, and hence the test case cannot be assigned to any specific
group. To overcome this, the Naïve Bayes classifier is used, where the
probabilities for classes of each individual feature are calculated and then
combined (product of the probabilities) to determine the class for the
test case. The assumption is that input variables (features) are
independent of each other.
Figure 1.3: Concept of the naïve Bayes approach.

Support Vector Machine (SVM): In this method (→Figure 1.4), the
classification of observations (in general, binary classification) is made by
identifying the best hyperplane that separates the different samples in a
relatively better way, and hence makes a better classification of the
samples. In this approach, the margin, that is, the gap between the
boundary of the classification and the nearest observation, is maximized,
with a possible “penalty or loss function” for observations that are not
classified properly. It is referred to as the SVM since the observations
(referred to as support vectors) that are closest to the boundary dictate
the determination of the best solution. This method can be used for
nonlinear classification problems by transforming the variables to a
linearly separable space. The kernel function that is applied to solve this
type of problem could be expressed as the dot product of the features
(vectors) in an expanded feature space.

Figure 1.4: Concept of the SVM approach.

1.3.2  Numerical prediction

This is a supervised learning method in which numerical values (target
values) are predicted based on an algorithm that trains on a dataset with
input and output (target values). The model that is developed with the
help of the algorithm can be used to predict target or output values from
new (unseen) input values. The techniques used in this method include
linear regression, decision trees, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs).

  In linear regression, the best fit equation is derived to obtain a
relation between dependent and independent variables by minimizing
the errors of prediction (→Figure 1.5).

Figure 1.5: Concept of linear regression.
In the decision tree method (→Figure 1.6), the prediction is made step by
step as if going through a tree from its roots (node) to the leaf (node)
through its branches. This prediction is controlled by the number of
branches and the values of the training weights that are assigned to the
branches. In general, the decision tree approach is used for cases where
high accuracy is not a criterion and relatively low memory usage is
desired.
Figure 1.6: Concept of the decision tree approach.

In the case of ANNs, layers of “neurons” (circles in →Figure 1.7) are used
to input the data, process them, and then obtain the output, similar to a
structure that is utilized by the brain. The connections between the
neurons are created with randomly assigned weights of the linkages,
which are continuously updated until the error between the actual data
and model prediction is minimized. Weights and biases are utilized to
transmit the data through the layers in an optimized manner to minimize
the difference between the predicted and the given target value during
the training process, for which different algorithms are available.
Figure 1.7: Structure of an artificial neural network.

Convolutional neural network (CNN): This is the algorithm that is
utilized for deep learning involving images and video. It consists of the
following main layers that perform different tasks of feature detection
and classification:

  1. CNN is the convolutional layer that identifies different features.
  2. Rectified linear unit maps negative and positive values.
  3. Pooling reduces the number of parameters required for the

  network by performing nonlinear downsampling.
  4. Fully connected layer provides the probabilities of classification in

  each of the different classes.
  5. Softmax function outputs the predicted probability of each class.

There are three modes of computation for deep learning: CPU, GPU, and
cloud-based computation. CPU-based computation is simple but takes
time and is applicable for small deep learning datasets, whereas GPU is
more efficient. The cloud-based approach uses cloud resources to
conduct the computation without the need to rely on local computational
resources.
  Hyperparameters: The ANN structure is defined by a set of variables
that are collectively known as hyperparameters. These variables include
the learning rate, weight utilization, loss function, and layer size.
Learning rate is the step size that is used to refine the model to reduce
the error/loss, as the model weights are updated. At each increment of
the learning rate (linear or exponential), the error is measured, and a
low/high learning rate causes slow/fast progress. Therefore, it is
desirable to have an optimal learning rate, which is dictated by the
algorithm and the data. The concept is illustrated with respect to
reaching a minimum in →Figure 1.8. For low learning rates, the
improvement in accuracy (decrease in loss) will be insignificant, whereas
for a high learning rate, the loss function will tend to increase, without
reaching a minimum.

1.3.3  Dimension reduction

Principal component analysis (PCA): In this technique, the data is
transformed to capture the variance in a few components, of which the
first one captures the most variance (→Figure 1.9). The principal
components are those along which most of the variations of the
observations can be explained. The first principal component vector
represents the one that is closest to the observations; that is, if the data
are plotted along this component, the sum of the squared distances
between the line and the observations is the minimum. Generally, many
features are minimized to three or less number of components (principal
components) so that the data can be plotted with respect to those
components in a two-dimensional or three-dimensional plot. The basic
purpose of PCA is to obtain a low-dimensional representation of the data,
ensuring that most of the variation in the data or information about the
data is captured.

Figure 1.9: Concept of the PCA.

1.3.3.1  Data modeling process
Generally, the data is presented in a CSV or an Excel file with labels for
supervised learning or without labels for unsupervised learning. The first
two steps consist of checking the data for outliers and missing values.
Missing values can be substituted with interpolate values, if appropriate.
Part of the data could be considered for developing the model (termed
as training data), and the remaining part could be further subdivided
into validation and test data. However, as we will see, in most cases the
splitting of the data into different groups is often performed by the
specific machine learning algorithm that is used for the analysis of the
data. The next important step is the identification of the features of the
data that could be used by the algorithm to classify or model the data.
The important features are those that can be used effectively as
predictors of the responses – using a model with a minimum number of
features makes it more useful and robust. Techniques such as correlation
matrix or PCA could be utilized to select the more important features.

  Cross-validation is used to evaluate the accuracy of a model by using
an unknown dataset, which is different from the training dataset. This
unknown dataset can be separated from the training dataset by hold-out
cross-validation or by resampling from a specific (k) number of subsets of
the data, training against all but one (k–1) and validating with the
remaining subset. The method is then repeated k number of times.

  To determine the minimum value of the function, one common
method that is used in machine learning is the gradient descent
approach. Similar to finding the bottom of a valley by walking (without
seeing, such as at night), this approach utilizes an iterative process with
partial derivatives by which each step is supposed to bring you closer to
the desired lowest point. With the objective of minimizing the error or
difference between the actual and the predicted value, the learning rate
and direction are iteratively updated to calculate the slope.

  The weights and biases used for the prediction could be adjusted
with the gradient descent approach. The slope at any point of a curve
could be visualized to obtain progressively smaller gradients as the
lowest point of a curve is approached. In the batch gradient descent
approach, the error from the entire training dataset is considered,
whereas in the stochastic gradient descent approach, the updates are
done for each training data. Finally, in the minibatch gradient descent
approach, small batches of the training data are used for updating the
parameters.

  The goal of the gradient descent approach is to minimize the cost
function or the error between the actual and the predicted values. The
learning rate and the cost function are utilized to achieve this objective.
The cost or the loss function is the difference between the actual and the
predicted values at any position, for the entire dataset and for one data,
respectively. It is minimized to find the minima. The algorithm
continuously iterates along the steepest descent direction to reach the
minima.

1.4  Fundamentals of probability, statistics, and
linear algebra

1.4.1  Statistical concepts

Statistics is needed for processing and analyzing data, understanding
patterns in the data, and interpreting and presenting the results of
analysis. Statistics can be descriptive and inferential; descriptive statistics
consider entire datasets and give outputs to characterize the data.
Examples are maximum, minimum, and average.

  Population refers to the entire data, whereas a sample refers to a
subset of the data. The subset can be selected from the population
according to a defined procedure in statistics. Inferential statistics enable
us to make predictions based on subsets of the data, especially when the
total population of the data is huge. The use of appropriate inferential
statistics conducted on a subset of the data should provide outputs that
would be very close to what would be obtained by considering the entire
population of the data.

  The data can be primarily of two types: numerical and categorical.
Examples of numerical data are temperature data: recorded
temperature values such as daily high temperatures in a city (e.g., 70 °F,
85 °F, and 62 °F). Examples of categorical data are vehicle types:
categories include “Sedan,” “SUV,” “Truck,” “Motorcycle,” and “Bicycle.”

  Aggregation helps us in grouping the data. In the case of numerical
data, aggregation is possible, such as obtaining the sum, minimum,
maximum, and standard deviation. Such aggregation is not possible in
the case of categorical data, such as ID, name, and rank. Numerical data
could be discrete or continuous. Discrete data is countable in a finite
amount of time (e.g., money), whereas continuous data is measurable,
but it takes forever to count (e.g., age). In the case of categorical data,
