10.3.3 Integration of AIoT into vehicle systems

This integration of AI and IoT in vehicle systems represents a landmark develop-
ment for the automotive industry as traditional vehicles turn smart and autonomous.
Evolution increases the safety, efficiency, and user experience in these systems and
adds to sustainable mobility. The main things to be looked into regarding this
integration are as follows:

l Autonomous Navigation: Autonomous navigation in the autonomous vehicle
  is a very complex phenomenon involving many up-to-date AI algorithms,
  sensors such as LiDAR, cameras, radar, and ultrasonic sensors, all of which
  collect all this information in real time to help the vehicle understand where it
  is and what the surroundings are through techniques such as SLAM and object
  detection. These AI algorithms optimise routes based on distances, traffic
  conditions, and preferences of the users and are also allowed to do dynamic
  rerouting based on changes in traffic scenarios. It maintains a safe distance and
  does speed adjustments through adaptive speed control. The vehicle adapts to
  traffic signals and events like pedestrians or emergency vehicles. The system
  also constantly learns from every journey to enable it to better navigate while
  enhancing safety and efficiency during transportation.

l Predictive Maintenance: One of the most critical advantages of AIoT in vehicles
  is predictive maintenance. It allows the system to monitor and analyse its opera-
  tional health in real time by continuously processing data from various sensors
  installed in vehicles. The critical parameters monitored involve engine tempera-
  ture, oil condition, brake functionality, and tyre pressure. Advanced AI algorithms
  that the cars can use to scrutinise all this data may look for patterns and predict
  anomalies associated with potential failure. The system can give an alarm to the
  driver when there is a variance in the tyre pressure past the critical threshold,
  where a probable blowout would be in the offing. It not only avoids sudden
  breakdowns but reduces the periods of downtime substantially. As a result, this
  enables timely repairs before breakdowns become catastrophic, reducing the need
  for emergency maintenance. Moreover, predictive maintenance addresses pro-
  blems before they become major issues, thus optimising the lifespan of the vehicle
  and overall reliability, which leads to reduced maintenance costs for owners.

l User Experience: AIoT in vehicles fundamentally changes user experience
  through highly personalised features that respond to individual driver
206 AIoT for smart transportation

  preferences and habits. With continuous learning and data generation, an AI
  system has the capability to analyse its customer’s interaction with it during
  driving time and could tailor the settings for a convenient, comfortable, as well
  as safe ride experience. For example, if often someone sets the climate controller
  on a particular temperature or likes particular types of music, when the system
  perceives that person, would do all these automatically on setting the vehicle.
  Other benefits include personalised navigation features, which can also suggest
  routes based on historical routes, traffic patterns, and even time of day, thus
  making travel much more efficient and enjoyable. This degree of personalisation
  makes the overall driving experience more intuitive and in line with the driver’s
  lifestyle, hence strengthening the driver’s relationship with the car.
l Traffic Management and Efficiency: AIoT integration is crucial for promoting
  traffic management and efficiency in that it allows vehicles to communicate
  freely with traffic management systems and other vehicles. Through V2I and
  V2V communication, autonomous vehicles can share with traffic lights and other
  users’ real-time data about speed, location, and manoeuvres. This information can
  then be used by the traffic management systems to optimise the traffic signals and
  make timely updates on road conditions, congestion, and accidents so that vehi-
  cles can make changes in their routes and speeds. For example, if a traffic jam is
  sensed ahead, the system can reroute the vehicles to minimise delays, hence
  improving overall traffic flow. With the use of real-time data and analytics, AIoT
  deals with congestion, reduces travel times, and enhances road safety, thereby
  making the transportation network efficient and better organised.

Abstract

Intelligent transportation systems (ITS) use information and communication tech-
nologies (ICT) to manage traffic and various modes of transportation. They provide
innovative services such as vehicle assistance, systems that automatically notify
emergency services, and apps that offer alternative transportation options.
Considering that ITS networks are growing every day, the presence of blockchain
technology, which operates effectively on both closed and open networks, and
cybersecurity technology to protect networks from cyber threats should be taken
into account. Blockchain technology positively impacts ITS by ensuring that data
shared across transportation networks is integrated, transparent, and secure. For
instance, blockchain can secure transactions within ITS, such as parking fees and
toll payments, preventing fraud and manipulation. ITS systems, which involve
intense data exchange and continuous network connectivity, are vulnerable to
threats such as data theft, ransomware, and DDoS attacks. Cybersecurity in ITS
should be based on confidentiality, integrity, and availability and these concepts
should be protected by relevant cybersecurity products. The ITS network should be
constantly monitored and log records should be maintained and reviewed with the
help of security information and event management (SIEM) systems or security
operation center (SOC). Consequently, integrating blockchain and cybersecurity
technologies with ITS not only enhances the security, efficiency, and effectiveness
of these systems but also significantly boosts user trust in the network. Together,
they form a key component for developing future-ready transportation systems, that
are secure, reliable, and resilient against both physical and cyber threats. This
chapter will analyse how blockchain and cybersecurity technologies can be applied
to ITS.

11.1 Introduction

Intelligent transportation systems (ITS) have emerged as a crucial component of con-
temporary urban infrastructure, offering innovative solutions designed to enhance
sustainability, efficiency, and security in transportation. ITS is designed to enhance
traffic flow, optimise travel times, and mitigate environmental impacts through the
augmentation of information and data exchange between vehicles and infrastructure.
Nevertheless, this extensive network structure and data sharing render the system
susceptible to cybersecurity threats. Continuously connected vehicles and infrastructure
are susceptible to various cybersecurity threats, including data breaches, ransomware
attacks, and distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) incidents. Consequently, safe-
guarding the principles of confidentiality, integrity, and availability in ITS is of para-
mount importance. The security of ITS networks necessitates continuous monitoring
through advanced security solutions, such as security information and event manage-
ment (SIEM) and security operations centres (SOC), to facilitate immediate threat
detection. In this context, the integration of blockchain technology into ITS emerges as
a significant innovation for enhancing security and maintaining data integrity. This
study aims to contribute to the safety, resilience, and efficiency of future transportation
systems by examining the potential integration of blockchain technology and cyber-
security solutions to ensure data security and privacy within ITS.

11.4 Communication technologies

Connected vehicles use various communication technologies to exchange data
with other vehicles, infrastructure, or pedestrians. Inter-vehicle communication
can be classified as Dedicated Short-Range Communications (DSRC) or cellular
vehicle-to-everything (C-V2X) wireless communication technologies based on
cellular networks (4G-LTE and 5G-NR). These two types of communication are
explained below.

11.4.1 Dedicated short-range communications

DSRC and ITS-G5 are two-way wireless communications technologies designed
for short- to medium-range interactions that enable direct communication between
vehicles and other objects without relying on cellular networks or other infra-
structure. The IEEE 802.11p standard is specifically designed to implement V2V
and V2I communications over short distances. Europe uses the ITS-G5 standard,
whereas the United States uses the DSRC standard.

11.4.2 Cellular vehicle-to-everything communications

C-V2X is a technology that is considered an industry standard for vehicular com-
munications. This communication model is standardised by the Third Generation
Partnership Project (3GPP) and is based on cellular network technologies such as
LTE/4G and NR/5G.

  C-V2X establishes a communication framework that enables vehicles to
interact with various entities, including other vehicles, pedestrians, roadside infra-
structure, and mobile networks. This technology utilises two interrelated commu-
nication modes, referred to as ‘PC5’ and ‘Uu’, to enhance connectivity across a
broad transportation network. The ‘PC5’ mode, recognised as direct communica-
tion, operates independently of cellular networks, allowing vehicles to connect
directly with other participants. In contrast, the ‘Uu’ mode, which depends on
network communication, enables vehicles to tap into mobile networks for timely
information about road conditions and traffic situations [24].

  5G-V2X communication can be engineered to integrate a range of sophisti-
cated technologies, such as massive MIMO, device-to-device communication,
multiple radio access technologies, full-duplex radios, mmWave, cloud computing,
  Blockchain technology and cyber security in ITS 221

and Software Defined Networks (SDN) [25]. Millimeter waves are defined as
frequencies beginning at 24 GHz and extending upwards. Although mmWave
bands are characterised by a restricted coverage area, they provide remarkably
high bandwidth and rapid data transmission rates. Furthermore, full-duplex
radios enable simultaneous transmission and reception within the same
frequency band.

  5G NR-V2X has the potential to reach impressive data transmission rates of up
to 20 gigabits per second (Gbit/s) and offers ultra-low latency, estimated at around
1 millisecond. This advanced technology enables the transfer of both unprocessed
and processed information gathered from vehicle sensors, as well as real-time video
feeds while accommodating a significantly higher number of simultaneous device
connections. Furthermore, it is engineered to manage multiple simultaneous con-
nections effectively in densely populated environments [24].

11.5 Vehicular networks

Vehicle networks (Vehicular ad-hoc network-VANET) represent essential ele-
ments in the development of architectures for ITS [26]. The core principle of this
specialised vehicle network is based on the connectivity of vehicles to share
information with one another and with RSUs. In the context of VANET, two dis-
tinct communication models are employed: the V2V communication model, which
facilitates interactions among vehicles, and the V2I communication model, which
enables connections to RSUs.

  The On-Board Unit (OBU) used in connected vehicles is responsible for
recording traffic and driving data. Another component of VANET is the RSU.
RSUs are the year-end unit and also provide connectivity to central units to receive
traffic and safety information.

  The design of Vehicle Ad-hoc Networks (VANET) enables both vehicles and
RSUs to function as mobile nodes or wireless routers, thereby improving con-
nectivity between vehicles and facilitating the establishment of a broad network. The
performance of VANET is significantly affected by the density of vehicles and RSUs
within a given region. Nonetheless, urban settings present challenges, including
congested traffic, high-rise structures, and unpredictable driving patterns, which may
impede the effective operation of VANET systems.

  A prominent challenge encountered by VANETs is their struggle to effectively
manage the substantial amounts of data generated by vehicles and RSUs. In
response to this issue, the IoV has been proposed as a solution. This framework
leverages internet-based services, including data storage and computational
resources, to enhance road safety, optimise traffic management, and provide info-
tainment options, thereby seamlessly integrating internet capabilities into the
vehicular network. The IoV is fundamentally based on the principles established by
the IoT [27–29].

  The IoV represents a comprehensive and interconnected network framework
distinguished by its enhanced manageability, controllability, and reliability. This

system encompasses a diverse array of users, vehicles, objects, and networks, all
functioning cohesively. It leverages advanced technologies, including artificial
intelligence, deep learning, cloud computing, and big data analytics, to facilitate its
operations. The ideal goal for IoV is to achieve deep integration among people,
vehicles, objects, and their surroundings, thereby reducing social costs, enhancing
transportation efficiency, improving the service level of cities, and ensuring that
individuals are satisfied with and enjoy their vehicles [7].

11.6 Security in intelligent transportation systems

ITS and connected vehicles, along with their positive features, also bring security
risks. The connectivity of vehicles can expose them to attacks from the outside
world. In this case, new and increasing attack vectors will emerge, allowing
security vulnerabilities to be exploited by malicious third parties.

  The sensors and other technologies embedded in connected vehicles facilitate
the generation of substantial amounts of data pertaining to the vehicle. For instance,
onboard diagnostics (OBD) systems gather diagnostic data regarding various
engine components, including fuel levels, ignition systems, transmissions, and
emissions controls. Furthermore, automotive manufacturers and aftermarket part
suppliers are adding sensors to other vehicle components – traditionally monitored
through OBD systems, such as windshield wipers and headlights. Tyre pressure
monitoring systems serve as an example, employing sensors to continuously
monitor the pressure in each tyre and alerting the driver when it falls below a
specified threshold. Geolocation devices collect data that identifies a vehicle’s
geographic location, while advanced versions of geographic tracking accumulate
detailed information regarding vehicle usage, such as driving behaviour and his-
torical locations [30].

  Connected and autonomous vehicles are estimated to possess processing power
equivalent to approximately 20 personal computers, contain around 100 million
lines of programming code, and generate data ranging from 3.2 gigabytes to 32
terabytes daily [31]. As the volume of data produced by connected vehicles
increases, concepts related to big data and the privacy and security of this data
become critical within ITS. Additionally, there is a growing diversity in the types of
data that can be collected and processed. For instance, the proliferation of ITS and
connected vehicles leads to the generation of various data types and quantities,
including traffic speed, traffic volume, travel times, transit passenger counts, and
vehicle locations.

  Today, many vehicles utilise sensors to measure data related to driving beha-
viour, such as accelerometers, Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), or
cameras, as well as the functioning of systems like the engine, air conditioning, and
airbags. These sensors can be either embedded (i.e., provided by the vehicle’s
internal system) or externally sourced (e.g., connected through an external device
like a smartphone). However, they also gather significant amounts of personal data
  Blockchain technology and cyber security in ITS 223

about drivers, passengers, and other road users, raising a wide array of privacy and
data protection concerns.

  Most of the data generated within the vehicle relates to identified or identifi-
able individuals. For instance, data may include directly identifying information
(e.g., the driver’s identity, fingerprints) and indirectly identifying information such
as details of trips taken, vehicle usage data, or technical specifications of the
vehicle that can be cross-referenced with the Vehicle Identification Number (VIN).
Additionally, information related to driving behaviour, such as driving style or
distance travelled, as well as data regarding wear and tear on vehicle parts, or
information collected by cameras, may indirectly pertain to the behaviour of the
driver concerning other individuals present inside or outside the vehicle.

  Attacks targeting connected vehicles can lead to traffic accidents, financial
losses, and the exposure of sensitive and/or personal data. Due to the potential risks
posed by these attacks to the safety and privacy of vehicle occupants and all other
road users, appropriate security measures must be implemented to mitigate
these risks.

  In light of these considerations, privacy and security in vehicles emerge as
crucial concerns. Below are some potential threats arising from possible attacks by
hackers on connected vehicles:

1. Remote control: If hackers manage to commandeer a vehicle, there is a
  potential risk of remotely controlling, stealing, or causing accidents with the
  vehicle.

2. Unauthorised access: Many vehicle manufacturers prefer the use of keyless
  systems that rely on wireless key fobs. This new access mechanism presents
  opportunities for illegal entry into vehicles and the potential disruption of
  wireless communication between the vehicle and mobile applications or
  systems.

3. Personal data theft: As the number of sensors in vehicles increases, there is a
  heightened risk of hackers stealing information used to identify individuals,
  such as location data and even financial information.

While communication between connected vehicles may be conceptually similar to
communication between any two objects in the Internet of Things (IoT), this per-
spective, although technically accurate, does not hold in terms of security. Unlike a
rebootable computer or smartphone, connected vehicles require significantly higher
standards of safety, security, and privacy [7,16].

11.7 Blockchain technology

Blockchain technology is considered one of the most significant technologies of
our time. Blockchain is a method of cryptographically verifiable digital distributed
data recording using sequential and successive blocks. In blockchain, the blocks
that store data are linked to each other using cryptographic hash algorithms. This
linkage occurs through the process of writing the hash of the previous block into the
subsequent block [32].
224 AIoT for smart transportation

  Blockchain networks host smart contract software that enables the transfer of
assets or data. Smart contracts are small pieces of code that contain information on
how transactions should be executed. Due to its distributed nature, blockchain
networks employ consensus algorithms to verify the accuracy of the data. These
consensus algorithms are essential for validating the accuracy of data written in the
distributed structure. Therefore, blockchain is a technology that utilises Distributed
Ledger Technology (DLT). In distributed ledgers, data is shared openly without
being subjected to any cryptographic filtering. However, in blockchain technology,
the data pertaining to transfer transactions is recorded in the ledgers using crypto-
graphic algorithms, ensuring the secure storage of the transactions [32,33].

  In summary, blockchain is a cryptographically secure distributed ledger that is
extremely difficult or nearly impossible to alter and whose updates depend on
consensus or smart contracts.

11.7.1 Historical development

When examining the historical development of blockchain technology, it is
appropriate to consider the pre-Bitcoin and post-Bitcoin eras. Although Bitcoin is
merely a product of blockchain technology, it serves as a milestone in this field
because it demonstrates the proper functioning of blockchain technology.

  In 1982, Chaum’s paper “Blind Signatures for Untraceable Payments” can be
regarded as the foundational work in the pre-Bitcoin era of blockchain. This paper
introduced the concept of anonymisation, which is also integral to blockchain tech-
nology, by using digital signatures for untraceable and anonymous transactions [34].

  In 1991, Haber and Stornetta, in their paper “How to Time-Stamp a Digital
Document” discussed how digital documents could be timestamped using crypto-
graphic methods. This paper showed that documents containing video, audio, or
text could be made tamper-proof using cryptographic hash algorithms. This work
also contributed significantly to the development of blockchain technology in the
pre-Bitcoin era [35].

  Bitcoin was introduced to the world in 2008 through the paper “Bitcoin: A
Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System” by the pseudonymous Satoshi Nakamoto.
The insights provided in this paper on how blockchain technology works laid the
foundation for Bitcoin, a digital currency for peer-to-peer money transfers.
Bitcoin’s creation involved the integration of DLT, game theory, cryptography, and
consensus algorithms. Today, almost all applications running on blockchain net-
works are built on this concept [36].

  Another major revolution in the history of blockchain technology was the
Ethereum project, proposed by Vitalik Buterin in 2014. Unlike Bitcoin, which
functions as a currency, Ethereum emerged as a vast blockchain network where
blockchain applications could operate. Ethereum is a virtual operating system with
its programming language (Solidity), allowing distributed applications and smart
contracts to run. This project represents another significant milestone in blockchain
history [37].

  In Ethereum and Bitcoin networks, no permission is required to join the net-
work, thus these types of networks are referred to as public or permissionless
  Blockchain technology and cyber security in ITS 225

networks. Contrary to public networks, the idea that blockchain networks could
also be used in closed, permissioned environments led to the creation of the
Hyperledger product by the Linux Foundation in 2015 [38]. The Hyperledger
architecture, while similar to public networks in terms of end-to-end network
structures and cryptographic usage, introduced a different perspective to block-
chain technology by requiring permission to join the network and offering diverse
consensus mechanisms.

11.7.2 Fundamental concepts of blockchain

The fundamental concepts that ensure blockchain’s transparent, secure, distributed,
and decentralised structure are as follows.

11.7.2.1 Peers, nodes, peer-to-peer network
Peers are the fundamental element of a blockchain network. In a vehicular network,
the blockchain network’s peers will be vehicles and they will be connected to each
other. Figure 11.3 illustrates the peers of an ITS, every peer will have three com-
ponents, an application, a smart contract, and one signature.

  In a peer-to-peer network architecture, devices within the network communicate
with each other directly, without relying on a central server. Each device in the
network maintains a list of all other devices, allowing it to connect and perform
transactions directly with another device. This eliminates the need for traffic to pass
through a server or intermediary device, establishing a direct peer-to-peer connection
between two devices. In blockchain networks, each node whether it is a computer,
server/client, or smartphone – is capable of executing consensus protocols.

  Nodes in a blockchain network are critical components capable of performing
multiple functions within the system. They verify transactions and ensure the dis-
tribution of ledgers. In other words, by distributing the ledgers through participants,
nodes decentralise the blockchain, preventing it from becoming a centralised
structure. Nodes can not only carry out transactions but also act as miners. Mining
involves the production and verification of blocks within the blockchain network.
While every miner represents a node, not every node represents a miner.

  Generally, blockchain nodes can be classified into three main types:

1. Full nodes: These nodes store a complete copy of the blockchain and are
  responsible for approving and distributing transactions. Miners, who perform
  mining operations, typically fall under this category.

2. Simplified payment verification (SPV) nodes: These nodes do not have the
  necessary hardware to perform mining. Devices such as mobile phones and low-
  powered computers are examples of SPV nodes. These nodes do not track all
  transactions on the blockchain but instead rely on peer nodes for information.

3. WEB nodes: These nodes generally represent wallets where cryptocurrencies can be
  stored. Through these wallets, users can manage their transactions on the network.

nodes store the endorsement policy and their signatures, which are valid across the
network. Additionally, they record all transactions happening within the network on

transactions are recorded. DLT is established when copies of a ledger are dis-
tributed across multiple locations within a network. Unlike traditional databases,
distributed ledgers are not managed from a single point and do not require a cen-
tralised data storage system [39,40].

copy of the distributed ledger and can access the records shared within the network.
