7.3.1.3 Sensor networks
Wireless sensor network (WSNs) is commonly used in the large-scale imple-
mentation of IoT sensors in transport systems. These networks consist of spatially
distributed autonomous sensor nodes that watch and report on their environment
independently of one another. WSNs use different media access control protocols
including ZigBee, LoRaWAN, and BLE to form low-power and adaptive networks
designed to forward messages over long distances.

7.3.1.4 Crowdsourcing and participatory sensing
The use of smart mobile phones and applications has made crowdsourcing and
participatory sensing useful methodologies for data collection in ITS [13]. Social
activities such as Waze and Google Maps utilise the availability of mobile devices
with participation from the users to gather data including traffic conditions and the
occurrence of traffic incidents and the condition of the road. The discussed Cyber-
Physical System (CPS) assigns roles to users to share information through their
smartphones, which makes them active nodes in the transportation environment.

7.3.1.5 Edge computing
Indeed, edge computing structures have a central role in data collection and pro-
cessing in ITS systems. Through edge computing, the system enables peripheral
nodes to utilise computational resources closer to the data source, filter, preprocess
data, and make localised decisions. This helps reduce the need for communication
bandwidth and minimises the latency caused by transmitting raw data to the cen-
tralised processing units. Fixed targets like Road Side Units, smart junction boxes,
and other vehicular computers can process and even make decisions based upon
patterns in the data that they receive in real-time and reduce time sensitivity in the
application [14]. Acquisition data methods in smart transportation systems include
the collection and transfer of data from various nodes to central or distributed

7.3.2.2 IoT communication protocols
The IoT plays a crucial role in smart transport systems, as it enables the integration
of a large number of sensors, actuators, and devices. There are two well-known IoT
communication protocols:

1. Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT): MQTT is a low overhead,
  lightweight publish-subscribe transported protocol suitable for devices.
  Transportation systems employ digital communication extensively to provide
  control command and to coordinate data acquisition and distribution of data
  gathered by sensors respectively [16].

2. Constrained Application Protocol (CoAP): CoAP is a web transfer protocol
  specifically designed for constrained networks and devices. In particular, it facil-
  itates the systematic monitoring and control of traffic lights, roadside equipment,
  and other IoT devices integrated into transport infrastructure networks.

7.3.2.3 Vehicle Ad hoc networks
Two of the most important protocols for the direct exchange of information
between vehicles as well as with roadside units are Dedicated Short-Range
Communications (DSRC) and Cellular Vehicle-to-Everything (C-V2X).

1. DSRC: DSRC is a short-range wireless communication protocol that operates
  in the band of 5.9 GHz. The objectives encompass social, content, and con-
  nectivity aspects, enabling the streaming of applications such as entertainment,
  traffic control, and collaborative collision avoidance [17].

2. C-V2X: C-V2X is a cellular vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication
  technology that uses existing LTE and 5G networks for V2V, V2I, and V2N
  applications. Compared to DSRC it offers higher range and reliability and
  hence various applications are possible with it.

7.3.2.4 Bluetooth and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)
Short-range wireless communication in smart transport systems is typically pro-
vided via Bluetooth and BLE. Application of such protocols is in keyless entry, car
diagnostics, and in-car infotainment systems among others. In particular, BLE is
suitable for low-power IoT devices, including beacons and transportation structure
monitoring sensors.

7.3.3.2 Edge computing architecture
While cloud computing provides centralised computation solutions that have a
remarkable processing ability, edge computing architectures deploy computational
assets and decision-making at the data origin. Such an approach is most beneficial
for the time-critical applications for ITS where fast response time and maximum
availability of the response is essential.

  Some of the edge devices include roadside units, intelligent traffic signals, and
onboard car computers since they can hold and analyse data. This means that
decisions and optimisations can be made quickly, on-the-fly, without necessarily
having to interface with the centralised cloud infrastructures all the time.

  On the same note, edge computing architectures can reduce load time and
network bandwidth demands involved with the transfer of raw sensory data from
various IoT sensors and gadgets to the cloud.

7.3.3.3 Fog computing architecture
Fog computing adds an intermediary layer between edge devices and the cloud,
which further expands the concept of cloud computing by making it a hierarchical
architecture for processing and data storage. For instance, ITS can use the place-
ment of fog nodes at communication hubs or traffic management centres to process
and filter data coming from nearby edge devices.

  Fog computing is a bridging technique, allowing relevant information to be
forwarded to the cloud for further analytics and extended period storage. This
architecture enables effective data collection, preprocessing, and localised
decision-making while enhancing the responsiveness of transport systems by
reducing latency and network congestion. It also allows real-time monitoring,
analysis, and control of traffic flow, vehicle operations, and infrastructure main-
tenance. Furthermore, it enhances data safety and privacy based on the processing
of sensitive data over local networks and minimises the transfer of raw data over
public networks. Overall, fog computing is critical to the optimisation of efficiency,
dependability, and scalability of ITS and to opening doors to smooth and networked
mobility-related solutions.

7.3.3.4 Hybrid architecture
Fog computing, edge computing related to cloud computing, and hybrid archi-
tecture are terms frequently used within ITS to solve the various needs and diffi-
culties presented by the transportation ecosystem. Hybrid architecture delivers
scalable and adaptable solutions made from the best of both architectural models
and is adapted to specific use cases and applications. While long-term traffic pat-
tern analysis and predictive maintenance reasonably align with cloud-based pro-
cessing and storage, applications depending on decision-making on the edge of
cloud computing, such as autonomous vehicle navigation and real-time traffic
signal control, may require low latency only through edge computing. Besides this,
by judiciously placing fog nodes, data from edge devices can be preprocessed and
aggregated, freeing up resources in the clouds and lowering bandwidth require-
ments over the network.

7.3.3.5 Software-defined networking (SDN)
SDN is a concept for transportation networks that separates the data plane, referred
to as forwarding traffic, from the control plane, which symbolises the network’s
intelligence. This separation allows for centralised management and program-
mability of the network, which thus improves flexibility, scalability, and
adaptability.

  With ITS, SDN architectures have to allocate the network adaptively
according to resources available in real-time traffic conditions, prioritise critical
data flows, and allow for the seamless introduction of new services and
applications.

  With SDN, it is possible to abstract the underlying network infrastructure and
provide a centralised control plane to more efficiently and responsively manage the
orchestration of complex transportation networks.

7.4 Data processing and analysis

7.4.1 Big data analytics

The sheer quantum, velocity, and variety of data being generated in smart trans-
portation systems make it essential to embrace big data analytics approaches. Here
are some of the most important big data analytics approaches that have been used
as follows.

7.4.1.1 Batch processing
Traditional batch processing techniques such as MapReduce and Apache Hadoop
are employed for fault-tolerant and scalable processing of large volumes of his-
torical transportation data. It facilitates parallel processing of data across nodes,
thereby dramatically easing the analysis of gigantic datasets [18]. Batch processing
is mostly deployed for applications like traffic pattern analysis, long-term fore-
casting, and predictive maintenance, where the urgency to process in real-time is
not at a high level.

7.4.1.2 Stream processing
Many applications in an ITS need to process real-time or almost real-time continuous
data streams from streams of IoT sensors, vehicles, and other source applications.
Stream processing frameworks, including Apache Kafka, Apache Spark Streaming,
and Apache Flink, allow these data streams to be ingested, processed, and analysed
as they are consumed in real time. Stream processing is essential for applications that
include real-time traffic monitoring, incident detection, and predictive analytics in
which responses and inferences must be realised within a timely manner to be
effective in the management of transportation.

7.4.1.3 In-Memory data processing
Applications that involve ultra-low latency and high-throughput data processing
make use of in-memory computing techniques. Databases as well as in-memory

gation, real-time control of traffic in the signal, and dynamic optimisation of routes.

7.4.2.1 Supervised learning in traffic management
This methodology is quite instrumental in traffic management as it uses historical

Deep Learning algorithms can identify hidden patterns or anomalies as there is no
labelled data. For example, with reference to traffic, clustering algorithms can

ning interventions before problems arise is quite achievable.

Deep learning has revolutionised transportation, more so by the implementation of
CNNs and RNNs, where CNNs support in-time vehicle detection in real-time video
feeds, thereby enhancing the level of safety and effectiveness of operations, and
RNNs mainly dominate time-series forecasting, as predicting traffic flow during
peak hours can help in optimising traffic signals and routes.

7.4.2.4 Reinforcement learning for dynamic optimisation
Such complex transportation problems are now dynamically solved through rein-
forcement learning (RL). RL algorithms can optimise traffic signal control using
real-time data on observed interactions in the environment, thus smoothing the flow
of traffic and reducing congestion. More importantly, autonomous vehicle navi-
gation relies heavily on RL, equipping vehicles to learn and adapt to changing road
conditions and patterns of traffic.

7.4.2.5 The power of ensemble and hybrid models
Examples of ensemble and hybrid modelling techniques provide good ways to
combine different approaches in machine learning to further enhance the accuracy of
prediction. For instance, the combination of a deep model with random forests may
help improve the robustness of vehicle detection to yield more accurate responses in
terms of traffic conditions. Synergy brings about solutions that can effectively be
applied in the navigation of complex reality in transportation scenarios.

7.5.1 Traffic monitoring and management

IoT sensor data, among other data sources, are the bedrock of intelligent monitoring
and management systems of traffic. Such current data regarding the level of traffic
flow, the number of vehicles, and the degree of congestion are obtained through
traffic cameras, inductive loops, and radar sensors [19]. These data are coupled
with data gathered from external sources, including meteorological data and
crowdsourced data gathered from mobile applications to create a complete view of
traffic conditions. All of this is done by using next-generation analytics and
machine learning models to predict traffic patterns, identify obstacles, and adjust
the timing of the traffic signals. These systems tend to reduce travel times and
traffic, and enhance safety through variable speed limit adjustments, intelligent
ramp metering, and dynamic route guidance.

7.5.2 Predictive maintenance and asset management

IoT sensors must be utilised to monitor the state of transportation infrastructure and
assets such as roads, bridges, and automobiles. These sensors detect flaws and
fissures in infrastructure using strain gauges, accelerometers, and acoustic emission
sensors. Vehicle performance and maintenance requirements, along with fuel
consumption, can also be updated using onboard diagnostics and telematics devi-
ces. Predictive maintenance models analyse this data in terms of predicting prob-
able failures and planning proactive maintenance, which minimises downtime and
increases the life cycle of transportation assets.

7.5.3 Route optimisation and fleet management

Telematics data, GPS tracking devices, and traffic monitoring systems provide the
data that enables fleet management as well as advanced route optimisation. The
analysis based on machine learning algorithms, optimisation approaches, real-time
traffic conditions, vehicle positions, and delivery schedules actually designs and
modifies routes in real time. These optimisations result in more efficient and sus-
tainable transportation operations, meaning reduced emissions, fuel consumption,
and time devoted to the delivery of goods. By using data analytics and dashboards,
fleet managers have access to a better basis for decision-making and resource
allocation related to driver behaviour, vehicle utilisation, and operational
parameters.

7.5.4 Autonomous vehicle systems

Fully autonomous cars will rely substantially on integrating multiple sources of
data and information sensed by IoT devices and sensors, including cameras,
LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging), radar, and ultrasonic sensors that monitor
the environment around the vehicle at high resolutions to enable object detection,

awareness and decision-making ability to autonomous vehicles.

7.5.5 Smart parking and electric vehicle charging

Use of intelligent parking systems utilise data analytics and IoT devices for opti-
misation of the use of parking space and availability, obtain real-time occupancy

Traffic flow sensors such as inductive loop sensors, radar detectors, and infrared
sensors are suitably mounted on various roadways. These sense all characteristics
of vehicle movement, including speed, and transmit the data to concerned centres.
It provides real-time input about traffic density, vehicle counts, and speed patterns.
This information helps the adaptive traffic signal control systems to know the
extent to which the timing needs to be adapted according to prevailing traffic
conditions. For instance, it could make a road traverse longer in green lights during
peak hours while shortening the wait time when roads are under off-peak traffic.
Optimising traffic signal operations will certainly reduce the amount of delay the
cities experience, travel time, and eventually road accidents.

7.7.2 Environmental sensors

Sensors can be installed in the environment to measure air quality, temperature,
humidity, and noise levels. Such sensors form the backbone of monitoring urban
environments, providing data for transportation plan development and public health
initiatives. For example, the increase in levels of air quality pollutants can be
detected by air quality sensors, which, in response, trigger an alarm that leads to a
change in the mode of operations by public transportation systems or increased
implementation of cleaner vehicles. Noise sensors can also be crucial in coming up
  Role of data and IoT sensors in smart transportation 143

with sections where excess traffic noise is caused and intervening in appropriate
places with the installation of sound barriers or by changing the traffic pattern.

7.7.3 Connected vehicle sensors

The vehicle is equipped with GPS, LiDAR, cameras, and ultrasonic sensors. The
infrastructure on the road can receive information from the vehicles (Vehicle-to-
Infrastructure, or V2I) as well as the other vehicles about live conditions of road
conditions, traffic signals, and hazardous conditions. For instance, in the case of a
sudden stop ahead of it, a car will notify surrounding vehicles to slow down gra-
dually thus enhancing common safety. V2I may also enable vehicles to receive live
updates from roadside equipment on changes in traffic signals, which could opti-
mise travel routes and reduce stop-and-go driving.

7.7.4 Parking sensors

The parking sensors can be installed in car parking lots and garages. They monitor the
real-time availability of parking spaces. Such sensors can use ultrasonic or infrared to
identify whether a space is occupied. That information is passed to drivers through
mobile applications, which guide them towards the nearest parking spot. These sen-
sors reduce the burden of urban traffic congestion, decrease emissions because of
idling cars, and improve drivers’ experience by saving time that they would have spent
trying to find an appropriate parking spot. Additionally, cities could gain some insight
based on the data from parking and leverage it to make a difference in decisions
related to how they develop infrastructure or pricing strategies.

7.7.5 Public transport sensors

Public transportation systems integrate a wide variety of sensors, including RFID
tags, GPS trackers, and occupancy sensors, to improve the reliability of service and
experience for the users. GPS trackers can give a view of real-time location,
enabling the proper updating of schedules by transit agencies and information to
passengers at any desired time about expected arrival times. Occupancy sensors
may indicate crowd level on buses and trains, thus guiding agencies on safety and
managing how many people the system can carry. For example, when travelling is
heaviest, alerts can be directed to users based on crowded conditions and ways of
alternative travelling. Thus, data-driven insight makes routing more efficient, and
services are well optimised to improve quality.

7.7.6 Infrastructure sensors

There are sensors embedded within roads, bridges, and tunnels, which can monitor
the structural integrity together with the levels of traffic loads. These sensors can
detect stress, vibrations, and other indicators of structural health. Sensors can be
installed on bridges to monitor the weight limits and alert the authorities in the case
of excess traffic loads that go beyond safe thresholds. These sensors enable
proactive maintenance and prevent failure through real-time data on the conditions
144 AIoT for smart transportation

of infrastructure, thereby extending the life of critical transportation assets and
reducing long-term maintenance costs for safety improvement.

  In summary, sensors play a critical role in any functional and efficient smart
transportation system. Sensors could provide the foundational data required to
better manage traffic flows, improve safety, enhance public transit management,
and understand better how to plan urban development. Integration and sophistica-
tion in smart transportation solutions should increase further as sensor technology
matures. So, therefore, the future of transportation lies in reaping this information
to create efficient, effective, and responsive systems to dynamic needs in urban
mobility. This thus allows cities to pave the way for safer, cleaner, and more sus-
tainable transportation networks through these innovations.

7.8 Real-world case studies

Several real-world deployments and implementations have demonstrated the
potential for transforming data and IoT sensors into a means of creating smart
intelligent transportation solutions. This chapter covers several real-world case
studies that clearly illustrate the tangible benefits and impacts of these technologies
on the transportation sector.

7.8.1 Amsterdam Smart Mobility Living Lab

Amsterdam was the first city that started applying data and IoT technologies to
optimise its transport network under smart mobility initiatives. It is an initiative of
Amsterdam Smart Mobility Living Lab which comes through a collaborative effort
of local administration, academic institutes, and business partners. Such IoT sen-
sors and data sources like Bluetooth sensors, traffic cameras, inductive loops, and
crowdsourced data from mobile applications among others have been put in this
living lab. The data collected is thus analysed, modelled, and used to determine the
traffic pattern, congestion hotspots, traveller behaviour among other things with the
help of algorithms of machine learning and state-of-the-art analytics tools [26].
Among city thoroughfares, the intelligent traffic signal control system is the major
project of the living lab, which uses traffic data and predictive algorithms to change
the timing of signals in real time. This technology has reduced travel times by a
huge 10% and very much reduced emissions.

  In addition to this, smart parking solutions have also been incorporated in the living
lab by using cameras and sensors to know the availability of parking. Once merged with
navigation applications, the information can help drivers locate a parking space without
showing up at the juncture of possible traffic created by cars turning around.

7.8.2 Singapore’s autonomous vehicle testbed

Being aware of the potential for better safety and increased productivity in trans-
portation, Singapore has been aggressively pursuing autonomous vehicle technology.
Using its advanced infrastructure and accommodating data-driven technology, the
  Role of data and IoT sensors in smart transportation 145

nation has created an extensive testbed for testing autonomous vehicles. There is an
equipped network of diverse IoT sensors, including cameras, radar, LiDAR, and V2X
communication systems, to provide high-resolution information on the environment
surrounding the vehicle to enable accurate obstacle avoidance and navigation. In the
testbed, experiments were performed together with industry partners and research
institutes on a range of use cases in autonomous vehicles, from last-mile deliveries to
freight transportation and on-demand mobility services [27].

  Yet another example would be the Punggol Digital District, where passengers
travelling on clearly established routes are transported around by self-driving
shuttles equipped with the latest sensing capabilities and data processing capacities
to ensure safe, comfortable, and result-driven travel.

7.8.3 PredictiveBC: British Columbia’s predictive analytics
  for bridge maintenance

It is the predictiveBC, a best-in-class predictive analytics system adopted by the
British Columbia Ministry of Transportation and Infrastructure to maximise asset
management and bridge maintenance plans. PredictiveBC uses data ranging from
many sources, which includes environmental data and inspection records as well as
sensors in monitoring structural health. Such data are processed with the applica-
tion of machine learning algorithms for it to be analysed and examined so that the
rates of deterioration of bridge components can be forecasted, together with pos-
sible failure or maintenance requirements. It links over 2,700 bridges within the
province, allowing transportation officials to effectively manage resources, prior-
itise maintenance activities, and extend the service life of critical infrastructure
assets. PredictiveBC has been found extremely cost-effective and operationally
efficient, reducing maintenance spending likely by up to 20% while raising the
service life of the monitored systems by about 25% [28].

7.8.4 New York City’s intelligent traffic management

New York City has proposed a highly integrated intelligent traffic management
system to cope with the problems of congestion and improve mobility in this highly
populated metropolitan environment. This system collects data from a wide range
of crowdsourcing sources such as social media, mobile applications, cameras, and
traffic sensors. Real-time advanced analytics and machine learning techniques are
applied to process and analyse this information for proactive management of traf-
fic, incident detection, and pattern recognition of congestion [29].

  This is the critical Midtown in Motion initiative, part of the system that uses
adaptive signal management technology in a dynamic way to change the traffic signal
according to real-time traffic conditions. For instance, for the Midtown Manhattan
neighbourhood, this programme has proven to reduce travel times by 10% and elim-
inate large parts of emissions. The city also has intelligent parking guidance systems.
Together, the goals are to reduce the time spent driving around while looking for a
place to park and the emissions from driving around that accompany it. These systems
detect open parking places using sensor and camera data and route cars there.

7.9 Challenges and future trends

Although IoT sensors and data provide various benefits in smart transportation,
several emerging trends and challenges must be considered for the responsible and
sustainable development of these technologies.

7.9.1 Data privacy and security

Smart transport systems would involve handling enormous amounts of data, which
means there would be issues of data security and privacy. For instance, personal
identification data and location data are sensitive information that should be pro-
tected against misuse and unauthorised access. Strategies for encryption, robust
data governance frameworks, and approaches for analytics would have to be
implemented to safeguard personal data while offering the benefits brought by
data-driven solutions in transportation.

7.9.2 Scalability and interoperability

This is where scalability and interoperability grow as a key issue with the spread of
IoT devices and data sources. Solutions from single vendors, communication proto-
cols, and various heterogeneous data formats may prevent seamless integration and
exchange of data in transportation systems. Open data formats, standardisation efforts,
and the development of interoperable architectures are needed to achieve scalable and
efficient data management and analysis across different transportation systems.

7.9.3 Integration of emerging technologies

The integration of the newest technologies, such as edge computing, blockchain, and
5G networks, brings both opportunities and challenges for further development in the
transportation sector. 5G networks promise high bandwidth and extremely low latency.
This can enable real-time data processing and transfer for applications in transporta-
tion. Edge computing reduces latency and improves the decision-making capacity at
local levels by bringing processing power closer to sources of data. Blockchain tech-
nology can secure and decentralise data sharing in transportation ecosystems and aid in
providing transparent and reliable transactions. New technologies and their possible
impacts on smart transportation are described in Table 7.5. Some of these are 5G

7.9.4 Ethical considerations and governance

AIoT-enabled smart transportation technology raises major ethical issues with
responsibility, transparency, and algorithmic bias issues. Machine learning algo-
rithms and decision-making processes should be designed to conduct fair con-
siderations for diverse populations while being tested against all potential biases.
Transparency should thus form data collection, processing, and the basis on which
decisions will be made to build trust in the face of public consciousness. Lastly,
sound governance frameworks and regulatory policies should be enacted so as to
ensure that these data and AI technologies are used most responsibly and ethically
in transportation systems.

7.9.5 Sustainability and environmental impact

The smart transport solutions have the potential to significantly reduce the impacts on
the environment from transport through data-driven and IoT sensor solutions.
Optimised routing, traffic management, and promotion of green modes of transport
would lead to reduced emissions, improved air quality, and savings in the consump-
tion of energy. Moreover, energy consumption and carbon footprint in data centres,
  Role of data and IoT sensors in smart transportation 149

communication networks, and the manufacturing processes of IoT devices have to be
minimised further by inculcating practices and making use of renewable energy [30].

7.10 Conclusion

This transformation has been driven by the integration of diverse data sources, IoT
sensors, and advanced analytics, paving the way for intelligent, efficient, and sus-
tainable transportation ecosystems. Transportation agencies and service providers
use data and IoT sensors to optimise traffic flow, improve asset management,
enable autonomous vehicle systems, and offer sustainable mobility solutions. Data-
driven decision-making, predictive analytics, and real-time monitoring have
become the cornerstones of ITS, clearing the path for safer, more effective, and
environmentally friendly transportation experiences [31]. But there are also various
challenges now staring at completely realising the full potential of AIoT-enabled
smart mobility. Some of these key steps on this journey include incorporating
emerging technologies, grappling with issues of privacy and security of data,
ensuring scalability and interoperability, and establishing principles of ethical
governance. All these require concerted efforts by stakeholders in the form of
transportation authorities, technology suppliers, researchers, and policymakers to
ensure that these issues are faced appropriately while implementing data-driven
transport solutions.

  As the global population continues to grow and transportation needs increase
further, it is the data and IoT sensors that are destined to play increasingly impor-
tant roles in defining the next generation of mobility. The marriage of IoT, machine
learning, and advanced analytics holds the key to unlocking creative solutions for
safety improvement, reduction in traffic congestion, decreased emissions, and
convenient and smooth transportation for all. Beyond just enhancing mobility,
AIoT-enabled smart transportation helps society achieve its greater objectives:
sustainability and economic growth, along with improved quality of life. These
solutions can reduce urban sprawl, provide better access to transportation resour-
ces, and create vibrant, living communities by optimising transportation networks
and promoting the use of integrated mobility services.

  Next, the transport sector will be in the epoch of revolution, wherein the
power of data and IoT sensors will persistently influence how people and pro-
ducts move. Realising a future intelligent, sustainable, and user-centric trans-
portation system answering the needs of the modern world would thus mean
embracing this paradigm change and promoting interdisciplinary collaborations.
