14.1 Introduction

Transportation is a foundation of modern society, enabling the flow of commodities
and people essential for economic growth, social interaction, and cultural
exchange. However, with the increasing urbanization and globalization, transpor-
tation systems face numerous challenges that include everything – from environ-
mental pollution to traffic congestion, from infrastructure upkeep to safety
concerns. These challenges necessitate innovative solutions to optimize existing
transportation networks, enhance efficiency, and promote sustainability.

14.1.1 Overview of transportation challenges

Traffic congestion is one of the most significant challenges facing transport sys-
tems worldwide. As the global population rushes to cities, the sheer number of
people makes it seem like urban roadways were designed for a bygone era. They
are becoming increasingly jammed. This bottlenecking leads to inefficiencies that
increasingly feel like a drain on productivity. Moreover, the rigid, sprawling nature
of many urban transport systems opts for fossil fuels over other energy sources,
leading to high air pollution levels. These modes of transport contribute mightily to
the emissions of greenhouse gases that are changing the Earth’s climate for the
worse. Inadequate upkeep and old facilities can mean big trouble for transportation
safety and performance. A worn-out or improperly maintained infrastructure is a
safety hazard, and it makes the network function poorly.

  In addition, transportation services are becoming more and more necessary and
put pressure on the already underdeveloped infrastructure, aggravating the con-
gestion problem. The call for moving freight, especially in a globalized and
e-commerce world, is incessant. Unfortunately, the logistics and distribution
systems that do the actual work are so poorly organized that they cause untold
delays and problems throughout the supply chain.

  Considering these difficulties, the pressing requirement for revolutionary tech-
nologies to transform the transport system into smarter, safer, and more sustainable
becomes clear. These same “game-changing” opportunities offered by artificial
intelligence (AI) could equally be said to change the game for the transport sector.

14.1.2 Introduction to AI for transportation

Across numerous sectors, AI is creating disruption. There is no exception in the
transportation sector. AI is a diverse set of technologies and techniques that allow
machines to emulate human intelligence, learn from data, and use that data to make
decisions. Intelligent transportation systems (ITS) hold unlimited potential to
transform many dimensions of the industry’s operations, from managing traffic to
optimizing routes to using so-called “smart” vehicles.

  AI can sort through massive data sets and pull out pertinent details. In traffic
systems, this means examining the data captured every minute, essentially an ever-
growing ocean of numbers. AI works well with large datasets, and to gain the
maximum benefit, one needs to understand the implications of the techniques it
  AI on the move 281

uses at that massive scale. If predictive algorithms live up to their name, one could
be looking at transportation systems that are safer, more reliable, and more efficient
than anything seen so far. Meanwhile, AI is the best friend an autonomous vehicle
developer could ask for. That is because the technology that undergirds these “self-
driving” cars is fundamentally about AI-driven perception and decision-making in
increasingly complicated (and oftentimes perilous) roadways.

  At its core, AI makes the adaptive, responsive, and ITS of tomorrow possible. This
chapter looks at the application of AI in today’s real-world transportation problems. It
examines the potential of these relatively novel solutions to modernize the systems and
address the often overwhelming challenges they face – problems ranging from inefficient
mass transit options to poor road safety for drivers, cyclists, and pedestrians alike.

14.4 Traffic prediction and forecasting

AI models use historical traffic data, weather forecasts, and other relevant factors to
predict future traffic conditions. When it comes to route planning, mode selection,
and travel times, these forecasts assist drivers and transportation authorities in
making informed decisions.

14.4.1 Traffic prediction

Traffic prediction problems can be divided into traffic status prediction, traffic flow
prediction, and travel demand prediction. Traffic status prediction involves using
electronic navigation systems to avoid congested roads, while traffic flow predic-
tion is crucial for monitoring and guiding people’s flow due to excessive traffic.
Travel demand prediction involves anticipating travel demands for online taxi
services. To solve these problems, spatio-temporal data, including road networks,
area-based traffic, trajectories, incident data, meteorological data, and temporal
data, is used. Data is preprocessed through map-matching, cleaning, storage, and
compression. The overview of forming intelligent transportation from spatio-
temporal data is represented in Figure 14.2.

  Figure 14.2 is illustrated step-by-step. Map matching is a crucial process that
converts spatial data into road networks with latitude/longitude coordinates. It is
applicable to convert taxi trajectories into road sequences, allowing for traffic flow
calculation on corresponding roads. The second step, data cleaning, is crucial for
predicting future traffic by correcting errors in spatio-temporal data, such as GPS
points, to ensure accurate predictions. The third step involves data storage, which
becomes challenging as spatio-temporal data collection increases. For example,
travel time prediction methods use the average travel time of similar historical
trajectories to find similar trajectories efficiently. This method surveys various
approaches for storing and retrieving large temporal and spatial data. Data com-
pression is crucial for reducing costs in communications, computing, and storage of
large spatio-temporal data. Area-based traffic flow calculations only require the
number of trajectories, so recording the entire trajectory information is unneces-
sary. Various methods are being explored to compress spatio-temporal data effi-
ciently [37].

14.4.2 Traffic forecasting

Forecasting data related to traffic, such as speed, flow, travel demand, and travel
time, is crucial for public safety. These problems can be divided into region-based
and network-based problems. Area-based problems involve treating cities as
separate areas and estimating traffic data for each. Governments need to monitor
crowd flow to avoid safety issues. Network-based problems consider road network
constraints, such as intersection flow, speed, and travel time. For example, choos-
ing the shortest travel route is essential when planning travel. Both problems help
ensure safe and efficient transportation [37].

14.5 Traffic signal optimization

By dynamically modifying traffic signal timings as a response to demand patterns
and real-time traffic circumstances, AI-based optimization algorithms aid in the
reduction of traffic congestion [4]. By doing this, traffic flow is enhanced, delays
are decreased, and idle time at crossings is minimized. Urban transportation sys-
tems require efficient traffic-demand management strategies to keep up with the
growing number of vehicles on the road every day. While modifications to urban
infrastructure may take years or even be impossible, optimizing traffic signal tim-
ing is one of the quickest and most affordable ways to optimize traffic flow in
metropolitan networks and minimize congestion at junctions [30].

14.5.1 Background of traffic signal timing

The factors that affect traffic signal timing (TST) and controller settings and eva-
luations are described in this section.

14.5.1.1 Traffic signal timing parameters
Providing people with speedy and safe guidance at intersections is one of the pri-
mary goals of TST settings. Certain TST setup parameters must be within the
control of the traffic management authority, while others must be flexible enough
to adjust to variations in traffic demand. The following are the control settings:

l Green time is the time period in seconds during which traffic moves at the
  saturation flow rate.

l Cycle length is the time required for a signal to end a single cycle.
l Phase sequence is the sequence of signal phases during the signal cycle.
l Change interval is the time between red and yellow signal phases to provide

  clearance before conflicting traffic movements.
l Offset is the time relationship between coordinated steps regarding timing.

  There can be fewer stopped cars and delays if the green phase period for a
given movement is extended. On the other hand, longer delays and a greater
number of stopped automobiles in subsequent movements are typically associated
with an extension of the traffic movement’s green time. Therefore, time is typically
allocated via an ideal signal timing strategy to maximize traffic throughput, such as
the average wait delay time.

14.5.1.2 Designing traffic signals structurally
The three traffic signal control techniques are actuated, adaptive, and fixed-time.
Fixed-time techniques, which use offline optimization algorithms to minimize
average latency and maximize network capacity, are appropriate for steady and
continuous traffic flow. By lowering average latency, early models established the
groundwork for fixed-time techniques. The preset traffic signal plan, however,
needs to be revised since urban traffic networks are dynamic, and even little dis-
ruptions can alter traffic volume.
  AI on the move 287

  Using sensor technologies to detect the presence of vehicles, adaptive control
algorithms modify the TST plan at each stage in accordance with current traffic
circumstances. Later, visual systems saw widespread use. Sensors are another tool
used in actuated control techniques, and actuated controllers use historical data to
ascertain the cycle duration. Installed on all roads within the signalized network,
adaptive techniques employ current data to anticipate cycle duration for real-time
traffic circumstances.

  Transportation systems, especially in urban areas, are extremely dynamic, and any
disturbance can suddenly change traffic volume. Therefore, it would be more advan-
tageous if the system could reduce traffic congestion in real-time. Real-time data col-
lecting is now a relatively easy process thanks to the introduction of sensor technology
and other strategies. With a wealth of data and available computational power,
immediate traffic management and prediction of traffic scenarios may be possible [30].

14.5.2 Optimization models for traffic signal control

An essential tool for facilitating efficient urban traffic flow is traffic signal opti-
mization. However, traffic signal operation is now severely limited by the data
available from conventional point detectors. Researchers have recently become
interested in connected cars, a developing technology that senses the current traffic
situation in its entirety. Traffic controllers can use the new data source to make
better-informed decisions. After much effort, a new traffic light control architecture
has been created for the linked car environment.

  The optimization model, which is the core component of the traffic signal
control system, produces the timing plan, which includes the cycle length and phase
distribution. As such, it needs to be carefully designed to maximize the control
effect. The suggested optimization models are categorized into three groups in this
section: models based on rules, models based on mathematical programming, and
models based on AI [33].

optimization criteria to establish control parameters. This kind of model primarily uses
the conventional fixed-time control algorithm, substituting real-time traffic condition
awareness in the context of connected vehicles (CVs) for previous traffic data.

critical flow ratio for the least delay cycle length, is the basis of the standard

14.5.2.2 Mathematical programming-based models

The most popular technique to optimize the timing scheme of intersection signal
control is mathematical programming, which is defined as choosing the best ele-
ment from the feasible region according to some criterion. Important studies have
made significant contributions in this area as an ideal paradigm within the trans-
portation sector research. A few illustrative studies are examined hereunder.

  Using information from linked cars, Feng et al. created a method for real-time
adaptive signal phase allocation [13]. The algorithm minimizes line length and
vehicle delay by optimizing step sequence and duration. The approach, which was
validated using VISSIM, reduces overall delay by 16.33% in high admission rate
instances, outperforming active control.

  In order to optimize traffic signal timing, Li et al. created a model framework
that takes into account vehicle characteristics such as fuel consumption and journey
time [22]. They used intelligent driving models to forecast vehicle paths in envir-
onments with linked vehicles, and the outcome was a mixed-integer nonlinear
programme – a popular option in priority control research.

  Priemer and Frederich presented a phase-based traffic control technique [29],
which predicts future queue lengths using comprehensive enumeration algorithms
and dynamic programming. The technique minimizes the overall queue length at
intersections by using vehicle position and speed data. In order to enable the con-
troller to get knowledge from its performance gradually, Cai et al. used approx-
imation dynamic programming [6]. Li and Ban divided the problem of splitting up
signal coordination and optimization into two stages: a level of intersection that
employs dynamic programming to increase step duration and a corridor level that
  AI on the move 289

optimizes offsets of each intersection. This method combines adaptive traffic signal
control with travel-time estimation [23].

  Vehicle platoons are represented by the vehicle traffic signal control problem
that Pandit et al. developed as a work scheduling problem on a processor [28]. The
Oldest Job First method was employed to reduce intersection delays. In comparison
to vehicle-active approaches, the Webster method, and pre-time signal manage-
ment methods, the assessment findings demonstrated that the algorithm decreased
vehicle delays under low and moderate traffic loads.

  The nonlinearity of constraints and objective functions is the primary obstacle
in mathematical programming. This can be overcome with heuristic algorithms or
dynamic programming techniques, but the computing load may cause issues for
real-time control.

14.5.2.3 AI-based models
Because AI does not require prior knowledge about model parameters or dynamics, it
reduces the need for costly calibration procedures, making it an attractive research
topic for controlling traffic signals. AI systems can learn from their surroundings,
enhancing performance over time and adjusting to changes in the environment [22,32].
Given that real-time traffic information can be interpreted, this is especially pertinent to
the control of traffic signals in a CV environment. Fuzzy logic, neural networks,
reinforcement learning, and multi-agent systems are some AI techniques being studied.
Because of this, AI systems are a viable way to enhance real-time traffic light control.

  In a multi-agent system, the car and traffic signal controller are seen as “agents,”
providing benefits like parallel processing, learning, and coordination without
needing a model. Using a collaborative multi-agent framework, Khamis and Gomaa
created an adaptable multi-objective traffic signal control system using reinforcement
learning [17]. They also showed how well traffic non-stationarity can be handled
using Bayesian probability interpretation. A cooperative vehicle intersection control
(CVIC) method, which minimizes the potential overlap of trajectories from opposing
approaches, was proposed by Lee and Park [21]. It is built on a multi-agent model.
CVIC looks for safe movements for every car approaching an intersection and
applies them. To address system failures caused by trajectory overlap and impractical
solutions, an extra algorithm was created. Comparing the CVIC algorithm to con-
ventional active intersection control, a simulation-based case study revealed that the
former greatly enhanced intersection performance.

  Through experimental interactions with the environment, controllers can update
their rules through reinforcement learning. The goal is to identify the best course of
action that maximizes overall gain, especially when there are trade-offs between
long- and short-term rewards. It has been effectively used in a number of scenarios.

  Unlike digital or classical logic, fuzzy logic is a popular intelligent-control
approach that uses continuous logical variables to assess analogue input values. A
WSN, a phase sorting module, and a fuzzy logic controller are the three compo-
nents of the multi-controller system that Collotta et al. proposed. The WSN gathers
traffic data, the stage sorting module chooses the order in which to execute the
stages, and each controller uses neuro-fuzzy networks to calculate green time
intervals based on current traffic conditions.

14.6 Dynamic route guidance systems

AI-powered navigation systems offer real-time route recommendations based on
traffic conditions, road closures, and incidents to help drivers avoid congestion and
choose efficient routes. Dynamic route guidance systems (DRGS) strategy aims to
achieve dynamic user balancing in the network, even under unusual conditions as
unexpected traffic volume increases. Experiments using traffic micro-simulators on
urban road networks showed positive results, with link-level analyses showing that
more widespread DRG devices make travel options more convenient for users. The
strategy maintains feasible alternatives in equilibrium situations by distributing
users among feasible alternatives, and the system does not exhibit unstable beha-
viour even with an increase in DRG devices [9].

14.6.2.1 Advanced traveller information systems (ATIS)
ATIS systems provide real-time traveller information to improve convenience and
travel efficiency. These systems include current traffic conditions, hazard warn-
ings, and real-time route guidance. The objective is to reduce traffic congestion and
improve road infrastructure capacity. The system inputs raw data from various
detection devices, real-time traffic patterns, and global roadway network informa-
tion. The collected data is filtered and aggregated by an information management
layer, which then disseminates it to specific ITS services and applications. The
outputs can be used by public and private application providers or disseminated to
subscribers. Road users can be classified into static and dynamic users. Dynamic
users accept ATIS route recommendations, optimizing their travel by selecting the
route that has the minimum travel time. Route recommendations are useful for long
trips, but for short trips or when en route, local perceptions can be inaccurate,
leading to poor performance [34].

14.6.3 Route guidance system

Dynamic components, such as the route guiding system (RGS), have been the focus
of research on wayfinding and route guiding. RGS is seen as a useful technique for
292 AIoT for smart transportation

reducing traffic, improving the road network’s functionality, and helping users who
need to become more familiar with the system with their navigation. Research has
been done on how users behave when using the popular RGS Advanced Traveler
Information System (ATIS). Finding the route that is both the shortest and requires
the least amount of travel time is the primary goal of routing in navigation systems.
ITS have begun to pay more attention to DRGS, which offer routing recommen-
dations to users based on actual traffic conditions. There is less congestion since the
user’s location, the availability of information, and the destination nodes decide
how information is sent [10].

14.8 ITS

AI is used in ITS to integrate various transportation technologies and infrastructure
components, such as traffic signals, electronic toll collection systems, and variable
message signs. By coordinating these systems intelligently, AI helps improve
safety, optimize traffic flow, and enhance overall mobility.

14.8.1 Intelligent traffic management

The use of AI in ITS can improve traffic management in several ways. For one, AI
can help optimize the timing of traffic signals. Another way that AI can impact
traffic management is by using data from the various sensors and cameras dis-
tributed along the roadways and within vehicles themselves. The applications of AI
in these two areas can lead to more efficient traffic systems and achieve many of
the same goals as the timing of traffic signals.

  Furthermore, AI in ITS may have even greater advantages for public trans-
portation. To begin with, it can make it easier to bring up public transit while
discussing transportation in general. For instance, when the systems that govern
mass transit work together with those that govern other modes of transportation,
such as operating a vehicle or truck, it is possible to manage everyone who travels
through an area in a way that is advantageous to the community. It becomes pos-
sible to prioritize bus and tram travel at the many intersections where those vehicles
meet and must co-mingle with other private or commercial vehicles. Moreover,
because many of those electronic toll collection systems exist in a public–private
partnership with a construction company that built the road, electrons make their
way to those contractors in a more efficient way when AI is in charge.

  Additionally, predictive maintenance that uses AI can ensure timely repairs of
infrastructure, thus decreasing the chances of malfunctions and outages. Overall,
using AI in an ITS makes the network more responsive, resilient, and sustainable.

14.8.2 Improved safety and incident response

Road safety stands to gain in several ways from using AI in ITS. For one, AI may
help ITS detect and respond to incidents even more quickly than humans. The
reason why real-time road safety might be achievable through ITS and AI working
together is that, firstly, they can do the same job but better, and secondly, there is
the potential for something new to occur: synergy. The synergy between ITS and
AI can happen in three ways: through improved incident detection, modification of
traffic management systems, better, more effective responses, and an overall
improvement to the road safety situation.
