2.4.1.1 Data acquisition and collection layer
There are several traffic detection methods which include ultrasonic sensors, surveil-
lance cameras, and light beams, each with its own merits and demerits. Blob detection
algorithms are employed on video streams processed at a local server by the system
due to their high performance and noise reduction capabilities [13,14]. After the
detection of traffic density by image processing techniques, a message is sent to the
microcontroller via the local server. The system is augmented with the sensors for
measurement of vehicle density, which are essential elements used in various traffic
management applications. These sensors measure distance by emitting a sound wave at
a specific frequency and waiting for its echo back. Using the formula for calculating
distances, distance can be defined as: Distance = ((a Â b)/2), where ‘a’ is equal to the
velocity of sound and ‘b’ the time taken. There exist three pairs of sensors fixed at
specific intervals along each roadside to calculate traffic density, with each sensor
providing a binary reading (either 1 or 0). At the node level, the density is determined
by aggregating the readings from all sensors on that roadside. The system classifies
traffic density into three states: Low (Condition 1: P1 = 1, P2 = 0, P3 = 0), Medium
(Condition 2: P1 = 1, P2 = 1, P3 = 0), and High (Condition 3: P1 = 1, P2 = 1, P3 = 1).

2.4.1.2 Data processing and decision-making layer
The system’s Data Processing as well as the Decision-Making Layer is responsible for
regulating road traffic following the prevailing conditions. In normal flow, every signal is
operating on its fixed time interval of (Q seconds) per turn. Nevertheless, when it comes
to increased volumes of traffic, a fixed-time control system would not be sufficient hence
requiring a density-based management module. Traffic density is calculated by the
module using data and adjusts signal times accordingly as shown in Figure 2.7 [14].

2.4.1.3 Application and actuation layer
On the Application and Actuation Layer, it obtains a rush interval through the
Regression Tree algorithm and updates this data daily to the server. The centralised
server then processes this data and generates detailed reports displayed through a
web application. Graphical representations of daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly
traffic conditions are included in the reports which can be beneficial for future road
planning and resource management.

2.4.3 Fleet management and optimisation

Fleet management and optimisation through AIoT encompasses the mingling of machine
learning with Internet of Things technologies for improved effectiveness and perfor-
mance. This approach, therefore, offers a unified framework through which continuous
monitoring, data analysis, as well as operational optimisation, will be undertaken in all
these considered vital factors for ensuring the reliability of fleet systems [16].

  Let us consider the case of bus fleet management, where a public transport com-
pany maintains a fleet of buses to serve passengers across a city, as shown in Figure 2.9.
Traditionally, such management involves manual data collection, such as monitoring
the sale of tickets and following up on demand along particular routes. These, however,
prove somewhat inefficient as buses might run on a strict schedule without considering
actual needs. This sometimes causes overcrowding at the stop or along the route, even
though some buses are still running. With modern fleet management powered by
technologies like AIoT, such inefficiencies can be redressed with real-time updates and
dynamic decision-making. For instance, every bus in the fleet can be equipped with a
tracking device and sensors to transmit data about where it is, who is on board, and how
many people expect to board at upcoming stops into a central control system. The
system analyses this data to optimise fleet operations, like fine-tuning bus fre-
quencies, rerouting buses to higher-demand areas, and deploying extra buses when
needed [17]. The real-time data continuously collected allows for the optimisation
of bus schedules and routes that consume less fuel and deliver better overall
satisfaction of passengers. This means fewer chances of overcrowding, with buses
reaching destinations on schedule, making passenger transport relatively more
comfortable and reliable [17].

2.4.4 Predictive maintenance for vehicles

Figure 2.10 illustrates the predictive maintenance process. In the automobile
industry, predictive maintenance is expressed as monitoring the condition of a
vehicle’s subsystem or component for diagnosis of faults or defects and forecasting
maintenance requirements for the vehicle [18]. One illustration of predictive
maintenance of systems is the braking system. For example, the braking system can
monitor the sensors’ output responses to determine if the brake pad is wearing out
and advise the driver to take action [19]. Traditionally, organisations have devel-
oped maintenance regimes based on fixed time intervals or distance travelled.
These maintenance regimes do not consider specific uses and particular wear of
respective vehicles [18]. In contrast, predictive maintenance employs algorithms
that analytically process data in real-time through real-time data from the vehicle’s
sensors attached to the engine. After processing, the algorithms can demonstrate
predicted behavioural trends or identify potential incidents that may serve as
warnings for planned maintenance, such as worn-out brakes or engine failures. A
sophisticated form of predictive maintenance combines other forms of data with a
model that has been trained with pre-processed data, which anticipates the assault
of any physical, electrical, or temperature data, or simply incoming live raw sensor
data about the vehicle’s condition to evaluate, recommend, or alert an incident [19].

2.5.1 Case study 1: Predictive maintenance in electric vehicles

In this study, we focus on a new form of predictive maintenance in EVs that gathers
information about the impact of regeneration braking on the battery. We will deter-
mine if it is possible to use this information to predict the degradation of the battery
and time before failure. For that reason, a LiFePO4 EV cell was charged and dis-
charged several times, often ending with acceleration disabled or enabled. Voltage,
temperature, capacity, and internal resistances of the cell during cycling were
recorded. State of Health Level (SHL) and Total Available Energy (TAE) measure-
ments from the final discharge phase were used to construct classifiers. We also
investigated the degradation monitoring accuracy with a battery at full capacity his-
tory [19]. This research furnished new empirical data for EV batteries, particularly
about how regenerative braking impacts the life of these batteries under diverse
conditions of travel. Such a proactive approach would enable better predictive
maintenance and is likely to avoid any unwarranted battery failure [19].

  This type of empirical evidence on SHL, TAE, and battery health under different
travel scenarios is of immense value in the discovery of new insights into battery per-
formance. Such information becomes useful for battery manufacturers and the devel-
opers of EVs in enhancing the methods of battery health assessment so that the overall
reliability of the EV batteries becomes easier to assess. Research is also informed by this
piece towards contributing to the development of smart-charging schemes about the
exploitation of the energy storage capabilities of EVs. Optimising these schemes to
minimise energy consumption benefits the owner of an EV and also benefits the energy
supplier by minimising the power consumption in the whole system [19].

  To this effect, this study draws attention to the appreciation of the impact of
operation conditions on health. Such a new understanding may thus lead to new
methods of better-quality battery evaluation that promote longer life and superior
performance. By emphasising these factors, the study helps decompose several fears
associated with the dependability of EV batteries. Currently, a few models of EVs
exist for study and improvements, so the findings generated from this study are
critical to further advance battery technology and strengthen the spread of EVs [19].

2.5.2 Case study 2: Fleet management optimisation

In this case study, a large automotive Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM)

driving conditions over long distances, and fuel consumption, were also collected. The
main objective of the data collected here was to improve the analysis of customer care
with the optimisation of scheduling and servicing the dealer network. By resorting to
such a large dataset, the OEM aimed to achieve better vehicle reliability concerning
customer satisfaction and minimise unseasoned breakdown [19]. To achieve pre-
dictive maintenance and optimisation of fleet management, data was processed in
more than 150 features. These focused on the areas, such as fuel-saving metrics, fault

2.6 Challenges and solutions in AIoT implementation

The section below lists the problems encountered during the implementation of
AIoT in transportation and the solutions developed to address them.

2.6.1 Technical challenges: Scalability, data privacy, and
  security

Artificial intelligence coupled with the Internet of Things, AIoT has stemmed inno-
vative applications, but this also comes along with quite significant technical chal-
lenges. Scale issues are much at the forefront because an enormous amount of data is
being handled by the growing number of interconnected devices that AIoT systems
handle efficiently [20]. As the size of the network increases, so does the need for
scaling the ability of data storage and processing without letting bottlenecks affect the
level of performance. Solutions to this include adopting scalable cloud services and
utilising edge computing in which data is processed locally to reduce latency and
obviate the need for a heavy load on central servers [21]. Data privacy is another major
issue. IoT devices tend to collect sensitive information that needs strong measures for
data protection. Solutions to such issues include strong encryption techniques, secure
authentication mechanisms, and data anonymisation techniques for protecting IoT
devices against unauthorised access and resultant data breaches [20,21]. Security
challenges are intertwined with these concerns, as the interconnected nature of AIoT
systems makes them attractive targets for cyberattacks. To secure potential threats and
maintain general system integrity, multiple layered security approaches have been
recommended including reliable access controls, firmware updates on schedule intru-
sion detection systems, and incorporating secure boot mechanisms [21].

2.6.2 Infrastructure and integration issues

There is a modern challenge to AIoT in transportation, such as significant infrastructure
and integration challenges. One of the major problems to be addressed will have to do
36 AIoT for smart transportation

with the interoperability of different technologies and transport systems. For instance,
when communication takes place among autonomous vehicles, smart traffic lights, and
intelligent management systems for transportation, the interference of multiple commu-
nication protocols and data formats can disrupt the exchange of information that is meant
to be well-coordinated in real time [21]. To conquer this challenge, the industry must
develop universal standards and protocols that include similar V2X communication to
allow all parts of the transportation network to communicate with each other.
Furthermore, the infrastructure supporting AIoT in transportation needs to have the
ability to process large volumes of data in real time. The existing road networks and
traffic management systems may not be armed to support the high-speed, low-latency
demands of AIoT applications such as real-time traffic monitoring or navigation for self-
driving vehicles. Some solutions to these demands would include the improvement of
infrastructure through the integration of high-speed communication networks such as 5G
and the widespread incorporation of edge computing [22]. Integrating AI technologies
into existing transportation systems also presents challenges, as it requires significant
computational resources and compatibility with legacy systems. Modular and scalable AI
models that can be deployed incrementally and ensure compatibility with current infra-
structure through gradual, phased integration are presented as solutions [22].

2.6.3 Regulatory, ethical, and social implications

The implementation of AIoT in modern transportation raises ample regulatory,
ethical, and social implications that need great attention for its proper execution. On
the regulatory front, any transportation system that incorporates AIoT technologies
needs to travel under complex and constantly evolving legal frameworks [22].
Regulations need to work towards issuing standards for safety in autonomous vehi-
cles, data privacy issues concerning tracking a vehicle and the information of its
passengers, and accountability of AI systems when deciding on certain things.
Because technological changes are gradual, clear, and adaptive regulations that keep
in line with those improvements must be developed for safety and compliance pur-
poses [22,23]. Ethically, applications of AIoT in the transportation sector have to be
designed in a manner that works reasonably and transparently. For instance, self-
driving cars should be programmed to make ethical choices during emergencies [21].
The algorithms used to manage traffic cannot feature biases that may favour parti-
cular groups or individuals. Therefore, making AI systems explainable and trans-
parent in their decision-making processes would maintain public trust. Socially, the
impact of AIoT on transportation has its associated positive and negative sides.
Safety, efficiency, and access to transportation systems can be improved with the
application of AIoT, but traditionally, it may cause loss of jobs for drivers and
increased data collection may also worry people over privacy. This can be achieved
by social implications of workforce transition programmes, the protection of personal
data ensured through strong privacy protections, and equitable distribution of the
benefits of AIoT technology to various sections of society. Engaging with commu-
nities, fostering public dialogue, and developing policies that balance innovation with
societal needs can help navigate these challenges effectively.

2.7 Future trends and innovations

Trends and innovations for the future of AIoT in transport are listed below.

2.7.1 Emerging technologies in AIoT for transportation

The future of transportation is being profoundly influenced by several emerging
AIoT technologies. Perhaps, one of the most impactful rollouts has been made with
5G connectivity. It assures ultra-low latency and high bandwidth that will enable
faster real-time communications between various vehicles, infrastructures, and
even central systems. 5G also enables more complex applications like Vehicle-to-
Everything communications which improve traffic management, vehicle safety,
and generally transport efficiency. The future evolution towards 6G will more
likely unlock even more significant progress in terms of data transfer speed and net-
work reliability [21,22]. Edge computing is another great innovation brought about
by AIoT. Because data is processed closer to where it is generated rather than being
primarily dependent on centralised cloud servers, edge computing avoids latency and
bandwidth. This is especially critical in applications that have to have data processed
immediately like autonomous vehicles. Sensors and cameras installed in the vehicle
as well as infrastructure must process real-time data to ensure safety and operational
efficiency [22].

  Artificial Intelligence, coupled with machine learning, is moving up every day
and thus offers more sophistication in predictive analytics and decision-making
processes. The algorithms of AI can analyse enormous volumes of data to optimise
routes, manage traffic flow, enhance the functionality of autonomous systems, and
much more [22]. These algorithms can learn from historical data and real-time data
and will modify over time to make better judgements. Another area in which
blockchain is coming out as a key tool in securing data within transportation is data
security and its associated transparency. This is because, owing to its decentralised
nature, blockchain technology will come up with a secure method of dealing with
data transactions, provide vehicle-to-vehicle communication, and establish trans-
parent supply chains for fleet management. Therefore, the integration of AIoT
within smart infrastructures will facilitate highly adaptive transport systems.
Scalability is one of the most critical issues in any AIoT system since the systems
need to be versatile and function in different situations. For example, AIoT systems
have an intelligence scaling advantage through edge computing where data is
processed at the periphery of the networks reducing dependency on cloud provision
and ensuring sustainable timely resolution of issues. This enhances the probability
of better traffic management, leading to the creation of more intelligent and
effective transportation networks [23].

2.7.2 Impact of AIoT on future mobility and transportation
  trends

AIoT technologies are set to have a profound impact on future mobility and trans-
portation trends. One of the most anticipated changes in the near future is the

adoption of autonomous vehicles. Autonomous vehicles will receive a large chunk of
inputs and instructions from AIoT, which will aid in navigation, decision-making,
and interaction with their surroundings. Autonomy will reduce human error, increase
traffic flow, and provide novel mobility solutions like ride services on demand and
autonomous public transport [24]. Another dimension of its influence will be on the
concept of a smart city, as AIoT will see transportation systems seamlessly integrated
with other urban infrastructure. This will result in the usage of real-time data which
will optimise traffic signals and reduce travel time. In exchange, it will be presented
with fewer levels of congestion accompanied by better air quality and lower carbon
emissions in the urban transportation network [24].

  The future mobility landscape would include shared and sustainable transpor-
tation solutions. AIoT technologies would help handle shared mobility services,
such as car-sharing or bike-sharing schemes, through optimisation of fleet opera-
tions and real-time availability information. Shifting towards shared transportation
models would drive away the grip of vehicles over the urban population and
compel people to become more sustainable in their mode of mobility. Overall,
AIoT has tremendous potential to greatly alter the ways and means through which
transportation systems are moved and interacted with. It will engineer a smarter,
more connected, and more efficient transportation service arrangement – a trans-
portation future that is more sustainable, personalised, and responsive to the needs
of modern urban environments.

2.8 Conclusion

Artificial Intelligence of Things, in itself, is profoundly altering the transportation
landscape through its seamless integration of advanced artificial intelligence with
interconnected devices. This has, therefore, led to tremendous innovations, such as
smart traffic management systems, and autonomous and connected vehicles, as
well as improved fleet management and predictive maintenance. From the opera-
tional frameworks and core technologies outlined within this entire chapter, AIoT
has been developing and shifting towards increasing roles in ensuring that trans-
portation is both sustainable and efficient. The real-world applications and case
studies further boost this transformative impact of AIoT. Such examples indicate
that successful implementations of AIoT deal with concrete challenges and help
deliver actual added value. Despite these significant steps forward, challenges
remain today. These range from technical barriers through scalability and privacy
of data to infrastructure and regulatory issues. The future will feature the injection
of technologies such as 5G, edge computing, and advanced sensors into the trans-
portation sector that will power the next wave of breakthroughs – the additional
smartness and responsiveness of the system. In short, AIoT technologies are going
to revolutionise the game of transportation by adding the much-needed efficiency,
safety, and user experience that they need. Continued evolution of these technol-
ogies paves the pathway towards intelligent, interconnected, and sustainable
transport systems.

3.2 History of ITS

In building ITS, the system has gone through several stages that differentiate it

3.3.3 Edge computing and cloud integration
3.3.3.1 Edge computing

If you are not familiar with edge computing, it essentially means taking the pro-
cessing of data closer to its source rather than sending everything back to a central
data centre. This has many good things for transportation:

l Low latency: This is important for time-sensitive decisions, such as making
  decisions in millions of a seconds like autonomous driving.

l Bandwidth efficiency: Processing data locally and sending only important
  information to the cloud can reduce the demand for high bandwidth.

l Improved reliability: Critical applications can continue running uninterrupted
  in the event of a loss of connectivity to the cloud due to local processing.

3.3.3.2 Cloud integration
Cloud integration provides us with scalable computational power and storage to
process big datasets, run AI algorithms at large scale, as well as provide platforms
for machine learning model development [6]. It allows:

l Scalability: We can scale the processing capabilities up and down depending
  on our data ingestion workload.
  Advancement in intelligent transportation systems using AIoT 45

l Data collection: A plethora of data points are coming from multiple directions,
  all making their way to a data pool with adjusted data aggregation.

l Collaboration and sharing: Cloud platforms facilitate sharing in a way that
  different sectors, for instance, government agencies, transportation companies,
  researchers, etc. can easily share data and collaborate.

Edge to cloud integration
With the combination of both cloud integration and edge computing, we can utilise
the low latency and reliability of edge computing but also leverage the processing
power and analytics capabilities in the cloud [7]. This integration helps with:

l Real-time applications: Self-driving and real-time traffic enforcement.
l Big data analytics: This serves well for policy-making, long-term planning,

  and smoothening the entire system.

  AIoT in transportation is the confluence of IoT sensors, wireless communica-

cloud resources to enable smarter, safer, efficient transportation systems.

3.5 Safety and security

3.5.1 Collision Avoidance Systems
3.5.1.1 Advanced Driving Assistance Systems (ADAS)

Collision prevention systems are one of the most prevalent applications for AIoT
technology within ADAS [14]. ADAS are safety-critical systems that can detect
imminent collision threats according to sensor data fusion such as radar, LiDAR,
camera, and using AI algorithms and activate preventive measures. Some examples
include:

l Automatic Emergency Braking (AEB) System: When the mechanism indicates
  that a crash with other cars, pedestrians, or obstacles may be imminent, an
  alarm is produced and the device automatically applies brakes to avoid colli-
  sions/entities associated with damage or reduce vehicle speed.

l Lane Departure Alert (LDA)/Lane Keep Assist (LKA): As the ADAS system
  perceives that the vehicle is departing from its lane when detected by sensing
  the pitch angle of the vehicle with respect to lane lines, it alerts the driver
  through steering wheel vibrations/visual and audio warnings.

l Blind Spot Monitoring (BSM): Warns the driver of vehicles in their blind spots.

3.5.1.2 Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication
V2V communication allows cars to spontaneously share and track their speed,
position, and headed intention with other cars to support cooperative safety appli-
cations. Some examples include:

l Collision Prevention: using V2V [15] and roadside infrastructure cars can
  predict possible collisions, especially in intersections when a car intends
  to turn.
  Advancement in intelligent transportation systems using AIoT 49

l Emergency Electronic Brake Lights (EEBL): if a lead vehicle brakes suddenly
  on a wet road surface, its brake system will send notifications to following cars
  which may be out of sight yet.

3.5.3 Cyber security and solutions
3.5.3.1 Challenges

With the increase in the interconnection between transportation systems and
dependency on the internet, several cyber security [17] challenges and risks have
come up which are:

l Data privacy: protecting private information derived from automotive, street,
  and traveller data.

l Vulnerabilities: the entire transportation ecosystem is vulnerable to hacks,
  malware, and other cyber threats, which can cause major disruptions or reduce
  safety and accuracy.

l Interoperability issues: secure communication becomes a major challenge in
  multi-vendor systems with different subsystems and devices.

3.5.3.2 Solutions
The mitigation of these security challenges requires the dependence on robust,
solution-oriented, and proactive redirecting cyber security measures in transporta-
tion networks:
50 AIoT for smart transportation

l Encryption algorithms and protocols have been commonly applied in trans-
  portation AIoT applications to protect data transmission sensitivity between
  vehicles, infrastructure, or cloud systems.

l Authentication and access control: All critical systems or devices need multi-
  factor authentication, as well as identifying rigorous user policies to ensure
  only the right users are able to log on or link before they can connect to them.

l IDS (Intrusion Detection System): It is an integral part of Network Traffic
  Monitoring where the arrival of anomalous activities and potential cyber-
  attacks are swiftly detected and recognised.

l Software and firmware updates in a timely manner: Vulnerabilities are being
  found all the time so you need to constantly be updating your systems.
  Simultaneously, new malicious code, ransomware, or worms emerge all the
  time. An important action is remote software and firmware updates which if
  executed on time can prevent the cyber attacks from happening in the first
  place and protect critical data.

l Several cyber security industry standards and regulations such as the National
  Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) or the International
  Organization for Standardization (ISO) have been proposed and published for
  cyber security in transportation systems, but these do not extensively cover
  potential vulnerabilities from Cybersecurity and Emerging Solutions (CAES).
  These standards and guidelines will require transportation organisations and
  service providers to take these measures to manage and mitigate cyber risks.

  In summary, safety and security in transportation can be significantly
improved by implementing advanced collision prevention systems, emergency
services, and incident management, as well as addressing cyber security challenges
with effective solutions. All these efforts can make the entire transportation eco-
system safer, more reliable, and more resilient.
