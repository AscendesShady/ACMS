  This decentralised approach provides enhanced security, transparency, and
resilience compared to traditional, centralised databases, as there is no single point of
failure, and records are verified and updated across multiple nodes simultaneously.

11.7.2.3 Transactions
When combining an ITS with blockchain, the information that needs to be stored in
transactions must include both transportation-related data and be aligned with the
fundamental structure of blockchain. Blockchain transactions typically contain the
following key information.

  Sender and receiver information: Specifies which party is sharing data or
assets with whom. In ITS, this could represent data sharing between a vehicle,
sensor, or road infrastructure with other systems.

  Timestamp: Records the exact time the transaction took place. In ITS, it can be
used to log when specific traffic data or events (e.g., an accident, road closure) occurred.

  Transaction Data: This is where the actual transaction data is stored. In ITS,
this could include transportation system data like vehicle locations, speed infor-
mation, road conditions, traffic light scenarios, or accident reports.

  Transaction Fee: The fee paid for processing the transaction in blockchain. In
ITS, if there is a fee system for data transfer or services, this information could be
included here.

  Signature: A digital signature used to verify the identity of the transaction
sender. In ITS, it is used to authenticate the identity of the party sending the data.

  Block Hash: Refers to the previous block and ensures the integrity of the
transaction chain.

  The algorithm for the phases of a transaction is as follows:
  Phase 1: Transaction proposal and endorsement: A transaction proposal is
created using vehicle data (vehicleData), client ID (clientID), and chaincode ID
(chaincodeID). Endorsing peers validate the proposal and send the endorsed mes-
sage back to the client.
  Phase 2: Transaction submission and ordering: Endorsed messages, along
with vehicle data, are sent to the Fabric gateway. The gateway forwards these mes-
sages to the ordering nodes (orderer peers). Ordering nodes verify the signatures on
the messages, add them to blocks, and broadcast the blocks to other nodes.
  Phase 3: Transaction validation and commitment: Transactions within the
received blocks are validated. If the signatures are valid and the endorsement
policies are met, the transaction is marked as valid and committed to the ledger.
Otherwise, the transaction is rejected.

11.7.2.4 Block structure
Block hash: This is the cryptographic summary of all the data within a block. It
ensures the integrity of the transactions within the block. If any transaction is
altered, the block hash changes, which results in breaking the chain.

  Previous block hash: A block contains the hash of the previous block, which
enables the cryptographic linking of blocks. This ensures that each block in the
chain is connected to the previous block, securing past transactions.
  Blockchain technology and cyber security in ITS 229

  Transactions: A list of transactions contained within the block. In ITS, these
transactions could represent road conditions, traffic data, communication infor-
mation between vehicles, or infrastructure elements (traffic lights, sensors). Each
transaction includes a specific timestamp, sender and receiver information, and
data details.

  Block size: Indicates the total size of all transactions within the block. In ITS
applications, since each transaction consists of traffic data or sensor information,
the block size varies depending on the density of the data.

11.7.2.5 Consensus and smart contract
In blockchain networks, all transactions from the first transaction of the chain are
stored on distributed nodes. In the ITS network, these nodes will be server struc-
tures in a distributed architecture. The consensus or agreement protocol ensures
that the ledgers held by these distributed nodes remain identical and synchronised
at all times. These standard consensus protocols ensure that all data is stored
redundantly across multiple points.

  Smart contracts, on the other hand, are small code fragments running within
the ITS infrastructure. These codes perform checks such as ensuring the correct
flow of data, confirming accuracy by the involved parties, and verifying whether
the current application is functioning properly.

11.8 Security measures of ITS and BC (blockchain)
  integration

There are methods to attack any system with network infrastructure, so it is also
possible for cyberattacks to target a blockchain-based ITS infrastructure. In addi-
tion to cyberattacks, blockchain-based systems can be targeted by attacks specific
to this infrastructure. Beyond blockchain attacks and cyberattacks, ensuring data
security and data privacy is also essential. Therefore, security measures can be
evaluated under three main headings.
230 AIoT for smart transportation

11.8.1 Cyberattacks

Cyberattacks known as Denial of Service (DoS) and DDoS are high-volume attacks
aimed at disrupting services and can occur at almost every layer, from layer 3 to
layer 7. A DoS attack involves a high-volume attack from a single IP address, while
a DDoS attack refers to high-volume attacks on a network from multiple sources.
These attacks can be prevented using cybersecurity products that filter volumetric
attacks and allow only normal traffic to pass through. Recently, these solutions
have also been implemented in cloud environments. Servers that store data from the
blockchain-based ITS network can be hosted in a cloud environment, with a DDoS
protection solution deployed in front of them.

  Additionally, Man in the Middle (MITM) attack is very popular, especially in
networks where there is a flow of information between two peers. MITM can be
prevented using cryptographic algorithms and also, cyber security products such as
NAC (Network Access Control) can be applied to prevent unauthorised devices
from connecting to the network [43].

  Certificates should be created for the vehicle and nodes. These certificates not
only help to ensure secure data transmission but also provide mutual authentication
between users and servers. Besides certificates, ensuring endpoint security using
antivirus and endpoint detection and response solutions (AV and EDR) for all
devices in the network infrastructure is very important in terms of threats such as
viruses, malware, and adware.

  If the vehicle systems using blockchain technology are somehow exposed to the
internet environment or if there is an application developed with data received from
there, they should be protected against web attacks that may come from the internet
environment with cybersecurity products such as web application firewalls (WAF),
intrusion prevention and detection systems (IDS, IPS). In addition, using a data loss
prevention (DLP) solution to prevent data leakage will provide security at the data layer.

11.8.2 Blockchain attacks

Just like network structures, there are cyber attacks on blockchain infrastructures,
some of which may be cyber attacks on network infrastructures mentioned in the
previous heading, while others are attacks on the basic concepts of blockchain. The
most well-known blockchain attacks are sybil attacks, pool attacks, and majority
attacks. A sybil attack is an attack type that aims to take control of more than one
peer in the network and aims to cut off the peer’s relationship with other peers. A
sybil attack is a type of attack on peer-to-peer networks that involves taking control
of more than one peer. Thus, the peer whose communication with the network is cut
off remains outside the network [44].

  Majority and pool attacks are types of attacks that typically target validators or
miners in open blockchains. Miners are responsible for keeping the ledgers up-to-
date and implementing consensus, and they usually join a mining pool to increase
their hash power. If the majority of miners (more than 50%) or mining pools are
compromised, it can cause financial losses or reputational damage to the block-
chain network. Furthermore, consensus mechanisms sometimes use algorithms
  Blockchain technology and cyber security in ITS 231

such as Proof of Work (PoW) or Proof of Stake (PoS). An attacker who gains
majority control under these mechanisms can manipulate the network and poten-
tially cause an incorrect block to be written. Such an attack can result in both
financial losses and a loss of trust in the network.

  In private blockchain networks, since there are no miners, attacks may directly
target the consensus mechanism, smart contracts, or, if present, the identity infra-
structure. Additionally, the network infrastructure must be well-designed and pro-
tected against potential takeover attacks. To prevent disruptions between peers, the
network connections’ signal quality must be maintained at a high level [45,46].

  Consensus attacks are generally carried out with the intent of disrupting ser-
vices through DoS/DDoS attacks. As a solution, DDoS prevention services should
be employed. Attacks targeting smart contracts can jeopardise business logic and
network performance. To prevent this, smart contract code must be designed within
a secure software development life cycle (SDLC), and the code should undergo a
risk analysis before being deployed to the production environment. This helps to
avoid smart contract code manipulation [47].

  If the digital identity library is manipulated, it may cause issues with access
control to the network. Therefore, the servers hosting the digital identity library
must be protected against cyberattacks.

11.8.3 Data privacy and data security

Ensuring data privacy and data security is critically important for an ITS operating
on a blockchain infrastructure. Data manipulation can lead to vehicle accidents,
misdirections, and numerous other issues within the vehicle network.

  For data privacy, techniques such as user anonymisation, data encryption,
transaction encryption, and data sharing policies can be implemented. Since
anonymisation and encryption of transactions and data are fundamental concepts of
blockchain, ensuring data privacy may be more straightforward. Additionally,
policies should be applied regarding which data can be shared, and all data on the
network should be classified. After classification, DLP should be implemented to
control the sharing of critical data externally.

  Cryptographic signatures, distributed ledger technology, and consensus algo-
rithms in blockchain technology ensure data security. Furthermore, strong authen-
tication methods should be used to enhance data security. In terms of data security,
it is also important to periodically back up data and analyse log records with a
SIEM product [48].

11.9 Conclusions

This section discusses the potential benefits and challenges of integrating block-
chain technology and cybersecurity solutions into ITS. While the data-driven nat-
ure of ITS is vulnerable to security and privacy threats, blockchain technology has
the potential to minimise these threats. Blockchain provides transparent and secure
data sharing, while cybersecurity solutions protect the integrity, confidentiality, and
accessibility of the system. The integrated use of these technologies will not only

contribute to the enhancement of security in the future development of ITS but will
also increase users’ trust in the system, thus leading to wider adoption of these
systems. The integration of blockchain and cybersecurity technologies into ITS
plays a key role in building sustainable and secure transportation systems.

12.5 Ethical considerations

The strong use of technology in future transportation systems is accompanied by
broad ethical questions, which need to be carefully addressed and resolved as they
become more relevant [29]. As our communities continue to move towards more
independently operated cars, smart infrastructure, and connected transportation
systems, we must accept the ethical debates that result from this shift. In this
exchange, we will explore the major ethical challenges facing the future of trans-
portation mobility, diving into the complications and challenges – and essentials – of
ethical technology integration in transportation sector solutions.

(a) Security and welfare: The future of transport mobility revolves around safety
  and welfare – the two most ethical of all things! The coming years will see a
  significant focus on the ethical implications of autonomous vehicles and advanced
  driver assistance systems, particularly regarding passenger safety, pedestrians, and
  other road users. There is an ethical obligation to pursue strategies to reduce risk,
  prevent harm, and ensure public health and safety. Furthermore, when it comes to
  ethical safety concerns in future transportation, the considerations should extend
  towards examining who takes responsibility for accidents and what are the pos-
  sibilities of technology failures along with whether regulations would need to be
  modified suitably over these emerging technologies.
  Future of mobility 249

(b) Equity and accessibility: In planning a transportation system of the future, it is
  essential to put fairness and availability first to ensure that everyone shares in
  better mobility outcomes. Ethics in transportation planning should focus on
  equity and fairness by affording everyone equal access to multiple ways of
  moving. It also needs to fit the requirements of varied populations and work
  towards filling mobility gaps in order not to heighten socioeconomic dis-
  parities. That means things like considering the impacts that new transportation
  technologies have on disadvantaged communities and creating holistic strate-
  gies for providing transit access to everyone, regardless of ability or income.

(c) Confidentiality and data security: The increasing interconnectedness of
  transportation networks with a data bias brings ethical questions about privacy
  and how information is handled. One of the main ethical duties is to protect
  user privacy and data security whenever personal and behavioural mobility data
  are collected and processed. It is essential to ensure that data protection is
  handled responsibly and that data collection and storage are transparent
  regarding how the data is used. Three major components of ethical transpor-
  tation practices are data protection, privacy, and security, with a key emphasis
  on preserving data anonymity and giving individuals real control over their
  personal information.

(d) Ecological consequences: It intersects with the moral concern for environ-
  mental harm as transportation technologies continue to advance, emphasising
  the need to pursue environmentally sustainable and eco-friendly transportation
  systems. With long-term global concerns about reducing the carbon footprint
  and environmental damage, ethical transportation should prioritise sustainable
  technology, including electric vehicle adoption, environment-friendly design
  practices, and comprehensive environmental impact assessments. Making
  ethical choices about the future of transportation mobility should ensure
  emissions, resource consumption, and ecological impact are considered first.

(e) Societal and community impact: The influence of transport mobility on society
  and communities presents an acute challenge for the future of transportation
  infrastructure as well as services. Good mobility planning efforts should minimise
  negative impacts (e.g., noise, traffic congestion, urban sprawl) and maximise
  benefits to the community’s social health, quality of life, and unity. Such questions
  include the impact of transportation innovation on urban development, regional
  economies, and community well-being. More fully evaluating and mitigating the
  social impacts of ethically compliant transportation practices is also crucial.

(f) Ethical governance and regulation: Effective governance and regulation is an
  important consideration for future transportation mobility addressing ethical
  issues. Ethical governance systems ensure transparency, accountability, and
  well-defined processes in responding to stakeholders. These mechanisms
  ensure the creation and deployment of transportation technologies are ethical
  and derive from the values of the public. Effective regulatory frameworks are
  essential for both promoting technological innovation and protecting the public
  interest while upholding ethical values such as security and the well-being of
  individuals and communities affected by transportation upgrades.
250 AIoT for smart transportation

(g) Ethical Artificial Intelligence and automation: The integration of AI, machine
  learning, and automation into transportation systems requires careful considera-
  tion of algorithmic bias, liability, neutrality, and human oversight. It is pertinent
  that automated systems work ethically and transparently per societal norms,
  which is a requirement for the successful integration of transportation mobility
  with modern technologies. This includes addressing challenges around decision-
  making protocols, data-induced biases, and the ethics surrounding ceding
  authority to automated systems within transportation management and decision-
  making.

(h) Human-centred design, user experience: The future of transport mobility
  ethics must focus on human-centred design, with user experience at its core and
  consideration for the diverse needs of users. Ethical design methods should
  uphold the guiding principle of prioritising user safety, comfort, empowerment
  etc. while taking all possible measures to mitigate any negative consequences
  of integrating evolving transportation technology into the system. In addition,
  ethical considerations like collecting input from diverse user groups, engaging
  users in the design and development of transportation technologies, and
  ensuring equitable access to transportation innovations will help shape the
  future of mobility.

Finally, ethical dimensions play an invaluable role in shaping the future of trans-
portation mobility by adhering to the highest values of society and promoting
human welfare while navigating the complex challenges and opportunities brought
by transportation innovation. The fact that a perfectly ethical transportation system
may not be achievable does not mean that stakeholders should not strive to develop,
implement, and manage future transportation solutions with ethical principles at
their core.

12.6 Conclusion

Urban transport is about to change thanks to technology and innovation. As the
world continues its journey on this transformational path, navigation through future
transport mobility technologies promises to be safe, secure, and equitable while it
benefits society. We have no choice but to engage with the possible challenges and
ethical dilemmas presented by these advances. The future of mobility must be
private. Smart transportation raises an ethical issue: Automation and autonomy
cause employment displacement. To mitigate the impact on workers and to ensure
that the benefits of technology advance towards those whose livelihoods are
affected, it is essential to conduct retraining and reskilling programmes for dis-
placed dependent employees and help them transition into new roles. The appli-
cation of AIoT systems for important transportation tasks will raise ethical
questions regarding safety and reliability, and resilience in the face of life-
threatening situations. The solution to this issue lies in ensuring security and
planning for system failures in smart transportation systems, both in their design
and operation. AI should be used ethically to optimise traffic patterns and routes, as
  Future of mobility 251

these efforts will ultimately help our cities while ensuring justice, transparency, and
accountability. Therefore, transparency, inclusivity, and the public’s best interest
are vital to realising the full potential of Smart Transportation Networks and
shaping a future of ethical, sustainable, and equitable digital mobility.
