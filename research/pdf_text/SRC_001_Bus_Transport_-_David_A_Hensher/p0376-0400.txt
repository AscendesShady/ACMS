  One of the themes used in support of a particular urban public transport investment is its environmental beneﬁts. There
is a widespread view that rail is environmentally more friendly than bus. What is very unclear in this position is the precise
meaning of environmental friendliness. There are many elements that deﬁne the underlying environmental consequences of
the delivery of public transport, beginning with sourcing of resources, manufacture, implementation and operation.
Alternative sources of energy deliver different emission outcomes; and when combined with the use of public transport,
provide a multidimensional set of emission indicators that should be used as the real descriptors of environmental
advantage.

  To highlight the risk of presuming the environmental advantage of rail, consider the following evidence as presented in
Puchalsky (2005). Bus rapid transit (BRT) is an evolving public transport mode consisting of rubber-tyred vehicles running
on dedicated rights-of way (ROW) for all or part of a transit route (see Hensher et al., 2014). The vehicles are typically
diesel powered, although some are dual diesel and electric. BRT has emerged as a low-cost contender against light rail
transit (LRT) in many situations, and even heavy rail. The relative merits of both modes are often debated generically and
for speciﬁc applications (see O’Toole, 2008; Hensher et al., 2015). One area of comparison is energy use and emissions.
Puchalsky (2005) puts the case as follows: electric rail vehicles emit no propulsion system pollution at their point of
operation. They are responsible for fuel cycle emissions from electricity-generating plants, which tend to be located on the
urban periphery, and other upstream processes. Diesel buses, however, have typically been perceived as producing strongly
negative pollution-related externalities directly into the high density areas that they serve. Diesel buses are also responsible
for other emissions due to reﬁning and other processes in the fuel cycle. In the area of energy consumption, rail, with its
low-friction steel-on-steel support and guidance technology, has been assumed to be superior to rubber-tyred buses.

  Electric LRT and other rail passenger modes are often considered superior to diesel BRT and other bus modes in terms
of emissions because only of a consideration of the point of use,. Diesel technology, however, has improved dramatically
(post 1996 in particular) because of high-pressure injection systems, advanced after treatment systems, and other measures
(Arcadis and Miller, 1998); as demonstrated by the successive Euro engine standards1 which are applied not only

throughout Europe but also implemented in Australia (where all new heavy diesel vehicles already have to meet the strict

(carcinogenic) ﬁne particulate matter, a noteworthy environmental concern with older diesel vehicles. Compressed natural

decreasing oxides of nitrogen (NOx) and particulate emissions in particular. Advances have also occurred in the generation
of electricity. This changing technology landscape requires a re-examination of old paradigms.

  The challenge in comparing the environmental impacts of rail and bus-based systems is in deﬁning the precise resource
commitment of each mode. Our focus here is on urban public transport, and within this sub-sector we recognise that rail
track is often shared with other transport tasks such as freight rail movements and interurban rail. On the other hand, buses
are just one element of a mixed modal setting in the majority of contexts, except where there are dedicated lanes or systems
in place. Different jurisdictions have rules that prioritise modes in such a way that the presence of infrastructure is not
dependent on the urban public transport task. Another way of saying this is that the dominating cost is a shared cost
amongst modal tasks such that the avoidable costs that can be assigned to urban public transport are a relatively small
contribution to total life-cycle cost.

  In this chapter we focus on determining some of the avoidable environmental features associated with each public
transport mode. These are typically those energy and emission metrics, estimated from average operating conditions that
are commonly used to compare the performance of urban passenger heavy rail, light rail and bus. Before setting out an
empirical framework within which to assess the relative environmental advantage of speciﬁc urban public transport modes,
it is necessary to add some caveats. Where a public transport mode’s components are manufactured, and indeed operated,
will inﬂuence the environmental performance. Thus the evidence presented in this chapter must be qualiﬁed by such
considerations around variability and location-dependence. This chapter focusses on urban Australia and a comparison
between rail and bus. In this context, rail systems are driven by electricity primarily generated by coal-ﬁred power stations,
and buses in the main use diesel which is almost sulphur free. Furthermore, the relevance of key performance indicators
such as grams of CO2 emitted per passenger (or per passenger kilometre) will be heavily inﬂuenced by the role of the car as
the major effective competing mode. Despite these caveats, the message is one that can inform the debate on how clean or
otherwise are our public transport modes in a way that reminds analysts that a priori prejudices, where they exist, should be
carefully scrutinised. Importantly, this demonstration serves to show that any generalisation claiming one urban public
transport mode is always more environmentally advantageous will tend to be oversimpliﬁed and not necessarily apply in all
circumstances.

29.2 Gathering the evidence on environmental advantage

When trying to understand the total environmental impact of particular transport activities, life-cycle assessments
(i.e., analysis of the energy or emission performance associated with each stage of a vehicle’s product-life from ‘cradle to
grave’dfrom the initial raw materials used in vehicle manufacturing through to the eventual disposal/retirement of the
vehicle) can prove useful. Typically, when framing transport life-cycle assessments there are three main process categories
that are considered for inclusion in the analyses: vehicle processes, infrastructure processes and fuel/energy processing.
This chapter compares emissions across the full fuel cycle (and, where possible, vehicle cycle) for bus and rail. The term
‘fuel cycle’ refers to a complete accounting of emissions and energy use from primary feedstock extraction (such as crude
oil production or coal mining) though ﬁnal energy use (including petroleum reﬁning and distribution, or electricity
generation and transmission). Similarly, ‘vehicle cycle’ emissions are the complete emissions caused by the manufacture
(including raw materials extraction and processing), distribution, use (including repair and maintenance), and disposal of a
vehicle. ‘Infrastructure emissions’ are the extra emissions caused by the construction and operation of relevant transport
infrastructure (such as roads, parking, street lighting and trafﬁc control systems; or rail tracks, stations, signalling and
switching systems).

  Literature values for transport ‘life-cycle’ emissions most usually refer to analyses of what we refer to here as ‘vehicle
cycle’ totals (i.e., net emissions from vehicle and fuel supply as well as from vehicle operation), but occasionally also
include estimated components for ‘infrastructure emissions’ (i.e., cover emissions from infrastructure provision and
maintenance as well as from vehicle supply, fuel supply and vehicle operation). Though infrastructure and vehicle supply
emissions are not explicitly dealt with in the modal comparisons derived here, some assessment of their possible impacts

  Most emission estimates in the literature, for the enhanced greenhouse impact of transport, are presented in terms of
carbon dioxide (CO2) equivalent ‘direct’ emissions. That is, most values are weighted totals (relative to an equivalent mass
of CO2) which include only the ‘directly’ radiative gases CO2, CH4 (methane) and N2O (nitrous oxide), and do not include
the indirect effects of gases such as CO (carbon monoxide), NOx (nitrogen oxides) and NMVOCs (non-methane volatile
organic compounds) (see BITRE, 2009c). Such emission estimates generally refer to ‘energy end use’ (i.e., are from the
direct combustion of fuel in the transport vehicles), and do not include further energy used to extract, reﬁne or otherwise
provide that fuel for vehicle use.

  In addition to the inclusion of indirect warming effects, however, a more robust estimate for the total contribution
(to global climate change) of Australian transport should also allow for ‘full fuel cycle’ (FFC) emissions from transport
vehicle energy use. That is, sector totals are even higher if emissions released during transport fuel supply and pro-
cessing are included as well as those from the direct fuel combustion. FFC estimates include energy use and emissions
due to fuel extraction, power generation (e.g., for electric railways) and fuel reﬁning or conversion. FFC estimates in
previous BITRE studies (as a main source of evidence) have typically added between 10% and 20% to energy end use
emission totals for Australian transport (BITRE, 2010; BITRE, 2009c; BTRE, 2006; BTRE, 2002; BTCE, 1995).
Chester and Horvath (2009) have estimated fuel production to add in the order of 17% to average per kilometre CO2
emissions from the operation of a US sedandfurthermore, their study includes estimates of vehicle manufacture, adding
approximately another 10% to resulting FFC rates of CO2 emission for that sedan, and of additional lifecycle com-
ponents (such as vehicle maintenance and infrastructure provision) adding around a further 20%e25% to the resulting
‘vehicle cycle’ emissions. Similar estimates performed for the Canadian transport sector (Gagnon, 2006) derived values
of around 14% for the additional greenhouse emissions due to vehicle manufacturing (above CO2 emissions directly
from vehicles); and of approximately 10% extra from emissions related to vehicle maintenance and road network
operation.

  A related process is used to estimate FFC rail sector emissions, with the added complication that the particular mix of
primary fuels used by each State’s electricity generation sector has to be considered (e.g., the average upstream CO2
emission rate due to the electricity supply in Victoria is relatively high compared to other states, due to the signiﬁcant

  We have drawn on published (BITRE, 2009a; 2009b, 2010, 2014, 2015) and unpublished data compiled by the Bureau
of Infrastructure, Transport and Regional Economics (BITRE) to estimate modal emission intensities, in terms of grams of
CO2 equivalent emitted per passenger kilometre performed (gCO2-e/pkm). The calculations have been undertaken by
accumulating the following evidence. We begin with the annual passenger numbers (all trips, including transfers and
concessions) for each of the capital cities over the last two decades for the various public transit modes, where values for
buses in the city-by-city results are for urban public transport (UPT) route buses (i.e., does not yet include an allowance for
charter/hire operators’ contribution or for school services where these are not open to the general public). Using resulting
estimates of total metropolitan trips (i.e., summed across all the capitals) for the different modes of traveldwhere the ﬁnal
bus task values are scaled to refer to all commercial bus use, both private and publicd annual passenger-kilometre values
are then derived, based on known estimates of average trip lengths. This essentially covers steps 1 and 2.

  The next stage is to calculate relevant emission level estimates for the estimated annual passenger-kilometre values. The
most recent greenhouse gas estimates for the Australian transport sector, provided by BITRE, include direct-from-vehicle
CO2 emission estimates by mode (noting that energy end-use values for CO2 from rail transport relate to non-electric
railways, and are thus dominated by freight operationsdso the contributions of passenger and freight are separated by
reference to the rail total, see Fig. 29.7). National bus emission values relate to total bus kilometres performeddbased on
national vehicle stocks; surveys of vehicle use, such as the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ Survey of Motor Vehicle Use
(SMVU, ABS, 2015); and national fuel sales datadand thus include both urban and non-urban travel. Using urban transit
authority fuel audit data, and modelled estimates of the typical difference in fuel efﬁciency between city bus use and long-
distance coaches (where the extra congestion and stop-start conditions of most urban bus routes leads to substantially
higher average fuel consumption rates than for non-urban use), allows a subdivision of the national bus emission total into
estimated urban and non-urban components (see Fig. 29.7). Allowing for the different average driving conditions, load
factors and fuel type mix (where CNG has become a major fuel for many capital city bus ﬂeets) between urban and non-
urban bus use, urban passenger kilometres are thought to account for around 60%e65% of the current national bus total for
CO2. These calculations cover step 3 through to step 6.

  Next, we need to add in an allowance for upstream or energy supply/processing emissions. For road vehicles, these
extra CO2 emissions mostly refer to oil reﬁning, while for railways the most important component is that due to power
generation for electric trains and trams (light rail). This component of the emissions estimates are referred to by some
sources (such as previous Australian Greenhouse Ofﬁce/Department of Climate Change reports) as ‘indirect’ greenhouse
emissions. To provide heightened clarity, this chapter distinguishes the terminology of ‘upstream’ emissions as described
above (as part of Full Fuel Cycle or Life-cycle aggregate emission estimates) from the indirect emissions arising from
indirectly-warming gas species (such as CO, NOx and non-methane volatile organics). The terminology of this chapter
allows a more effective contrast with the directly-warming gases (such as CO2, CH4 and N2O), on whose concentrations
the indirect gases have various ﬂow-on impacts.

  We then add in the effects of the other two main direct gases typically included in greenhouse gas inventories (CH4 and
N2O), to the CO2 values. This provides the CO2-equivalent values across the Full Fuel Cycle for these direct gases. For the
rail emission aggregates, passenger rail accounts for something like a third of the current FFC total for the rail sector.
Allowances for the impacts of the main indirectly-warming gases (CO, NOx, NMVOCs) on the CO2-equivalent values
(by mode) are then included (where the effect of such indirect allowances varies considerably by modedtypically adding

been considerably higher in the past, and has probably more than halved in magnitude since the 1990s, due to successive
improvements in pollutant emissions standards for vehicles. This covers steps 7 and 8.

be calculated as well as end-use CO2 emissions; where the FFC rail aggregates have to include estimates of emissions due

emission contribution from electric power generation (due to various data availability constraints), some estimates of non-

analysis had to rely principally on assumed average emission rates (based largely on international literature values) applied

estimates of detailed national energy use (by fuel).

meaning that the derivation of emission intensity values, dealing with the urban passenger component of total fuel con-
sumption, typically have to rely on an element of transport ﬂeet modelling (in order to estimate the separate contributions

and issues around standardisation or inter-survey variability when having to compare survey results over relatively small
sample sizes, lead to some unavoidable approximation during the estimation process. The inherent uncertainty levels
within the resulting emission intensity estimates are probably around Æ10%.

29.4 Conclusions

The primary objective of this chapter is to investigate the claim that rail is always a much more environmentally sustainable
public transport passenger mode compared to bus. This is a difﬁcult task, challenged often by the absence of quality data
which requires a good understanding of the data to make sensible apportionments. The evidence herein suggests there are
particular features of the passenger task that give rail an advantage but that the environmental equation, in the Australian
context, is relatively emissions neutral when a full fuel cycle is taken into account. Both urban rail and bus currently offer a
  Can bus be cleaner and greener than rail? Chapter | 29 399

similar order of average emissions abatement (per passenger kilometre) over car travel. The average emissions intensity of
both urban rail and bus could decline substantially in the future, depending on what happens with bus engine technology
(such as further movements to battery hybridisation, bio-diesel or other low-CO2 fuels5) or electricity supply (particularly if
the Australian generation sector manages a signiﬁcant amount of eventual decarbonisation6).

  Crucially, the evidence suggests average intensities are highly dependent on patronage levels. If rail is able to increase
its patronage per kilometre as governments invest more in rail-based systems in Australian cities, the performance evidence
will likely swing in favour of rail. However, the observed growth patterns of urban sprawl in recent years, together with
forecasts of population growth concentrating central area densities in the next 30 years, suggest that the spatial distribution
of travel within all metropolitan areas is likely to require a substantial increase in bus services. In turn, these increases in
bus services might well exceed the expected investment in rail (both light and heavy). Importantly, it is likely that the
patronage of bus services will continue to be greater than rail in most capitals because rail services are focussed on a few,
normally radial, corridors, in contrast to the widespread travel needs of the low density metropolitan areas of Australian
capital cities. Annual rail passenger numbers are substantially higher than bus in Melbourne, but within the other capitals,
bus patronage matches or exceeds rail totals. This means that, across the eight capital cities, total rail and bus patronage
levels, for aggregate metropolitan passenger numbers, are currently comparable, with around 855 million rail passengers
and 704 million UPT bus passengers in 2014, as shown by Table 29.1. Indeed, in Brisbane, the patronage of the bus system
has outperformed the rail system since the turn of the century.

  Whilst rail can clearly effectively serve high density nodes, the bus is likely to continue to have a major role in
servicing distributed public transport needs. This means the relative modal share (in terms of passenger kilometres per-
formed) between urban rail and bus may change very little into the future. However, one factor that could possibly lead to
some level of divergence in future passenger kilometre shares relates to average trip lengths. Even though, as mentioned
above, total passengers by bus often exceed rail in most cities, the pkm comparisons are less favourable to busdwith
population growth and its inﬂuence on standard urban form having already led to a signiﬁcant difference in average trip
lengths between the modes. The aggregate modal estimates imply that the current average trip on (heavy) rail in Australian
metropolitan areas is over twice as long as the average UPT bus trip. The resulting metropolitan rail task, in aggregate
passenger kilometres, is substantially larger than that of bus traveldwith a 2014 total, across the eight capital cities, of
around 12.6 billion pkm for rail (both heavy and light), compared with about 5.5 billion pkm for UPT bus services
(see Table 29.1). Any further lengthening of the average urban rail trip, if standard city growth patterns continue, could
lead to correspondingly higher rail passenger kilometre levels, and a possible increase in the consequent emissions per
passenger trip (i.e., relative to shorter average trips, as may be feasible under more compact urban forms).

  Within the performance analyses presented here, one of the main areas that remain difﬁcult to quantify precisely
concerns the appropriate subdivision of aggregate data on railway energy use or emissions into contributions by the various
rail sub-sectors. Similar obstacles are posed by having to separate aggregate road fuel consumption into respective use by
the various vehicle types, over differing areas of operation (see Fig. 29.5 above for an indication of the FFC modal
emission trends). The uncertainties or assumptions required within such detailed modal analyses makes the average
‘performance’ values given in this chapter approximate, albeit informative. With the derivation of relative emissions
intensity estimates for bus and rail travel, comparability of the various series are hampered by the need to allow for UPT
buses having generally higher gCO2-e/pkm averages than national bus values, and by rail emissions depending so
sensitively on the particular electricity generation characteristics for the particular operating conditions. This difﬁcult part
of the evaluations, concerning location speciﬁc details of emission dependence, is also an important area for future
research, which could help clarify the precision of the emission intensity estimates of this chapter. Furthermore, future
opportunities in Australia to changing the energy generation mix is an area for ‘future research’ which may impact on the
evidence presented herein.

30.1 Introduction

Transport policy and planning conversations commonly include extensive discussion on mobility and/or accessibility
priorities for cities, yet towns and regions often receive less attention. This is so, despite about one in ﬁve Australians living
in low density cities or towns of under 85,000 population, or in rural or remote settlements, with much higher proportions
found in Europe. For example, Meijers et al. (2016) note that over half of the EU 15 urban population lives in small and
medium-sized towns and cities of 5000-100,000 population.

  Mobility, as it relates to people in smaller towns and their hinterlands, is the focus of this chapter. It explores how
mobility can foster social inclusion in a low-density regional setting. The scope of the chapter excludes remote areas, which
have their own particular challenges that deserve separate attention. The analysis is particularly relevant for planning and
policy making for bus services, since bus is the main form of public transport in such settings.

  Australia’s population growth rate has been high over the past decade (w1.65% per annum). Table 30.1 shows that
growth rates have tended to decrease with increased regional remoteness, with Major Cities becoming increasingly
dominant. Inner Regional areas often beneﬁt in population growth terms from their proximity to Major Cities.

  Population ageing will be a major demographic challenge for Australian regions in coming years, with numbers aged
65 years or older expected to double nationally (RAI, 2015a). This will be a particular challenge for what the Regional
Australian Institute (RAI, 2015b) calls Heartland Regions and Connected Lifestyle Regions, which have relatively high
proportions of seniors, particularly those aged 65e74. The Institute notes, however, that ‘there is a strong pattern of
migration of people in their 80s and 70s from regional to capital cities’ (RAI, 2015a, p. 91). At the other end of the age
scale, relatively high young dependency rates (children under 15 years) also tend to characterise Australia’s regions.
Outmigration of young adults is another notable regional demographic trend, being adverse for regional development
potential (and also tending to increase the share of the regional population that is young or aged over 50).

  Discussion of the population groups most likely to be at risk of social exclusion due to relatively poor mobility op-
portunities, in both urban and regional settings, typically lists older people, youth, people with a disability, people with
language difﬁculties (e.g. recent arrivals), those on low incomes and those with little or no car access, with women and
single parents also sometimes included (Clifton and Lucas, 2004; Currie and Delbosc, 2011). The higher proportions of
older people and the young in Australian regions suggests, ceteris paribus, relatively greater transport disadvantage will be
present than in metropolitan areas, given the commonly poor levels of public transport in regional Australia.

  The National Institute of Economic and Industry Research (NIEIR, 2009) has examined access to services in Australia,
estimating representative distances travelled by residents of metropolitan, other urban, and rural/townships in Australia, in
order to access a core range of essential services. These are deﬁned as: education (from child care and pre-school through
the various levels of schooling to TAFE and Universities); health (the range of services from general practitioners through
local hospitals to major hospitals, medical specialists and allied health services such as dentistry and optometry); welfare
and related services (including Centrelink (welfare payments), aged and other residential care, and police services). NIEIR

30.2 Some concepts and deﬁnitions

The broad literature base on social issues and transport is characterised by differing deﬁnitions of many of the concepts.
Our shorthand deﬁnitions of key concepts used in the chapter are as follows.

  Mobility ¼ the capacity to move around by any means, including walking, cycling, private vehicles, public transport
and other mobility devices. Mobility is a pre-requisite for being able to undertake activities anywhere other than where a
person is currently located.

  Accessibility ¼ the ability to get to activities or opportunities, such as work, education, playing sport, visiting friends,
etc. While mobility affects accessibility, the latter is also affected by land use.

  Social capital ¼ ‘Social capital consists of networks of social relations which are characterised by norms of trust and
reciprocity’ (Stone, 2001, p.4). In terms of the scope of the present chapter, Stone et al. (2003) distinguish:

  Bonding social capital, which describes closed networks, such as the family and perhaps work. Bonding generates
  closer, denser ties. In this research, it is based on the frequency that the person keeps in touch with members of their
  close or extended family, friends/intimates and neighbours.
  Bridging social capital, which spreads resources between networks, allowing people to access multiple networks and
  therefore resources and opportunities. This research considers it subject to the frequency that a person keeps in touch
  with work colleagues and people associated with groups in their community.

  Social exclusion ¼ Refers to individuals or groups of individuals at risk of not being able to participate in mainstream
society. In the current chapter, risk of social exclusion is deﬁned using ﬁve dimension thresholds that describe the situation
of a person: (a) household income lower than $A500 gross per week (2008 values); (b) employment status - not employed,
retired, in education or training, looking after family, nor undertaking voluntary work; (c) political activity e did not
contribute to or participate in a government political party, campaign or action group to improve social/environmental
conditions, or in a local community committee/group in the past 12 months; (d) social support e not able to get help if
needed from close or extended family, friends or neighbours; and (e) participation e did not attend a library, sport, hobby
or arts event in the past month. Therefore, risk of social exclusion is measured using a scale from 0 to 5, each point
representing a different threshold that has been met and a higher aggregate score representing a greater risk of social
exclusion.

  Transport disadvantage ¼ different researchers have different conceptions of transport disadvantage. Our deﬁnition is
simple: transport disadvantage occurs where people experience a shortage of transport options, which restricts their
mobility and hence their access to goods, services and relationships.

  Wellbeing ¼ This term commonly refers to notions of happiness, life satisfaction, fulﬁlment and human ﬂourishing
(Ryan and Deci, 2001).

30.3 Key literature on regional mobility/accessibility and social exclusion

Most of the literature on connections between mobility, accessibility and social exclusion is urban-based in industrialised
countries, and where there is research this tends to concentrate on older people. The following summary includes some of
the broader evidence base which is relevant to regions, adding some region-speciﬁc information.

  In an important early contribution to the current generation of work in this ﬁeld, Church et al. (2001) identiﬁed three
processes inﬂuencing the relationship between social exclusion and transport, one of which has a strongly regional ﬂavour:
the nature of the time-space organisation of facilities and opportunities. Associated with this, geographical exclusion was
one of seven sources of potential exclusion suggested by Church et al. with rural areas, smaller towns and cities and the
urban fringe being particular settings where this is most likely to be a source of mobility related exclusion.

  In policy terms, the focus on connections between transport and social exclusion, and responses thereto, probably began
in earnest with the work of the UK Social Exclusion Unit (SEU, 2003). Links were drawn between the exclusion of people
who do not have access to a car, and their needs for education, employment, health and other services and to food shops, as
well as to sporting, leisure and cultural activities. Findings were organised into ﬁve groups of barriers that need to be
addressed in order to improve transport-based accessibility to key services, considered by the SEU authors to be central to
social inclusion. These are:

1. The availability and physical accessibility of transport
2. The cost of transport
3. Services are located in inaccessible places
4. Safety and security e fear of crime
5. Travel horizons e people on low incomes were found to be less willing to travel to access work than those on higher

  incomes.

  To a large degree, the work on transport and social exclusion at that time tended to be a narrowly perceived conversation
about mobility and accessibility to speciﬁc activities, rather than about expanding personal choice and the ability to be
mobile, for whatever reason. There was little attempt to go further and examine impacts on the wellbeing of those who beneﬁt
from transport improvements, nor on the subsequent beneﬁts to society. The European Mobilate project changed this by
examining connections between transport, the built environment and a number of personal characteristics and self-assessed
beliefs on the quality of life (wellbeing) of older people (Mollenkopf et al., 2005). Their research found rural older people in
the ﬁve European countries examined were particularly disadvantaged in relation to mobility, requiring ‘immediate inter-
vention’, as poor mobility adversely impacted on their wellbeing (Mollenkopf et al., 2005, p.293).

  Preston and Rajé (2006) critique the development of the accessibility planning approach to reducing mobility-related
social exclusion, as promoted by the Social Exclusion Unit (2003) and the UK Department of Transport, arguing that it is
too aggregate. They suggest that area accessibility, area mobility and individual mobility should all form part of the process
for identifying mobility-related social exclusion and formulating policy responses.

  A large Australian Research Council supported project study drew on elements of both the SEU approach and the
Mobilate methodology, exploring associations between mobility, social exclusion and well-being and including factors such
as social capital, sense of community, psychological well-being and personality in the variables of interest (see for example,
Currie, 2011; Stanley et al., 2011a; Vella-Brodrick and Stanley, 2013). Part of that research, undertaken by the authors of the
current chapter, found signiﬁcant associations between trip making, social capital, household income and a person’s risk of
social exclusion, with that risk in turn being signiﬁcantly associated with wellbeing (Stanley et al., 2010, 2011a; 2011b,
2012). Sense of community was also a signiﬁcant contributor to wellbeing. Bridging social capital was found to be more
important than bonding social capital (Stanley et al., 2010). This analysis primarily used Melbourne urban data.

  One of the aims of that Melbourne research was estimation of a monetary value for reducing risk of mobility-related
social exclusion. The aim was, in part to enable policy initiatives that reduce such risks to be able to compete for public
funding on a more equal footing against transport projects with more readily quantiﬁable beneﬁts (e.g. road projects whose
main purpose is, for example, to reduce travel time). The lack of a monetary value for reducing risk of mobility-related
social exclusion was seen as a bias in evaluation against modes for which a major purpose is to cater for such needs.
In outer suburban and rural/regional Australia, this is bus.

  The research found that making an additional trip possible for a person at risk of mobility-related social exclusion was
worth about $A20 (2008 prices) and this value increased as household income reduced. Using this value and adjusting for
household income levels of bus users, Stanley and Hensher (2011) found that a boarding rate of about nine passengers an
hour was sufﬁcient for an outer urban Melbourne route bus service to break-even, in terms of social inclusion beneﬁts
alone, given the high proportion of users of such services who are likely to be at risk of mobility-related social exclusion.
406 PART | IX Social Exclusion

  A number of studies have identiﬁed groups likely to be at risk of social exclusion for mobility or accessibility related
reasons. Hine and colleagues, for example, undertook research on transport and social exclusion in regional areas in the UK
from the 2000s, ﬁnding considerable accessibility difﬁculties for groups at risk of social exclusion, compounded by an
absence of, or poor quality, public transport (Mackey and Hine, 2004; Kamruzzaman and Hine, 2011). The activity choices
of those with a low income were signiﬁcantly lower, when compared to their high-income counterparts, due to ﬁnancial
constraint and poor connections both between public transport services and between transport routes and opportunities.
Non-car owning individuals were more reliant on walking, and low-income individuals made trips of a shorter distance.
Hine (2007) cites studies that point to employment barriers facing rural young people in the UK without their own
transport, a situation not helped by the limited public transport offerings in rural/regional areas since bus deregulation in
England (outside London), Scotland and Wales under the 1985 Transport Act.

  Stanley and Stanley (2004, 2007) examined the transport needs of people at risk of social exclusion in Warrnambool
and rural surrounds in regional Victoria. Accessibility of regional services was seen to be largely deteriorating, with
services moving away from small towns, transport costs being shifted to the consumer. Considerable hidden transport
disadvantage was identiﬁed among those groups usually listed as subject to such risks, such as youth, older people, people
with a disability, those on low incomes and the Indigenous community.

  The authors suggest that young people coming from families with low incomes and living on farms were perhaps the
most transport disadvantaged population cohort in the region.

  An overview of rural transport in the UK found that 37% of older people living in rural areas in the Republic of Ireland
have a need for transport that is not being met by public or private means, while in Northern Ireland, 71% of people regard
lack of access to public transport as a key disadvantage for older people living in a rural community (Centre for Ageing
Research and Development in Ireland, 2010).

  Currie (2007) draws attention to the reliance on car travel in rural and regional areas in Australia and the associated
dependence of young people on others for many mobility needs. He also notes the reduced trip making of young rural
people, compared to those in larger regional towns, and expresses particular concerns about their access to education and
employment opportunities. Researchers also note the important role of the car in meeting mobility needs of older Aus-
tralians, particularly in rural and regional areas (Hensher, 2007; Browning and Sims, 2007), with Betts (2007) associating
this with the need to improve inter-regional public transport service levels.

  Examination of community transport1 services over a large area of rural Western Victoria found these services carry
older people and those with a disability, while the mobility needs of younger people and those struggling on a low income,
such as sole parents, are signiﬁcantly less well catered for (Stanley and Banks, 2012). Similarly, the critical role of leisure
activity, and the associated social contact, was often overlooked. It was noted that it is through these contacts that not only
individual social inclusion and wellbeing is created, but also community strength and social capital are built, leading to a
better resourced and a stronger community.

  A recent transport needs analysis in three rural/regional areas of South Australia, undertaken by two of the current
authors, reﬂected similar ﬁndings to previous Australian regional studies. Importantly, however, it widened the groups of
people likely to be at risk of mobility-related social exclusion to include pre-school children (Stanley and Stanley, 2018). In
the SA regions studied, some areas had a high proportion of children with developmental delays, with these proportions
increasing in recent years. Local consultations indicated that a lack of mobility opportunities for pre-school participation
was likely to be a signiﬁcant contributor.

  Mobility for older people in Japan living in more rural areas and new towns was recently explored (Chikaraishi et al.,
2018), ﬁnding that lack of access to a car reduced the range of accessible options and number of trips taken. People were
more dependent on lift-seeking, and those without such contacts experienced greater isolation. Other researchers found
some reluctance to accept lifts in both older people in rural Japan and rural-based international university students in
Australia (Yamamoto and Zhang, 2018; Stanley and Stanley, 2004).

  Thus, while the regional evidence base on links between mobility and risks of social exclusion is relatively sparse, the
literature suggests mobility can be a problem to many people who live in rural and regional areas, especially for those
without a car. While this is more commonly recognised with older people, there are many others, including pre-school
children and youth, who may be at risk of social exclusion due to mobility difﬁculties in a regional setting. Improving
accessibility is seen as an important way of reducing exclusion risks but, in a rural or regional setting, loss of local/regional
services may mean that mobility enhancements will need to play a bigger relative role than in larger cities, in terms of
achieving better accessibility.

30.4 Data analysis

30.4.1 Method and data description

The authors were active contributors to an Australian Research Council supported project, Investigating Transport
Disadvantage, Social Exclusion and Wellbeing in Metropolitan, Regional and Rural Victoria. This project, which gathered
data from detailed survey interviews in metropolitan Melbourne and in the (regional) Latrobe Valley, about 180 km east of
Melbourne, was noted in Section 30.3 above and has been widely reported (Currie, 2011; Stanley et al., 2010, 2011a;
2011b, 2012; Stanley and Hensher, 2011; Vella-Brodrick and Stanley, 2013), so the method is not repeated here. The
analysis in this section is new analysis of the study’s regional data base.

  The project gathered information from a self-completed Victorian government travel questionnaire from April 2007 to
June 2008. A number of respondents to this travel survey aged 15 years and over were then given the opportunity to opt in
to an additional comprehensive home-interview, which gathered detailed information on factors such as demographics and
household composition, social exclusion risk factors, social capital and connectedness to community, subjective well-
being, psychological well-being, personality, transport usage and transport difﬁculties. However, the proportion of peo-
ple likely to be at relatively high risk of social exclusion in both of the metropolitan Melbourne and regional Victorian
survey samples was less than thought necessary to provide adequate insight into links between mobility and risk of social
exclusion. A supplementary survey therefore targeted people likely to be highly socially disadvantaged, interviewing them
at welfare agency ofﬁces.

  In total, 235 regional respondents participated in these surveys, 148 from the original surveys and a further 87 from the
special survey sample. Seven people did not report important travel information, such as the number of trips, so they were
excluded from the modelling reported in this chapter (3% of the total sample). This combined regional sample, as used in
the current chapter, has not been analysed to date, in terms of what it might show about links between mobility and other
inﬂuences, particularly trip making and bridging social capital, on risk of social exclusion.

  As explained in Stanley et al. (2012), a common way of measuring a person’s social capital (deﬁnition in Section 30.2)
is to ask questions about frequency of contact with a range of signiﬁcant others on a frequency of interaction basis. The
time periods used in such frequency questions are not equal in length, which means the social capital measures should be
treated as a series of categorical variables. An analysis of the distribution of responses was used to deﬁne three categorical
variables representing low, medium and high bridging and bonding social capital. The frequency of interaction basis was
reported using a rate from 1 (i.e., never speak) to 6 (i.e., in touch most days). Bonding social capital considers four groups
of people (members of extended and close family, friends and neighbours) so it is rated from 4 to 24. The ‘low’ level of
bonding social capital is deﬁned as ratings below 16, ‘medium’ considers ratings between 16 and 19, and with the ‘high’
level rating above 19. Bridging social capital includes two groups of people (work colleagues and people associated with
groups in the community) so it is rated from 2 to 12. The ‘low’ level of bridging social capital considers rating below 6,
‘medium’ ratings between 6 and 8, and ‘high’ ratings above 8.

  Table 30.2 summarises characteristics of respondents to the two regional surveys, who were not selected to be
representative of the wider community so much as to be representative of persons across a wide range of social exclusion
risk levels. The most striking differences between the two regional samples are that respondents to the special survey were
much younger, with a lower level of education and were much more likely to be unemployed and born in Australia.
Table 30.2 shows that some 23 of the 31 respondents aged 15e18 were from the regional special survey sample, which
might suggest that they will be more likely to be at risk of social exclusion and that the sample data on young people
should not, therefore, be used as a basis for extrapolating to the wider population of young people. However, the average
household income for respondents aged 15e18 was $54,500, which was higher than the mean for the whole sample, which
is likely to reduce relative risks of social exclusion for this group.

  Table 30.3 sets out some key descriptors of participants in the two samples in terms of numbers of social exclusion risk
factors (thresholds), with a maximum of ﬁve possible risk factors measured, and mean performance scores on some key
associated variables are also shown. The lower household incomes, lower bridging and bonding social capital scores but
higher average number of daily trips among special survey sample respondents are notable, as is the much higher average
risk of social exclusion among the special sample than among respondents to the original sample, as was intended from the
surveying approach that was adopted for that special sample.

30.4.2 Modelling and discussion

A generalised ordered logit model was estimated (Greene and Hensher, 2010), to explore associations between risk of
social exclusion and a number of key variables thought likely to impact this risk, such as number of trips, bridging and
bonding social capital levels, household income and car availability. As in our preceding research (Stanley et al., 2011a, b),
number of trips was used as the independent transport variable of interest because the authors see it as a proxy for the level
of a person’s engagement with wider society, which should lead to a signiﬁcant association with risk of social exclusion.
The model takes into account the ordered nature of the dependent variable, which is the risk of social exclusion index. This
type of model estimates the unknown marginal utilities for each attribute and Jþ2 unknown threshold parameters, where J
is the number of ratings. The generalised ordered logit model form allows for random thresholds and also for hetero-
scedasticity in the observed variance. In this research we analysed the existence of heteroscedasticity in the observed
variance, but we did not ﬁnd any signiﬁcant inﬂuence. The results are shown in Table 30.4,2 which identiﬁes only those
attributes that have a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the risk of social exclusion index with 90% conﬁdence level. It is noteworthy
that there were no observations with a social risk exclusion index of 5, so the maximum rating was 4.

  One of the most interesting ﬁndings is that daily household income and number of trips each had a non-linear effect on
the risk of social exclusion, and therefore were included as squared in the model. As expected, both of these variables had a
negative effect on the social exclusion index, suggesting that if an individual has a higher income or does more daily trips,
he/she will have a lower tendency to be socially excluded. Having a medium or high level of bridging social capital also
had a signiﬁcant effect on reducing the risk of social exclusion. This underlines the importance of striving to build bridging
social capital to reduce exclusion levels. The results also show that if the individual is between 15 and 18 years old, then
he/she has a lower tendency to be at risk of social exclusion. This result was somewhat unexpected, given the high
proportion of surveyed young people who were identiﬁed in the special survey, where exclusion risk factors were generally
expected to be higher. However, higher household incomes among households with respondents aged 15e18 years tend to
reduce estimated risks of social exclusion. Finally, the model suggests that respondents from the original regional sample
have a lower tendency to be socially excluded relative to respondents from the special regional sample, as reﬂected in the
signiﬁcance of the dummy variable for the original data source.

  The special survey sample provides some interesting contrasts in terms of links between mobility and risk of social
exclusion. Those in this sample had a much higher risk of social exclusion than respondents to the original regional survey
but also made more trips, on average, than respondents to the original survey. Even though the special survey group
averaged more trips a day, they came up with 74 activities that could not be done because of poor transport (w5 per 6
respondents), while the original regional survey group only elicited 30 replies to this question (w1 per ﬁve respondents).
The same types of activity appear as those not able to be done because of poor transport as were cited by the original
sample survey respondents, but with higher frequencies among special survey respondents: enjoyment, sporting/leisure and
visiting friends and relatives. Of some concern, given the large number of the special survey group who were unemployed,
13 respondents identiﬁed work as an activity they could not do because of poor transport, with another 8 reporting getting
to an interview for jobs.

  While the special sample undertook trips, they had relatively low social inclusion. Given that the sample has large
numbers of youth, younger adults and people with a low income, much of their travel is likely to be active travel or travel
by public transport. Table 30.8 conﬁrms this: those at higher risk of social exclusion make relatively fewer trips as car
drivers and more trips by active transport or PT.

  Such a ﬁnding should not be unexpected, for reasons such as lower household incomes affecting affordability and also,
as youth tend to test their growing independence from family. Youth and younger people are more likely to engage in
active transport, walking and cycling and using other mobility devices, such as skate boards, facilitating trip making. For
example, 7 out of 28 respondents aged 15e18 reported that all their trips on the reporting day were by foot, conﬁrming
evidence gained from some of our regional case studies reported in Section 3. Those with a low income who are risk of
social exclusion also build their bonding social capital from their peers, to maintain their wellbeing, although this is likely
to be short term wellbeing or happiness (De Vos, 2013). However, while bonding social capital is highly important in that
it builds social connectiveness and a form of participation, both important for mental health (Saeri et al., 2018), it is
bridging social capital that is more important for facilitating broader social inclusion. The availability of mobility op-
portunities for these groups to undertake particular activities associated with bridging social capital, outside social contact
with their peers, neighbours and family groups, is particularly important and may require some longer trips, such as for
work. Thus, in the absence of a car, public transport becomes very important as a means of linking people to opportunities
to become more embedded in society, reducing personal and social costs.

30.5 Discussion

The main implications that we draw from this new regional analysis are as follows:

1. If you are a regional resident at relatively low risk of social exclusion, you are likely to have relatively good bridging
  social capital, come from a household where income levels are relatively high and have relatively few mobility prob-
  lems, probably because high household incomes support car availability.3 Older people may be more vulnerable among
  these general descriptors and may need support achieving access to desired activities

3. The number of cars and income per household are highly correlated. The overall performance of the models showed that income provides a better
representation of the social exclusion index than number of cars. Therefore, the ﬁnal model only includes income.
  The roles of mobility and bridging social capital in reducing social exclusion in regional Australia Chapter | 30 413

2. Conversely, if you are a regional resident at relatively high risk of social exclusion, you are likely to have relatively
  poor bridging social capital, come from a household where income levels are relatively low, be younger and be
  more likely to experience activities you cannot undertake because of mobility problems. Trip making per se may be
  quite high with young people who are able to undertake shorter distance travel by active transport but being able to
  travel to undertake a broader range of activities, such as getting to work or a job interview, may be a problem, with
  adverse consequences for building important bridging social capital. Providing affordable travel opportunities that
  meet trip making demands, especially those that facilitate inclusion in the broader community, and which facilitate
  greater capacity for independence and social mobility (getting on in life, as distinct from getting by) is important,
  both personally and for the wider community.

  A strong case is building in this transport research. People living in regions with poor mobility opportunities are at
increased risk of social exclusion. Wilkinson and Marmot (2003) note that social exclusion increases the risks of divorce
and separation, disability, illness, addiction and social isolation, forming vicious circles that deepen the predicament people
face. They also point out that, as well as these direct effects, health can also be compromised indirectly by living in
neighbourhoods with concentrations of deprivation, high unemployment, poor quality housing, limited access to services
and a poor quality environment.

  The analysis in this chapter suggests that, as well as improving regional mobility opportunities, building regional
bridging social capital should form a key policy component of strategies to increase regional social inclusion. Supporting
this position, a meta-analysis has shown connections between social relationships (particularly those with a social inte-
gration component) has an effect on improving length of life that is comparable to quitting smoking and exceeds other risks
such as obesity and physical inactivity (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010). Reducing regional risks of social exclusion thus has
many positive and important ﬂow-on effects. These are beyond the scope of the present chapter but merit detailed
exploration. While it is recognised that social capital is a complex notion, the strong empirical evidence, shown here and
elsewhere, of the importance of social capital for social inclusion, facilitated by the ability to be mobile, to enable a person
to reach the range of chosen activities, belies the concerns about the veracity of these associations, as expressed by
Schwanen et al. (2015).

  The provision of core PT services, and associated ﬂexible mobility options, should form important elements of a
package of measures to reduce regional risks of mobility-related social exclusion. The trip values derived in this chapter
can be used to suggest public transport (bus) boarding rates required for a service to break-even in terms of the monetary
value of reducing risk of social exclusion, similar to the analysis undertaken by Stanley and Hensher (2011) for outer urban
route bus services, as summarised in Section 30.2 above. The $A12.81 value of an additional regional trip, calculated in
Table 30.7 in 2008 prices, can be updated to shed light on this matter, resulting in a ﬁgure of $A15.40 in 2016 prices
(updated by the increase in Victorian Average Weekly Ordinary Time Earnings from 2008-164). The unit trip values shown
in Fig. 30.1 indicate higher marginal trip values to persons from lower income households, with public transport users
generally having lower household incomes than the community at large. Assuming average household incomes for
regional route bus users some 20% below the combined sample average in Table 30.3 (which results in a ﬁgure that is still
higher than the special survey sample average household income) implies a unit trip value of $A18.50 (rounded), in 2016
prices. If a regional town route bus service costs about $A120 an hour to provide, the boarding rate needed to break-even in
terms of user social inclusion beneﬁts is thus about 6.5 passengers per service hour. Such services would recover only a
small proportion of their direct service cost, in ﬁnancial terms, but are of signiﬁcant social value, to both users at risk of
exclusion and the wider society in terms of savings in ﬂow-on costs, such as crime, unemployment, adverse health out-
comes, etc. Lower costs of service provision would warrant lower break-even boarding rates for socially viable services.
This regional break-even boarding rate is lower than the Melbourne number (w9) calculated by Stanley and Hensher
(2011), because the proportion of regional bus users at risk of mobility-related social exclusion is much higher than for
outer urban Melbourne bus users, where a wider number of mobility options are usually available).

  Examination of the regional special needs group showed that, while at-risk youth are mobile, their activities more
commonly involve interaction with peers, the bridging activities that connect them with societal opportunities (education,
work, a broader network of contacts) being less available. This ﬁnding was shown, in earlier urban analysis, to risk the
development of negative emotions, where socially excluded people may develop a belief of loss of control over the di-
rection of their lives (Stanley et al., 2010). There was also found to be a signiﬁcant association between a belief of lack of
personal control and poor bridging social capital, which supports the ﬁndings reported in this regional study about the need
to build bridging social capital.
