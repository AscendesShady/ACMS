Assessing sources of variation in public
transport elasticities: some warnings

21.1 Introduction

The worlds of research and practice rely extensively on published estimates of price and service elasticities to develop
predictions of switching behaviour towards or away from public transport. For at least 55 years we have seen an accu-
mulation of empirical estimates of direct and cross fare and service elasticities reported in the literature. There are some
classic reviews such as Goodwin (1992) and Oum et al. (1992),1 which have synthesised many of the better studies
undertaken prior to 1990.

  Kremers et al. (2002), Nijkamp and Pepping (1998) and Holmgren (2007) have undertaken meta-analyses on samples
of public transport elasticities to identify systematic sources of variation. They ﬁnd, in particular, that differences in
elasticities can, in part, be explained by the functional form of the model, whether the estimates reﬂect the short run or long
run, the nature of the data structure (e.g., cross section, time series etc.), location (such as country or city size) and whether
data is aggregated or disaggregated.

  A particularly interesting aspect of the studies is the evidence of differences obtained using revealed preference (RP)
data and that reported in stand alone stated preference (SP) or combined RP/SP data when individual choice or (aggre-
gated) share models are being used. One of the problems we have with the comparison is that the majority of SP studies do
not appear to calibrate their models to reproduce base modal shares2 and hence the comparison is likely to reﬂect as much
the failure to calibrate the model constants rather than any possible systematic under or over estimate of mean elasticity.
This is an important point, which we will return to in the empirical analysis, since it is easy for sceptics of SP or SP/RP
applications to argue that they under- or over-estimate compared to models estimated with RP data (although who says that
it is the correct reference anyway?).

  Unlike the meta analysis studies cited above, which studied each class of elasticity separately, we have pooled the data
for fare, in-vehicle time and headway direct elasticities, to give us a sample of 319 observations. In some of the previous
meta-studies (e.g., Kremers et al., 2002), price elasticities from different modes of transport are pooled, but it is the pooling
of price and service elasticities that is new in this study. The advantage of our approach is that we have a signiﬁcant sample
size to add conﬁdence to inference, in contrast to the earlier studies where sample sizes were often as low as 12 data points.
Holmgren’s sample sizes varied from 17 to 81. In addition, the focus is on establishing sources of systematic variations in a
broad class of direct elasticities, and so the assessment of candidate sources across three key attributes of public transport
seems appropriate. To control for possible biases attributable to a sub-class (e.g., fares), we introduce dummy variables for
each sub-class, normalising on one sub-class for identiﬁcation.

  The key purpose of this chapter is to suggest points of assistance for policy makers in the decision as to what extent,
existing knowledge on behavioural response as captured through direct elasticities, might be used in another context, and
what are some lessons we can learn from a meta analysis in guiding the deﬁnition of elasticity outputs obtained from new
primary data.

  The chapter is organised as follows. We describe the data set in the next section and the approach we had adopted to
establish potential sources of systematic variation. This is followed by the empirical evidence on three key direct elas-
ticities of public transport, namely fares, in-vehicle time, headway. The chapter concludes with comments on the evidence
and offers three very speciﬁc warning signals when selecting elasticities from secondary sources for use in particular
contexts, and when designing new studies that collect primary data.

21.2 The data source

The data was compiled from 40 available publications (see Appendix 21.B), a number of which were reviews of the
literature (i.e., Balcombe et al., 2004; Goodwin, 1992; Hanly et al., 2002; Lago et al., 1981; Litman, 2002, 2005; Luk and
Hepburn, 1993; Bly and Webster, 1981; Oum et al., 1992). Tracking the details on the nature of the data structure (e.g., SP,
RP, combined SP/RP; aggregate vs. disaggregate data), time period, years, elasticity formula used (e.g., point or arc), and
estimation method (e.g., single cross section, time series of cross sections, panel) is not easy when one has to rely on
secondary sources, and even when primary sources are available, there is often limited reporting to establish the precise
approach adopted.

  The studies that survived our culling are those where we have been able to identify some key crucial features of the
method used. Speciﬁcally, we set as our minimum requirements, complete information on the type of elasticity (e.g. direct
or cross), the applicable location, whether the data was SP, RP or SP/RP; the deﬁnition of the fare variable (e.g., an average
or a speciﬁc ticket type); time of day (notably peak, off-peak or all day); geographical location (i.e., country and city), and
the speciﬁc mode or mode mix (i.e. bus, train, public transport). Evidence on time period (short vs. long run), the span of
years of the data, and trip purpose were, surprisingly, poorly documented. In this chapter we have focused on three direct
elasticities for public transport.

  Having satisﬁed the criteria above, we then compiled the data set and undertook a check of the range of estimated
elasticities within each segment of interest. On close inspection, for the subset of data of interest herein from the fuller data
set, including other elasticities (e.g., cross elasticities, fuel price elasticities), we found two studies representing nine data
points that reported estimates substantially higher that those for the rest of the studies, namely as high as À1.825 for fares
and À1.920 for in-vehicle time (See Table 21.1). These data points are deemed to be outliers under the rule of exceeding
two standard deviations around the mean of the sample. Removal of this data reduced the sample size from 328 to 319. The
ﬁnal data points that have been pooled across fares, in-vehicle time and headways for public transport direct elasticities are
given in Fig. 21.1, and the ﬁrst and second moments and range are summarised in Table 21.1. The mean estimate
of À0.395 for fares is close to À0.38 reported in Holmgren (2007) and other reviews such as Goodwin (1992), Oum et al.
(1992) and Litman (2002). Graphical representation of the data is given in Appendix 21.A for a range of direct elasticities.

  A further breakdown of the elasticity evidence by a number of dimensions is given in Table 21.2 (based on the data
proﬁle in Appendix 21.A). A number of cells are empty or are not applicable (e.g., single fare effects for in-vehicle time
and headway). Overall, we ﬁnd that, on average, commuters are less sensitive to fares and in-vehicle time, but more
sensitive to headways, than non-commuters. When we drill further down, we ﬁnd that evidence from single cross sections
(in contrast to time series and before-and-after studies) is lower (contrasted to the overall means); responsiveness to fares in
the peak is similar or less than the overall fare elasticity, similar to the overall estimate for in-vehicle time, and considerably
lower for headway. Single fare commuters are less fare sensitive that all commuters, which is not unexpected given that
such individuals tend to be less frequent users, and would normally purchase a weekly or multiride ticket in a regular
commute. The evidence for SP vs. RP is discussed below, but it suggests that RP and combined SP/RP estimates are higher
than SP stand alone where data can be compared, namely in-vehicle time elasticity for commuters.

21.3 The evidence

We estimated a large number of ordinary regression models, controlling for heteroskedasticity, in searching for statistically
signiﬁcant sources of explanation of systematic variation in mean estimates of direct elasticities for fare, in-vehicle time
and headway. The ﬁnal model is summarised in Table 21.3. Fourteen variables explained 32% of the variation in elasticity
estimates across the sample. This suggests that there are a myriad of other systematic and non-systematic inﬂuences of
mean estimates. Other variable assessed included locations (UK, USA), all day fares, SP separated from SP/RP, commuting
vs. non-commuting, shopping trips, time series, before and after study, pensioner, short run vs. long run, and concession
vs. non-concession. In meta analysis studies, it is common that representation of elasticities in each class may contributes
to the lack of signiﬁcance as much a genuine behavioural non-signiﬁcance.

  The selection of candidate inﬂuences is based on hypotheses that have either been tested or have arisen out of previous
empirical studies. As an example, we hypothesise that the class of ticket (which carries a particular price structure) does
have an inﬂuence of the behavioural response to public transport use. We expect, for example, all other factors held
constant, (i) that those who choose multiride tickets are less sensitive to fare increases, because the price deal is more
attractive than other classes; (ii) that a bus and a train are seen as offering different types of services and controlling for this
is important in capturing responses to changes in fare and service levels; and (iii) the data speciﬁcation paradigms (i.e.,
stand alone SP, combined SP/RP and stand alone RP data) inﬂuence the evidence since there are mixtures of real and
hypothetical circumstances being studied, and there is evidence of hypothetical bias associated with choice experiment (see
Hensher, 2008).

We investigated the prospects of multicollinearity, which can often be a concern in meta analysis using mean estimates

from a sample of studies. A popular way to analyse multicollinearity is in terms of the effect of the intercorrelation of the

regressors on the variance of the least squares parameter estimates. The variance inﬂation factor (VIF) is a measure of this

effect.  VIFi  ¼  1/(1ÀR2k )  where  R2  is  the  R2  obtained  when  the  kth  regressor  is  regressed  on  the  remaining  variables.  The

  k
optimal value for this statistic is 1.0, which occurs when the R2 is zero or this variable is orthogonal to the other variables.

There is no consensus on what values of the variance inﬂation factor merit attention, or on what one should do with the

results. Some authors (e.g., Chatterjee and Price, 1991) suggest that values in excess of 10 are problematic. In the current

study we are well below this on all regressors (see last column of Table 21.3) and hence can safely reject the presence of

multicollinearity.

The evidence suggests a number of key directional impacts, of which two are particularly important from a

methodology point of view. There is evidence that models that use stand alone RP tend to produce lower mean estimates

than those that have combined RP and SP data. There are very few stand alone SP studies (12 associated with invehicle
time and commuting), and a tested SP stand alone dummy variable was not signiﬁcant.3 There are some essential caveats

to this e ﬁrstly many RP studies herein are earlier studies (i.e., 1970s in particular) than the majority of the SP/RP

studies, and in general we see an increase in mean estimates over time. Drawing on a separate analysis of 152 obser-

vations (not reported herein), that had information to be able to identify the year of the study, all other factors remaining

constant, the data suggest that as we move back from 2004, each year reduces the mean estimate by 0.00646 (given an

overall average for the 152 data points in which the year is reported of À0.3905). In addition, it seems that the majority
of studies, RP, SP and mixed SP/RP, are not calibrated4 to reproduce base population model shares, but that the SP

  The other important ﬁnding is the tendency for fare elasticities to be very sensitive to the class of ticket type. Relative to
a single and a weekly ticket, the most popular ticket types, we ﬁnd a systematically higher mean estimate for multiride, 1 h,
4 h and all day ticket types. We were not been able to establish any signiﬁcant variation between the use of unweighted
average fares and ticket types, given that the contrast between an average fare and fare classes was not statistically
signiﬁcant.

  Some intuitively plausible variations were established for peak, all day and off-peak estimates. Peak elasticities are
lower on average than off-peak and all-day estimates, and all day estimates are lower than off-peak (due to inclusion of
peak and off-peak). The peak estimates have a mean estimate that is on average 0.1958 lower than the overall mean
of À0.408 (i.e., À0.2122). Bus speciﬁc elasticities are slightly lower than train speciﬁc elasticities, but both are higher
relative to combined public transport, respectively by À0.0721 and À0.1101. This is an important ﬁnding, suggesting a
downward bias in bus and train speciﬁc responsiveness when using an estimate based on ignoring the difference between a
bus and a train.

  After identifying a number of systematic sources of elasticity variation, we have included some elasticity-type dummy
variables for fares, in-vehicle time and headway (the latter set to zero) to accommodate other sources of variation that are
speciﬁc to fares, in-vehicle time and headways. As shown in Table 21.1, fare and in-vehicle time elasticities are on average
greater than the headway elasticities, which is re-afﬁrmed by the negative parameter estimates for the two constants in the
model. Finally, estimates from USA and Australian cities are signiﬁcantly lower, suggesting that the wealthier cities are
less sensitive.

  We also ran separate regression models for each of three types of direct elasticities, summarised in Table 21.4.
Comparisons between the models are not straightforward, given that many of the fare-related variables are not applicable in
the in-vehicle time and headway models. An important ﬁnding from the separate models is that the distinction between RP
or SP/RP data has a much higher mean effect for each of the three elasticity types. Although one might expect some of
these to be lower and some higher relative to the overall estimate in Table 21.1, the models are not strictly comparable
because some variable are removed from some models. The overall message is clear however; importantly RP estimates are
lower than combined SP/RP estimates (and most notably for in-vehicle time), ticket class effects are similar and ranked the
same for fare elasticities, location is only signiﬁcant for fares, and time of day elasticities are not signiﬁcant for in-vehicle
time and headway. The sample sizes are sufﬁciently small given the descriptive proﬁles in Table 21.2 of speciﬁc segments
that behavioural inferences drawn from the in-vehicle and headway models must be cautioned.

21.4 Conclusions

The analysis of 319 mean estimates of three classes of direct elasticities for public transport have identiﬁed some sta-
tistically signiﬁcant inﬂuences that explain 32% of the systematic variation in mean elasticity estimates. The important
questions to ask about the evidence are “what guidance does it provide when an analyst is using elasticities from secondary
sources, instead of collecting new evidence from primary local sources?” and “what lessons can be used in the design and
application of studies privileged to collect new primary data?”.

  When one is evaluating the inﬂuence of pricing and service level policies on public transport patronage, in contexts
where typically public transport has a relatively small share of the market, especially in countries where the data herein is
predominantly sourced (i.e., USA, Australia, New Zealand, UK and a few car dominated European countries), the selection
of a mean elasticity estimate can be the difference between a sizeable or an insigniﬁcant predicted modal switch away from
or towards public transport.

  Our preference would always be to collect primary data as a basis for conclusions regarding effects of policy changes.
The evidence herein offers at least three warning signals in the development of new data sources and in the selection of
elasticities from secondary sources. First, drawing on public transport elasticities to use in a speciﬁc model context such as
bus or train, will tend to over-estimate behavioural response. Second, if one believes that elasticities based on stand-alone
RP data are ‘better’ estimates than those based on combined SP/RP data, then RP estimates will be lower than the estimates
reported from combined SP/RP data. We caution that this is possibly not a criticism of the underlying behavioural content
of SP data per se, but due in large measure to the absence of calibration designed to obtain the correct base market shares
(compared to shares from stated responses), given the role that the dependent variable (e.g., choice probability) plays in the
formula. The deviations from actual population shares are typically more pronounced in SP/RP data6 than RP data;
although RP data is not immune from this. Third, accounting for the type of ticket purchased has a clear inﬂuence on fare
sensitivity. Although we cannot claim that using an average fare estimate in studies will tend to over- or under-estimate fare
elasticities, the evidence supporting strong differences in behavioural response between ticket types is sufﬁciently
revealing to warn against ignoring the class of ticket. Relative to a single and a weekly ticket, the most popular ticket types,
we ﬁnd a systematically higher mean estimate for multiride, 1 h, 4 h and all day ticket types; and with a growing interest in
marketing such tickets, there is risk that using averages based typically on single fares, will under-estimate switching
response.

22.1 Introduction

In the broader literature, crowding is deﬁned as a “psychological state characterised by stress and having motivational
properties” (Bell et al., 2001, p. 320), which may have a number of behavioural implications such as a perceived lack of
control, stimulus overload, a lack of behavioural freedom or privacy and the presence of other stressors. Since the
mid-1970s when Hensher and McLeod (1977) published the ﬁrst study, as far as we are aware, that analysed crowding in
public transport (PT), transport academics, consultancies and PT policy makers have shown a growing, albeit slow, interest
in the study of crowding, and how it might be incorporated into the observed sources of utility associated with modal
choice. A reduction in crowding is regarded as an important component of PT service quality improvement and has
become a dominating consideration in the design of stations for proposed new rail systems (Hensher et al., 2011b). Most
recently, a number of studies, discussed below, have empirically estimated PT passengers’ willingness to pay (WTP) for
reduced crowding. These crowding studies derived estimates of the value of crowding (VoC) and/or investigated its impact
on the value of travel time savings (VTTS). A common ﬁnding is that the perceived value of travel, walking or waiting
time increases as the level of crowding increases.

  Table 22.1 summarises the reviewed PT crowding valuation studies, where their key characteristics are provided such
as the modelling framework, PT mode, location of study, the way of representing crowding, measure of VoC, and trip
purpose. The majority of studies used logit models (mainly multinomial logit e MNL) with SP data, with the exception of
Pepper et al. (2003), and all studies covered commuters. The UK and USA studies (see Dowling et al., 2008) investigated
rail crowding only. Crowding in a multi-mode context is addressed in one study conducted in Australia (Hensher et al.,
2011b) and one conducted in Israel (Polydoropoulou and Ben-Akiva, 2001). Only Douglas and Karpouzis (2005)
investigated crowding on platforms, and in access-ways and entrances, and its impact on valuation of waiting time and
walking time; while other studies focus on in-vehicle crowding.

  Wardman and Whelan (2011) undertook the most extensive meta analysis on crowding to date, using 208 UK
passenger rail crowding valuations from 14 unpublished studies and one published study, where time multipliers are used
as the measure of VoC. Travel in a crowded condition is shown to incur an additional cost relative to the same amount of
trip time in an uncrowded environment and can be valued in terms of equivalent time (e.g., minutes per minute).1 The time
multiplier can be deﬁned as the ratio of generalised time (travel time plus crowding) and the equivalent travel time without
crowding. The 208 empirical observations comprise 124 standing multipliers (ranging from 0.94 to 6.05) and 84 seated
multipliers (ranging from 1.0 to 1.78). Only one of the sampled studies used revealed preference (RP) data, the rest are
obtained from models estimated using stated preference (SP) data. The meta regression shows that systematic variations in
time multipliers include seated or standing, load factor and trip purpose.

  The purpose of this chapter is to provide a review of public transport crowding studies that have derived estimates of
the value of crowding, which offer an international perspective in contrast to the UK emphasis2 of Wardman and Whelan
(2011). We review published studies from the UK, USA, Australia and Israel. In addition to passenger rail, we also include
other transport modes such as bus, light rail and metro. Wardman and Whelan (2011) focussed only on in-vehicle
crowding, whereas we extend the review to include crowding at different locations, i.e., in public transport, access-
ways, entrances and platforms. In addition to the time multiplier, other measures of value of crowding are presented,
namely a monetary value per minute or hour, and a monetary value per trip. Given that the majority of reviewed primary

22.2 Valuation of in-vehicle crowding

Valuing in-vehicle crowding is a key research focus of crowding studies. If the number of passengers increases per unit of
time, there is a threshold beyond which not everyone is able to ﬁnd a seat and some people need to stand inside a vehicle,
which has a negative impact on the perception of the travel experience. The presence of passengers standing in aisles inside
trains or other modes makes it more difﬁcult for passengers that need to board or alight from a vehicle. Therefore, not only
is the perception or valuation of travel time affected, but the travel time itself increases due to friction effects between
passengers. As crowding has become a high agenda policy and planning matter in recent times, various ways of assessing
its role in modal usage and modal choice have been proposed and incorporated in stated choice experiments. A key output
of the estimated models is the value of crowding (VoC) and hence the beneﬁt of improving crowding, given the speciﬁc
deﬁnition adopted. In the published literature, in-vehicle VoC has been measured in three alternative ways, as the time
multiplier, the monetary value per time unit (minute or hour), and the monetary value per trip. These measures of VoC are
interchangeable if the value of uncrowded time and the trip length are known.

22.2.1 Time multiplier

Whelan and Crockett (2009) investigated the willingness to pay (WTP) for reduced rail crowding in the UK, using a stated
preference (SP) experiment (see Fig. 22.1 for an example choice question), where the crowding attribute is deﬁned as the
seat occupancy rate (ranging from 25% to 100%) and the number of standing passengers (ranging from 0 to 70). The
survey was conducted in 2008, with 2314 train passengers sampled, where the majority of them are commuters (83%).
Whelan and Crockett speciﬁed time multipliers (seated and standing) in terms of both load factor (the number of
passengers/the number of seats) and the number of standing passengers per square metre (standing passengers/m2). Their
results show that the time multiplier increases from 1.0 to 1.83 for seated passengers, and from 1.5 to 2.37 when the load
factor changes from 80% to 200%; while the time multiplier increases from 1.0 to 1.63 for seated passengers, and from

22.2.2 Monetary value ($) per minute or hour

In addition to the time multiplier, there are other ways to value crowding. In the context of train users, Lu et al. (2008)
conducted a stated preference experiment in Greater Manchester in 2005, which has ﬁve attributes including in-vehicle
time, fare, frequency, punctuality and crowding (see Table 22.2 for an illustrative example of their choice experiment),
where crowding and punctuality were shown as the combination of probability of occurrence and length of time (for
example, two out of ﬁve times someone stands for whole journey) (i.e., 30 min for Option B in the choice example shown
in Table 22.2).3 Within a multinomial logit (MNL) framework, the value of crowding was estimated as 12.05 pence per
person minute or 7.23 pounds per person hour in 2005 (2005£), which is more than twice as much as the value of
in-vehicle time (i.e., 5.68 pence per minute).

  Douglas and Karpouzis (2006) empirically estimated values of on-train crowding, using an SP experiment, sampling
584 Sydney train passengers in 2005. All surveys were undertaken on platforms at 17 stations as passengers were waiting
for their trains during the AM and PM peaks. The respondents were asked to choose between two rail trips differing in wait
time, onboard train time and the level of crowding (the ﬁve levels of on-train crowding were uncrowded seating, crowded
seating, standing for 10 min, standing for 20 min and crush standing for 10 min). An example of Douglas and Karpouzis’s
choice experiment is given in Fig. 22.2.

  On-train crowding adds additional travel cost. Douglas and Karpouzis (2006) estimated that crowded seating would
lead to a 17% increase in travel time. Given a valuation of crowding in terms of ‘per minute’, the time multiplier is 1.17,
which is within the seated multiplier range in Wardman and Whelan (2011) of 1.0e1.78. The time multiplier relative to
uncrowded seating is 1.34 for standing up to 10 min, 1.81 for standing over 20 min or longer, and 2.04 for crush standing
up to 10 min. Given a value of travel time savings in an uncrowded train carriage (i.e., all crowded seating and standing
was removed) of $8.45 (in 2003 Australian dollars) (2003$AUD) reported by Douglas Economics (2004) using an SP
experiment, Douglas and Karpouzis (2006) reported a value of crowding seating of 2003$AUD1.47 per person hour.
Hence, the generalised cost (i.e., time cost plus crowding cost) when seating in a crowded train is 2003$AUD9.92
(¼8.45 þ 1.47) per person hour. Other values of crowding levels are provided in Table 22.3. Douglas and Karpouzis
(2006) also investigated the impact of load factor on the valuation of crowding in a train, and found that there is a
non-linear relationship between the value of generalised time (travel time plus crowding) and load factor (LF); for example
the value of time increases from 2003$AUD8.45 per person hour when LF  80% (no crowding cost) to 2003$AUD 8.84
at 100% loading, and reaches 2003$AUD 12.05 per hour at 200% train loads.

Ben-Akiva (2001), in an SP experiment, investigated crowding deﬁned as the probability of getting a seat (out of ten
times) for bus and mass transit.4 The variable was treated as a linear effect. They found it to be a statistically signiﬁcant
effect, but did not report a WTP. They provide two cost variables (fare for those with no cars, and fare for those who own
one or more cars). The WTP calculated from the data (in Israeli shekel (NIS) 1999) is 36.39 NIS and 46.61 NIS
respectively for 0 and 1-plus cars owned. This suggests that if a traveller can always get a seat (10 out of 10 times), then
they are willing to pay up to 36.39 NIS to 46.61 NIS for this situation, which translates into (given the linear deﬁnition),
3.64 NIS to 4.66 NIS per trip. If there is a 50% chance of getting a seat, then the values per trip are 1.82 NIS and 2.33 NIS.

  Pepper et al. (2003) also investigated crowding in trains. The numbers and lengths of New Jersey Transit (NJT)
commuter trains accessing New York’s Penn Station are currently at the limits of available capacity during peak periods, as
evidenced by the signiﬁcant number of standees at these times. A study was undertaken to determine the design of the
multilevel coaches, so that they will provide the extra capacity needed and also reﬂect customers’ preferences. The study
focused on interior issues, including the seating conﬁguration and seat design, that relate directly to the amount of seated
(and standee) capacity on the new coaches.

  A computer-based survey, conducted in 1999, was administered to customers to quantify their preferences among key
elements of the multilevel concepts, and to estimate their WTP for those elements. With regard to the crowding attribute,
three levels of desirability for increased seating capacity were provided during the survey: same number of seats, 25%
more seats and 45% more seats. This study found that additional seating capacity, in the conﬁguration preferred by
customers, provides a substantial net beneﬁt to NJT passengers, equivalent to about US$2.20 fare value per trip in 1999.
The beneﬁts are higher for this application because of the crowded conditions on existing trains. The study also suggests
that multilevel coaches and improved interior design have beneﬁts well beyond increased capacity.

  This review of in-vehicle crowding WTP research reveals that SP is the dominant data paradigm for the empirical
valuation of crowding, and that there exist different ways of representing crowding in SP experiments (e.g., the number of
standing passengers combined with the percentage of seated passenger, probability of occurrence (seating or standing), and
the associated length of travel time, probability of getting a seat). There are three measures of crowding valuation (minute/
minute (or time multiplier), $/minute, and $/trip). As a primary study, only Hensher et al. (2011b) and Douglas and
Karpouzis (2006) have provided multiple measures of VoC. In addition to the written descriptions of attributes, Hensher
et al. (2011b), Whelan and Crockett (2009) and Douglas and Karpouzis (2006) also presented pictures in their choice
experiments to assist respondents understand crowding. All reviewed studies show that crowding conditions increases
generalised time costs. Litman (2010) concluded that in-vehicle travel time can be valued at about 25% of the average
wage rate for a seated passenger, 50% of the average wage rate for a standing passenger, and 100% of the average wage
rate in a crowded bus or train.

22.5 Conclusions and policy implications

Crowding can be a potential threat both to the health of the public transport industry (e.g., delays and low efﬁciency) and
passengers (e.g., safety and stress issues) (Cox et al., 2006). Hence to reduce crowding is important for improving the
attractiveness of public transport. Similar to the reduction in travel time and time variability, there is a WTP associated with
reduced crowding. This chapter has reviewed the few published public transport crowding valuation studies, in which stated
choice modelling has dominated the literature. Different ways of representing crowding in SP experiments are identiﬁed,
along with three measures of the value of crowding (i.e., time multiplier, dollar per time unit, and dollar per trip). The
predominant modal focus is on crowding in a train carriage, although there are some multi-modal exceptions. The impact of
crowding on travel time has been investigated in almost all studies, and the common ﬁnding is that travelling in crowded
conditions leads to increased values of travel time savings, with crowding viewed as an additional component of generalised
time, although careful separation of the crowding effect and travel time effect is important to avoid double counting.

  The evidence herein provides WTP estimates for project appraisal. Inclusion of a treatment of the WTP for reductions
in crowding in its various deﬁnitional states in the economic evaluation of public transport has become as relevant as the
valuation of travel time savings and trip time variability (or reliability). This growing area of interest in the crowding
literature is critical in identifying an extended set of user beneﬁts that can inﬂuence the design of platforms or stations for
new systems, and the extension of stations for existing systems. Increasing the capacity of platforms would result in an
opportunity for more carriages per train set and less crowding on platforms and within trains. Determining the WTP to
avoid crowding at stations and within public transport (including allowing for standing vs. sitting), is also an important
parameter in the calculation of the optimal number of seats (and hence standing space) that should be provided in a train (or
bus) under the assumptions of social welfare maximisation and/or some commercial criterion.

  The empirical ﬁndings summarised in this chapter, together with the review by Wardman and Whelan (2011), provides
a starting point for WTP evidence on crowding; however there is much more research that needs to be undertaken, as
suggested below. This review suggests a number of important avenues for future PT crowding valuation research. First, it
is quite likely that passengers may have different perceptions of crowding when sitting or standing in a mode with the same
crowding level. A few studies have investigated observed traveller heterogeneity using interaction terms in the utility
function; for example, Whelan and Crockett (2009) investigated the effects of trip purpose, income and distance on the
valuation of crowding; however unobserved preference heterogeneity at the individual level may also play an important
role in representing the WTP to reduce crowding. None of the reviewed studies addressed this issue. More advanced
discrete choice models such as mixed multinomial logit (MMNL) might be used to reveal unobserved between-individual

  All of the reviewed studies encompass the range of interpretations of crowding; however the number of studies
(published) focussing on a speciﬁc dimension of the WTP for crowding reduction (i.e., time multiplier, $/hour, and $/trip)
is very small, preventing the implementation of a formal meta regression analysis. The majority of the studies considered
in-vehicle crowding, and only Douglas and Karpouzis (2005) empirically investigated the impacts of different crowding
levels on walking time and waiting time valuation. There is a need for studies focused on valuing crowding on the
platform, and at the station in access-ways and entrances, given that the experience in these locations is also important in
inﬂuencing modal preferences, and consequently modal choice and project design. A successful public transport system
requires improvements (e.g., lower crowding and better reliability) across all important stages (access-way, entrance,
platform, station and in vehicle) of the passenger service chain.

  Another potential limitation of the existing crowding valuation studies is that they all adopt a linear utility speciﬁcation
following the theory of Random Utility Maximisation, which implicitly assumes that decision making occurs with certainty
or without risk (see Batley and Ibáñez, 2009; Li and Hensher, 2011). However for repeated trips, typically of commuting in
particular, the crowding level may vary from trip to trip and a distribution of crowding levels with corresponding
probabilities of occurrence exists in real markets, similar to the effect of travel time variability. Therefore, the true decision
making context is subject to ‘risk’ (probabilities of occurrence). The linear utility speciﬁcation implicitly assumes risk
neutrality (see Bates et al., 2001); however evidence from psychology, behavioural economics (see e.g., Tversky and
Kahneman, 1992; Holt and Laury, 2002), and recently travel behaviour research, shows that people may be risk averse or
risk taking (see e.g., Polak et al., 2008; Li et al., 2010b; Hensher et al., 2011a; Hensher and Li, 2012), suggesting non-
linear utility (or value) functional forms should be used to empirically address the real attitude towards risk. Studies
cited above by the authors on the valuation on travel time variability, show that empirically addressing risk attitudes in
choice models is behaviourally very appealing, and is associated with a statistically signiﬁcant improvement in model ﬁt,
and consequent impact on the valuation of travel time variability. However, all published crowding WTP studies are based
on the assumption of risk neutrality through the linear utility speciﬁcation.

  In previous choice experiments that have included a measure for crowding, only one level of crowding is used to
represent crowding for an alternative within a choice set. This ‘deterministic’ crowding is somewhat unrealistic given that
ﬂuctuations in the demand side and supply side would also inﬂuence crowding; and hence in reality there is a distribution
of crowding levels. In future stated choice studies a full distribution of crowding over a number of repeated trips should be
considered. For example, deﬁning three crowding scenarios, a commuter might encounter these three levels of crowding
(up to a probability of occurrence) when using a PT mode, deﬁned as a low level of crowding (Lcr), an average or common
(Acr) level, and a high level (Hcr). Each level should be associated with an alternative trip circumstance within a choice
task, along with corresponding probabilities (probLcr, probAcr and probHcr) of occurrence, to fully represent the crowding
attribute. As an example, under the attribute-speciﬁc Extended Expected Utility Theoretic model developed by Hensher
et al. (2011a), a utility function incorporating crowding might be given as Eq. (22.1), as a way to capture the effects of
