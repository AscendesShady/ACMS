 STEP 1

Understand How the TDM Policy Fits
into the Planning Fabric of the
Community

Eﬀective implementation of a TDM policy requires that it
  Build on the existing planning framework established in the
  comprehensive plan.
  Leverage community zoning and ordinance tools.
  Take advantage of design review.
  Capitalize on other sustainability eﬀorts.
Taking these actions ensures that the policy is not viewed solely as
another administrative hurdle but as a means to achieve a greater
community vision. is, in turn, will make political and public support
easier to garner. Furthermore, having a clear, desired outcome will help
determine the appropriate site-speci c goal and metric (explained in
steps 4 and 5).

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Identify how the TDM policy would ful ll goals and objectives of
  the comprehensive plan and other planning documents.
  Identify zoning and other ordinance opportunities that the TDM
  policy could leverage.
  Consider how the TDM policy can work synergistically with an
  existing municipal TDM program, if one has already been
  established.
  Consider whether other sustainability goals or plans can be
  advanced with a TDM policy.

 STEP 2

Identify Where the TDM Policy Should
Apply

Communities have exibility to choose where the TDM policy should
apply. Depending on the political context, inserting the TDM policy into
the jurisdiction planning ordinance or zoning code may make most
sense, so the policy applies universally across the jurisdiction. If growth
conditions occur on a smaller geographic scale within a jurisdiction,
incorporating the TDM policy as part of a more localized piece of
legislation, such as a community plan or an overlay district, makes more
sense. Multiple types of TDM requirements for diﬀerent parts of the
jurisdiction (e.g., providing a more stringent TDM model in downtown
and a more exible TDM model in the remainder of the jurisdiction) are
not uncommon.

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Identify where TDM makes most sense for new developments.
  Determine which legislative framework may support a TDM policy.
  Locate places where increased requirements may hurt more than
  they help.

 STEP 3

Determine the Types of Developments
at Should Comply with the TDM Policy

e TDM policy may not be relevant to all types of developments within
the selected geographic area identi ed in step 2. Furthermore, certain
obligations under the TDM policy may have disproportionate burdens on
certain types of development or may not make sense from an
administrative perspective. In determining an appropriate set of triggers,
communities should consider what may be required of new
developments (steps 5 and 7) and how that could aﬀect the types of
developments that are anticipated in the community.

CONSIDERATIONS:
  Identify land use and development types that place a larger burden
  on the community’s transportation network.
  Determine what future land uses and development types may be
  disproportionately aﬀected by additional requirements.

 STEP 4

Select an Appropriate Metric to Quantify
Site-Based Success

As discussed in chapter 4, it is best practice for communities to establish
a quanti able goal to keep sites accountable. e local jurisdiction will
need to determine an appropriate metric of success and will need to come
up with a protocol for setting that metric’s value. Metrics of success
should relate to the TDM policy objective determined in step 1 and
should be easy to measure. Principal measurements of success for TDM
policies include automobile trips generated, non-auto-mode use, and
auto parking space use—or some combination of the three.

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Make sure to choose a metric that relates to the TDM policy
  objective.
  Ensure that the metric of success is easy to measure and feasible to
  measure on an ongoing basis.

 STEP 5
Set the Appropriate Goal to Quantify Site-
Based Success

In addition to selecting the appropriate metric for a quantitative
performance measurement of success, the community will need to decide
how that value is set. e value can either be set through negotiation on a
site-by-site basis or by applying a universal formula throughout the TDM
policy area based on proposed land use or site location.

Universal performance benchmarks make the TDM policy more
predictable for developers and more streamlined for communities.
However, negotiating rates with new developments allows communities
to better control their external impacts.

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Make sure the goal is realistic to achieve by basing it on existing data
  for the area where it is being applied.
  Understand the impact that the established goal will have on the
  desire to develop in that area.

 STEP 6
Establish How the TDM Policy Will Be
Monitored

Communities that pursue a quantitative benchmark for new
developments will need to determine how that benchmark is measured.
e speci c monitoring tools that a jurisdiction should use depend on
the performance benchmark that is being used. Following are the
recommended monitoring tools used for each of the metrics discussed in
step 4:

  Automobile trips generated: driveway counts;

  Non-auto-mode use: mode split survey; and

  Automobile parking space use: parking counts.

Monitoring over a longer period allows a community to measure long-
term eﬀects of the TDM policy. Having information about the long-term
eﬀects ensures legitimacy of the TDM policy and garners long-term
support for its implementation. In determining frequency, consider that
surveys require direct contact from residents, employees, and visitors,
whereas traﬃc counts or parking counts do not. at said, to prevent
lower participation rates over time and therefore less statistically accurate
data, surveys are not recommended on an annual basis. Particularly when
a development is new, annual traﬃc or parking counts are recommended
to track the implementation of the TDM program.

However, as the building’s occupancy levels increase and the TDM
program matures as well as demonstrates compliance, the frequency with
which these counts are done should be carefully considered. As the TDM
program matures, its performance is less likely to change considerably
from year to year. e typical lack of variation in performance, combined
with signi cant cost to monitor a TDM program, may make frequent
monitoring eﬀorts unnecessary. In addition to frequency, the community
will need to decide which party will be responsible for monitoring and
evaluation. Most communities make it the property owner’s responsibility
to monitor and evaluate a project’s TDM program.
Consistent with best practices described in chapter 4, the community
should also determine how it will track properties that must provide
TDM over time. Jurisdictions with a large number of properties to
monitor nd that pursuing a CRM system for the purposes of tracking
TDM compliance simpli es their oversight.

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Ensure that the monitoring tool directly measures the TDM policy’s
  goal.
  Consider the cost of monitoring and the quality of the data when
  determining monitoring frequency.
  Decide who will be the party responsible for monitoring.
  ink through how ongoing TDM compliance will be tracked.

 STEP 7

Determine Appropriate TDM Strategies
for Properties Aﬀected by the TDM Policy

Communities need to identify how TDM strategies should be applied to
developments aﬀected by the TDM policy. e communities compared in
chapter 3 had three general approaches for determining applicable TDM
strategies:

  Strategies prescribed by the jurisdiction;
  Strategies negotiated between the jurisdiction and the developer;
  and
  Strategies both prescribed by the jurisdiction and negotiated
  between the jurisdiction and the developer.

Prescribed strategies provide developers with a checklist of tactics to
implement to achieve compliance with the TDM policy. is checklist
approach is eﬀective for jurisdictions that seek speci c strategies or
approaches. Communities with staﬀ who lack the experience to negotiate
bene t from the prescribed approach because it ensures some minimal
level of TDM strategy implementation. Developers gain from the
prescribed approach because they get a more streamlined and predictable
process.

at said, prescribed strategies also have their shortcomings. Certain
prescribed strategies may not always be relevant to proposed
developments, depending on their tenant mix. Prescribed strategies also
need to be reviewed and renewed on an ongoing basis to ensure they
maintain relevance with changes in technology. Last, prescriptive
strategies can restrict use of diﬀerent approaches seeking to achieve
similar outcomes.

Strategies negotiated between the jurisdiction and the developer result in
site-speci c solutions that are more relevant to the development than
prescribed actions discussed previously. However, negotiations result in
disparate TDM actions on a site-by-site basis and therefore require the
jurisdiction to create an organized system to track this information.
Furthermore, communities need to be prepared to provide developers
with clear expectations of what to expect from a round of negotiations.
Otherwise, they can lead to a lot of uncertainty for developers. To provide
more predictability, many jurisdictions publish example plans or
guidelines for complying with the TDM policy.

A handful of communities have a combination of both prescribed and
negotiated actions. is approach allows the jurisdiction to require
certain base actions but also provides exibility to negotiate additional
relevant site-speci c actions. Communities with this approach generally
have within their policy a menu of all TDM actions with an indication of
those that are required rather than optional. Such communities also have
language in their policies that alludes to solutions beyond the provided
list.
In line with best practices, regardless of the approach taken, one strategy
that should always be required is to maintain ongoing communication
with the jurisdiction through a property point person. As discussed in
best practice 5 (see chapter 4), this ensures that strategies including
monitoring and evaluation are implemented appropriately over time.

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Determine whether universal TDM strategies should be applied to
  all developments.
  Consider whether the jurisdiction has the time, resources, and
  expertise to negotiate with developers on TDM strategies.
  Seek to understand what the development community might prefer.
  Consider how TDM strategies will be tracked and maintained over
  time.
  Determine if it is more important to have a process that is
  consistent, streamlined, and predictable or to have exibility to
  evolve and maintain relevance.

 STEP 8

Determine Whether a TDM Plan Is
Required

TDM plans serve to outline the development’s TDM-related strategies
and the anticipated tactics it will use to meet them. Communities need to
determine whether to require a TDM plan as a means for a property to
demonstrate a clear path to successfully implement the site-speci c TDM
strategies. e decision should be made based on the outcome of steps 4
and 7. Jurisdictions that do not require TDM plans will be most eﬀective
where TDM strategies are predetermined, are applied universally, and do
not include quantitative measurements of success. However, most
jurisdictions reviewed, nine out of 10, require new developments to
complete a TDM plan before a building is occupied. Jurisdictions that
require a TDM plan should provide examples and guidelines for
preparing it.

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Determine whether developments should provide a TDM plan to
  organize their TDM implementation eﬀorts.
  If a TDM plan is required, jurisdictions should provide guidelines
  for preparing the plan to ensure consistency.

 STEP 9

Decide on an Enforcement Mechanism to
Ensure TDM Policy Compliance

Communities that monitor performance of TDM plans need to
determine whether they will have an enforcement mechanism to hold
new developments accountable for implementing the plans. Traditional
enforcement mechanisms include applying nancial penalties, limiting
the use of facilities, or both.

Financial penalties are typically tied to an upfront nancial contribution
that is required of the new development by the TDM policy. Once the
development has complied with the policy, all or part of the nancial
contribution is returned to the property owner. Financial contributions
can also come in the form of investments in local transportation
improvements or funding earmarked for a site-speci c TDM program.

Limiting the use of facilities is typically achieved by revoking a speci c
right that was granted to the development during the planning process. It
can also be achieved by halting the development process altogether until
compliance is achieved.

Creation of enforcement mechanisms within the TDM policy requires
buy-in from both the development community and political leadership.
is is best achieved through engagement with stakeholders and
education about the TDM policy and its intended outcomes.

CONSIDERATIONS:

  Understand what compliance tools the development community
  nds are most eﬀective at holding them accountable.

  Identify the most appropriate tool to leverage compliance.

Detailed Case Studies of TDM
Policy by Jurisdiction

e TDM policy ndings included in chapters 3 through 5
were based on a thorough review of the comprehensive

  Arlington County, Virginia

  Arlington County is an inner-ring suburb of Washington, D.C.,
  located across the Potomac River from the nation’s capital. is 26-
  square-mile jurisdiction with relatively high population and job
  density, as well as diverse transportation options, is considered
  urban in character. As of 2015, Arlington was home to 217,000
  residents and 222,000 jobs. By 2040, Arlington’s population is
  projected to grow to 283,000 people and 300,000 jobs.

HISTORY OF TDM ORDINANCE
Since 1990, Arlington has used TDM as an important tool to reduce the
impacts development has on existing infrastructure. e county’s
combined approach of coordinating land uses with transportation
infrastructure while applying TDM programs has resulted in at rates of
automobile traﬃc volume in the last 20 years despite increases in jobs and
population. e county incorporated TDM as a principal element of the
Transportation Master Plan. rough site-speci c development
conditions, the county requires TDM for new developments.

  Developer incentive: e TDM Ordinance for Arlington County is
  applied when following the site plan development option. Within
  certain districts, the site plan development option provides for site-
  speci c exibility in development form, use, and density, beyond
  what is otherwise permitted by right. For developers this exibility
  in development serves as an incentive to provide TDM.

TRIGGERS

In Arlington County, TDM is typically required for projects where a site
plan is needed for development. Need for a site plan is oen determined
on a case-by-case basis and provides the developer exibility in the
development form, use, and density, beyond what is otherwise permitted
by right.

  Size of development (density): e density of the development near
  site-density maximums may trigger a site plan. If appropriate, the
  county may request that TDM be incorporated into the development
  conditions for the site.

  Applicable land use: e majority of site plan review proposals are
  for hotel, residential, oﬃce, and mixed-use development. If
  appropriate, the county may request that TDM be incorporated into
  the development conditions for the site.

  Provided parking (parking spaces): Reductions in parking may
  require a site plan. If so, the site must demonstrate within the
  Transportation Management Plan (TMP) measures to reduce the
  need for parking on the site.

  Location: Typically, site plans are required for projects located in
  high-density zoning districts and within Metro corridors. If
  appropriate, the county may request that TDM be incorporated into
  the development conditions for the site.

  Traﬃc impact (number of vehicle trips): e traﬃc impact of a
  project does not trigger TDM.

REQUIREMENTS

Arlington County requires that projects triggering TDM obligations
address them during the following stages of development:

  Pre–site plan approval: Site-speci c TDM strategies are negotiated
  between the developer and the county as part of the site
  development conditions. Strategies highlighted in the TDM policy
  published in 1990 are used as guidelines for desired TDM conditions
  by the county.

  Post–site plan approval: No TDM requirements post–site plan
  approval.

  Preconstruction: No TDM requirements preconstruction.

  Preoccupancy: e developer must submit a TMP before obtaining
  issuance of the shell and core certi cate of occupancy. Unless
  otherwise speci ed, the developer agrees that all individual elements
  of the TMP shall be operational before issuance of the shell and core
  certi cate of occupancy, including the rst annual contribution to
  Arlington County Commuter Services (ACCS).

  Postoccupancy: Postoccupancy, any remaining TMP strategies are
  implemented for the site using the timetable and steps outlined in
  the TMP. roughout the life of the TDM program, TDM program
  managers are expected to regularly engage with Arlington County’s
  business-to-business TDM service provider, Arlington
  Transportation Partners. County staﬀ maintains involvement with
  the program, ensuring that the site maintains compliance with the
  obligations outlined in the site’s TMP.
STRATEGIES AND TDM PLAN ELEMENTS

e standard site plan conditions for high-density oﬃce, residential, and
mixed-use developments provide a base TDM program for developers to
include within their development conditions. e sample TDM program
includes the following components:

  TDM program participation and funding:
  Establish and maintain an active ongoing relationship with
  Arlington Transportation Partners.
  Designate a member of building management as the property
  transportation coordinator.
  Contribute annually to ACCS at a rate of $0.06 per square foot
  of commercial and $0.035 per square foot of residential,
  adjusted for in ation for 30 years.

  Transportation facilities and improvements:
  Provide information displays.
  Maintain on-site bus stops or shelters.
  Provide a shuttle (for hotel).
  Provide a bike facilities management plan.

  Carpool, vanpool, and car-sharing parking:
  Provide reserved spaces for carpools and vanpools.
  Provide carpool/vanpool parking subsidies.
  Encourage placement of car-sharing vehicles.

  Alternative transportation promotions, services, and policies:
  Distribute welcome kits with transportation information.
  Provide transit, bike-sharing, or car-sharing subsidies.
  Provide a sustainable commuter bene t program.
  Provide transportation information on property website.
  Reference the nearest Metro stations/bus routes/other
  transportation amenities in promotional materials and
  advertising.
  Distribute transportation information up to four times a year.

  Performance and monitoring:
  Perform ongoing surveys, traﬃc counts, parking counts, etc.
  Submit an annual report.

Based on clear, convincing evidence, the developer may propose
substitutions to certain strategies with other strategies, provided that the
proposed substitution furnishes an equivalent value.

METRICS FOR SUCCESS

Arlington County does not have performance standards. However, it does
monitor site performance through annual evaluation.

ENFORCEMENT

Arlington County deploys several enforcement techniques to ensure that
TMP strategies are eﬀectively implemented. ey occur both during the
entitlement process and postoccupancy.

  Staﬀ: Arlington Department of Environmental Services is
  responsible for site plan compliance, development conditions, and
  long term-monitoring of TDM programs.
  Enforcement mechanism: Development conditions are a result of
  negotiation between the county and the developer.

  Penalties (ﬁnancial): Developers may be ned for noncompliance
  with the TMP.

  Penalties (delayed construction): If TDM has been agreed to by the
  developer and the county, then lack of compliance can result in
  project delays.

  Penalties (use of facilities): Developers who do not comply with
  development conditions are not allowed to continue through the
  entitlement process and will therefore not be allowed to use the
  facilities they are constructing.

MONITORING AND EVALUATION

On an annual basis, county staﬀ will conduct on-site visits with property
management to monitor TDM implementation throughout the site. e
county posts a summary of results on its website.

  Survey: Within two years of a building’s occupancy, ve years aer
  occupancy, and each subsequent ve years, Arlington County may
  require properties to report mode split through surveys. Because
  most building owners have no experience with this kind of data
  collection, Arlington County typically conducts performance
  monitoring studies on behalf of buildings, to assist them with their
  reporting requirements.

  Traﬃc counts: Within two years of a building’s occupancy, ve years
  aer occupancy, and each subsequent ve years, Arlington County
  may require properties to report trip generation through traﬃc
  counts. Because most building owners have no experience with this
  kind of data collection, Arlington County typically conducts
  performance monitoring studies on behalf of buildings, to assist
  them with their reporting requirements.

  Parking use: Arlington County may require properties to report
  parking use through parking counts within two years of a building’s
  occupancy, ve years aer occupancy, and each subsequent ve
  years. Because most building owners have no experience with this
  kind of data collection, Arlington County typically conducts
  performance monitoring studies on behalf of buildings, to assist
  them with their reporting requirements.

  Annual report: Annual letters to the county manager, describing
  the TDM-related activities for the site, are required to provide an
  ongoing record of the site’s TDM activities.

SOAR ANALYSIS

Government perspective: Arlington County staﬀ views the strength of
their policy to be the consistency of the ordinance over time and across
developers. e TDM policy has a standard baseline set of conditions to
make the development process predictable. Over time standards are
modi ed, as staﬀ members work with developers on implementing those
conditions. Ongoing performance monitoring allows for data collection
that informs the eﬀectiveness of the TDM policy and encourages updates
over time. Last, the requirement to have a transportation coordinator at
each building allows the county to maintain a relationship with each
building. Having a chance to train building representatives creates an
opportunity to create more awareness of the TDM program.

Arlington sees several opportunities to improve its TDM policy. For one,
the county would like the policy to include building-based performance
goals, such as mode split and trip counts. is would hold a building
developer or manager accountable should it not be doing its part to
mitigate trips. e county would also like to see the policy transition to a
countywide ordinance, which would make the policy less discretionary.

As a result of this policy, Arlington has been able to approve more dense
developments over time without aﬀecting traﬃc. It has also been able to
win community support for in ll development. Over the past 25 years,
traﬃc in Arlington County has maintained the same level despite
increases in population.

Developer perspective: e developers interviewed saw the value of
Arlington’s TDM policy requirements as something that helps lease their
buildings. One perspective was that bad traﬃc congestion around the
building creates leasing diﬃculties. So developers see the policy as
holding themselves and other new higher-density buildings responsible
to do their part to address area-wide traﬃc congestion. Another
perspective shared was that tenants perceive things like real-time
transportation information screens, transit passes, bike-sharing stations,
and bike storage as amenities that make properties more desirable.

e developers interviewed felt an opportunity exists to improve
Arlington’s TDM policy by making it more outcome-based and less
prescriptive. is perception was expressed in terms of the types of TDM
strategies that are required. Developers would prefer their TDM
requirements to be part of a “living document” that could be adjusted
over time, based on results of required ongoing transportation surveys.
Related to this desired outcome-based approach would be any TDM-
related nancial contributions that developers make. Instead of making a

 at annual TDM payment, which is perceived as a tax rather than a value,
they would prefer to pay the county only when ongoing monitoring
eﬀorts demonstrate the desired outcomes have not been met. ese
payments would be made and directed to help the property achieve its
TDM goals. Developers would like the option to choose whether to make
that fee for service payment to Arlington County or some other
nongovernmental entity.

e developers interviewed unanimously agree that because TDM
strategies are so eﬀective in Arlington an opportunity exists to do away
with parking minimums, allow more shared parking, and streamline the
parking management plan review process for mixed-use buildings.

Developers would like a TDM policy that results in high levels of
transportation access with minimal traﬃc delay and maintains the
desirability to locate in their building and in Arlington as a whole.

  Bloomington, Minnesota

  Bloomington is located at the southern edge of Minneapolis and is
  the fourth-largest city in Minnesota. Bloomington has a population
  of more than 86,000 residents and almost 99,000 jobs. It is home to
  the country’s largest enclosed shopping center, the Mall of America,
  which contributes to Bloomington’s jobs per capita exceeding both
  Minneapolis and St. Paul. Bloomington comprises a mix of urban
  and suburban land uses and has medium transit access, including a
  light-rail line that connects to downtown Minneapolis and a bus
  rapid transit (BRT) line.

HISTORY OF TDM ORDINANCE

Although TDM has been used as a strategy to reduce traﬃc congestion in
Bloomington for several decades, it only became a formal element within
the city’s zoning ordinance in 2009. Before the TDM ordinance, the city’s
zoning code had provisions for TDM; however, these policies were
scattered and confusing for those who had to follow them. Furthermore,
these provisions did not indicate what development impact thresholds
would trigger TDM requirements. e city council determined TDM
obligations for new developments on a case-by-case basis, causing a level
of unpredictability for developers unsure about whether their plans
would trigger TDM requirements. In 2006, city staﬀ began working with
the development community to address these issues by implementing a
revised TDM ordinance with more predictability and clarity.

  Developer involvement: e city and development community
  were in full agreement that TDM obligations have to be simple and
  easy to understand. City staﬀers initially introduced a three-tier
  proposal for the TDM zoning ordinance. However, concerns from
  the development community over the complexity of project
  classi cation reduced this proposal to two tiers.

  e city and development community compromised on
  enforcement mechanisms to ensure compliance with the TDM
  zoning ordinance without aﬀecting development desirability in the
  city. As a mechanism of enforcement, the city proposed to guarantee
  TDM compliance through an upfront payment. However,
  developers were initially concerned that this nancial guarantee was
  too high and that it would discourage development in the city. In
  response, the city reduced the nancial guarantee to a more
  agreeable amount. e development community was also concerned
  about being unfairly penalized for ineﬀectively changing individual
  driving habits, with the city responding by adding a “good faith
  eﬀort” clause to ensure that a solid attempt at changing travel
  behavior would still result in a refund of the nancial security. Last,
  in a conscientious eﬀort to not aﬀect smaller development plans, the
  city—as a result of developers’ concerns—reduced TDM
  requirements for smaller developments that would be
  disproportionately aﬀected by the TDM ordinance.

  Developer incentive: A developer can reduce the amount of parking
  it is required to provide by up to 10 percent. Developers in special
  commercial districts may also increase their oor/area ratio (FAR)
  by 50 percent and their maximum building coverage by 40 percent
  as long as they would not have generated additional peak-hour
  traﬃc had they chosen not to increase their FAR and building
  coverage.

TRIGGERS

e city’s TDM ordinance splits TDM development requirements into
two tiers based on the proposed land use, the size of the development, or
the project’s parking requirements. Tier 1 TDM policies, which have
more intense TDM requirements for compliance, are targeted toward
larger developments in the city. Tier 2 TDM policies primarily seek to
increase the education and awareness of all transportation options and
are targeted toward moderately sized developments.

  Size of development (square footage): Tier 1 TDM requirements
  are triggered by the proposed number of parking spaces (see
  provided parking below). Tier 2 TDM requirements are triggered by
  developments or redevelopments that propose to provide more than
  1,000 square feet of nonresidential land uses and do not require a
  tier 1 TDM program.

  Applicable land use: Tier 1 and tier 2 TDM programs apply only to
  nonresidential projects. Places of assembly, schools (elementary to
  high school), parks and recreational facilities, and other institutions
  not in operation during the peak weekday traﬃc periods (6:30–9:00
  a.m. and 3:00–6:00 p.m.) are exempt from TDM program
  requirements.

  Provided parking (number of parking spaces): A tier 1 TDM
  program is required by city code if the development or
  redevelopment proposes to provide more than 350 parking spaces or
  an increase in parking of over 25 percent from existing parking
  space amounts.

  Location: Projects in special commercial districts may provide a tier
  1 TDM program in exchange for a 50 percent increase in FAR and
  may increase their maximum building coverage by 40 percent,
  provided they would not have increased traﬃc more than if they had
  chosen not to increase their FAR and building coverage.

  Traﬃc impact (number of vehicle trips): Traﬃc impact is not taken
  into account when determining whether a project triggers the TDM
  ordinance.

REQUIREMENTS

Bloomington requires that projects triggering TDM obligations address
them during the following stages of development:
  Pre–site plan approval: No TDM requirements pre–site plan
  approval.

  Post–site plan approval: No TDM requirements post–site plan
  approval.

  Preconstruction: Tier 1 projects require that a TDM plan be
  submitted before obtaining a building permit for the site. To prepare
  the TDM plan, a traﬃc study must be conducted beforehand that
  outlines the anticipated traﬃc and parking impacts of the new
  development or redevelopment project. Once the plan has been
  completed, the developer must obtain and le a TDM agreement
  prepared by the city attorney’s oﬃce between the property owner
  and the city. Tier 2 projects must provide a TDM plan describing the
  TDM strategies the developer agrees to implement to reduce peak
  single-occupancy vehicle (SOV) trips before obtaining a building
  permit.

  Preoccupancy: Tier 1 programs require a nancial guarantee in the
  form of cash, bond, or letter of credit equal to $50 per code-required
  parking space submitted before the site receives its certi cate of
  occupancy. e city retains the payment for at least two years from
  the date the property owner veri es that the leasable area of the
  development has reached occupancy of 30 percent (the TDM
  implementation date).

  Postoccupancy: Tier 1 TDM programs require ongoing annual
  reports for two years following the initial TDM implementation
  date.

STRATEGIES AND TDM PLAN ELEMENTS

Tier 1 projects require a TDM plan that includes the following elements:

  A description of the TDM goals, including peak-hour SOV trip-
  reduction goals.

  A description of TDM strategies and implementation measures and
  the anticipated SOV trip reduction associated with each strategy.
  TDM measures in the plan may include, but are not limited to, the
  following:

  On-site transit facilities;
  Preferential location of carpool and vanpool parking;
  Telecommuting;
  On-site bicycle and pedestrian facilities; and
  Employer-subsidized transit passes.

  A description of the evaluation measures and process that will be
  used to determine the eﬀectiveness of the TDM strategies used and
  progress toward achieving the SOV trip-reduction goals.
  A proposed total expenditure required to implement the TDM
  strategies for at least three years following the issuance of the
  certi cate of occupancy.

Tier 2 projects require a simpler TDM plan that includes a description of
TDM strategies the property owner agrees to implement to reduce peak
SOV trip generation.
