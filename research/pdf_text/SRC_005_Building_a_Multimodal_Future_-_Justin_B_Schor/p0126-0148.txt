From a monitoring and evaluation perspective, property owners are
unsure why they pursue ongoing monitoring and evaluation. ey are
also unsure whether anything is being done with the information they
provide to the city. ey are also not very clear how the city is empowered
to penalize property owners.

  Snohomish County, Washington

  Located north of Seattle is Snohomish County. is county of
  mostly suburban and urban character has low to medium transit
  access. e county hosts about 713,335 residents and 238,400 jobs
  and is considered the third-most-populous county in Washington.

HISTORY OF TDM ORDINANCE
Developer incentive: e county views urban growth areas as places to
focus future county growth. e comprehensive plan for Snohomish
County calls for urban growth areas to have higher densities, provide

 exibility in land use regulations, and mix uses.

TRIGGERS

TDM triggers in the county ordinance detail the overall trip reduction a
project should achieve based on where it is being developed and what is
proposed. Should a project fall under multiple triggers, the trip-reduction
obligations for that project are combined.

  Size of development (units/square footage/number of employees):
  e county ordinance does not have a trigger for the proposed size
  of a development.

  Applicable land use: e county ordinance applies a TDM trigger
  to all land uses.

  Provided parking (parking spaces): e county ordinance does not
  have a trigger for the amount of parking that is proposed.

  Location: If a development is proposed in an urban growth area, the
  developer must implement TDM measures to reduce 5 percent of
  p.m. peak-hour trips. Further TDM obligations may be imposed if a
  project is proposing a signi cant traﬃc impact (see traﬃc impact)
  within a transportation service area (TSA) that contains one or more
  arterial units in arrears or at design capacity (as determined by the
  Department of Public Works).

  Traﬃc impact (vehicle trips): If a proposed development is forecast
  to generate more than 50 peak-hour trips in a TSA that contains one
  or more arterial units in arrears or at design capacity, the county will
  ask the developer to mitigate the proposed project’s impact by
  providing TDM measures equal to a 10 percent reduction in peak-
  hour trips.

REQUIREMENTS

Snohomish County’s ordinance associates a monetary value to a
developer’s trip-reduction responsibility equal to $6,500 for every p.m.
peak-hour trip removed. More simply stated, the nancial responsibility
of a developer’s trip-reduction commitment is equal to $6,500 times the
trip reduction, times the overall trip generation. Given that amount,
developers have several options for satisfying their trip-reduction
obligation through “credits.” Developers may ful ll their trip-reduction
responsibilities through one or a combination of options discussed in the
“Strategies and TDM Plan Elements” section. Depending on the route
that the developer takes to ful ll the trip requirement, diﬀerent timings
apply.

  Pre–site plan approval: Should a developer seek trip-reduction
  credits through the construction of on-site design features or city-
  speci ed “additional trip reduction credits,” a TDM plan must be
  submitted to the city along with the development application. If the
  developer seeks to obtain trip-reduction credits through
  construction of oﬀ-site improvements or a voluntary contribution to
  the county, then the developer is not required to submit a TDM plan
  but must submit a written proposal of its intention.

  Post–site plan approval: No post–site plan approval requirements.

  Preconstruction: If the developer opted to achieve its trip-reduction
  credits through voluntary payment, then the payment must be made
  before obtaining the site building permit.

  Preoccupancy: If the developer chose to achieve its trip-reduction
  credits through the construction of on-site design features or city-
  speci ed “additional trip reduction credits,” TDM features in the
  TDM plan must be constructed before receiving the certi cate of
  occupancy. If the developer chose to achieve its trip-reduction
  credits through construction of oﬀ-site improvements, then the
  construction of improvements must be complete before obtaining
  the certi cate of occupancy.

  Postoccupancy: No postoccupancy requirements.

STRATEGIES AND TDM PLAN ELEMENTS

As described in the previous section, developers may choose to comply
with their project’s trip-reduction requirement through multiple means.
Following is a detailed description of each.
Trip-reduction credits through construction of oﬀ-site improvements:
Developers may meet their trip-reduction requirement by constructing
an oﬀ-site improvement that equals or exceeds the amount of their trip-
reduction nancial responsibility. e construction must be on a capital
improvements list prepared for the TSA by the Department of Public
Works with input from the local transit agency. Developers pursuing this
route must prepare a proposal that identi es their intent. e oﬀ-site
improvement must be constructed before any certi cate of occupancy or

 nal inspection will be issued for the development.

Trip-reduction credit through voluntary contribution to county: As an
alternative to constructing a speci c oﬀ-site improvement, the developer
may opt to voluntarily pay the nancial value of the trip reduction to the
county. ese funds are placed in a special account separate from the
general fund for use in implementing transportation mitigation
strategies. Developers pursuing this route must prepare a proposal that
identi es their intent. Funds must be submitted to the county before
release of a building permit.

Trip-reduction credit through site design features: Unlike the rst two
options, developers may get trip-reduction credits through their own
property’s site design. A 5 percent reduction is given to all commercial
and multifamily developments that incorporate all of the following
sitewide features:

  A design for a basic circulation system that provides continuity of
  pedestrian systems related to the primary road network;

  A safe, convenient pedestrian facility that meets the Engineering
  Design and Development Standards (EDDS) that joins the front
  building entrance(s) directly with frontage improvements;

  A safe, convenient pedestrian facility that meets the EDDS that joins
  the front building entrance(s) with all other on-site front building
  entrances;

  A safe, convenient pedestrian facility that meets the EDDS that joins
  building entrance(s) with any bus stop or pedestrian facility (e.g.,
  commuter trail) located adjacent to the development;
  Where practicable and desirable for pedestrian access, provision of
  special easements to facilitate pedestrian circulation between the site
  and adjacent neighborhoods, schools, shopping areas, transit
  facilities, or other activity centers;

  Where practicable and desirable, the use of minimum setbacks to
  reduce walking distances;

  Where practicable and desirable, the placement of vehicle parking to
  the sides and the rear of the buildings;

  Where practicable and desirable, lighting and weather protection for
  pedestrian facilities;

  For nonresidential developments, secure bicycle parking (preferably
  covered) spaces located near the front entrance(s) that produce at
  least 2 percent of the development’s calculated p.m. peak-hour trips;
  and

  For employment sites, assigned preferential parking spaces for
  carpools or vanpools that number at least 6 percent of any employee
  parking spaces.

e county will also provide a 5 percent trip-reduction credit to any
subdivision or short subdivision for single-family and/or duplex
residential units deemed “TDM compatible” by incorporating all of the
following on-site design features:

  A design for a basic circulation system that provides continuity of
  pedestrian systems related to the primary road network;

  A safe, convenient pedestrian facility that meets the EDDS that joins
  building entrance(s) with any bus stop or pedestrian facility (e.g.,
  commuter trail) located adjacent to the development;

  Where practicable and desirable for pedestrian access, provision of
  special easements to facilitate pedestrian circulation between the site
  and adjacent neighborhoods, schools, shopping areas, transit
  facilities, or other activity centers;

  Where practicable and desirable, lighting and weather protection for
  pedestrian facilities; and

  An overall density of at least four dwelling units per gross acre.
Additional opportunities for trip-reduction credits: Beyond the
strategies indicated in the preceding section, commercial and multifamily
developments may achieve additional credits by doing the following:

  Constructing or incorporating bicycle facilities and reducing
  automobile parking spaces (2 percent trip-reduction credit);

  Voluntarily agreeing to implement a trip-reduction program (5
  percent trip reduction credit); or

  Providing on-site measures with an area-wide impact (5 percent
  trip-reduction credit).

METRICS FOR SUCCESS

Projects in Snohomish County have a trip-reduction percentage as their
metric of success. Depending on the location and size of the project,
varying degrees of trip reduction are required.

ENFORCEMENT

Snohomish deploys several enforcement techniques to ensure that TDM
trip-reduction goals are eﬀectively achieved. ey occur primarily during
the entitlement process.

  Staﬀ: e Department of Public Works is required to enforce
  developer TDM obligations.

  Enforcement mechanism: TDM is enforced by law as part of the
  county code.

  Penalties (ﬁnancial): If the developer chooses not to incorporate
  planned TDM site features, ceases to maintain the TDM design
  features, or discontinues the trip-reduction program, the developer
  is required to make a payment to the county equal to the amount of
  the discount(s) resulting from the initial credit.

  Penalties (delayed construction): Failure to comply with TDM
  obligations will result in delays in the entitlement process.

  Penalties (use of facilities): Developers who do not comply with
  county code are not allowed to continue through the entitlement
  process and will therefore not be allowed to use the facilities they are
  constructing.
MONITORING AND EVALUATION

Snohomish requires a combination of surveys and annual reports to
monitor and evaluate performance toward trip-reduction goals.

  Survey: A survey is only required if the developer decides to
  implement a commute trip-reduction (CTR) program as an
  additional opportunity for trip-reduction credits. Surveys are
  conducted annually.
  Traﬃc counts: None.
  Parking use: None.
  Annual report: An annual report is required only if the developer
  decides to implement a CTR program as an additional opportunity
  for trip-reduction credits.

SOAR ANALYSIS

Government perspective: Snohomish County believes that the most
valuable part of its TDM ordinance is the option it gives developers to opt
out of TDM requirements for a development fee. is fee is then used
toward the administration of a regional TDM program that works mainly
with residential uses and is administered by the local transit agency.

e county notes that the TDM ordinance is somewhat out of date and
can be updated. A lot of the site design credits it provides to developers
have now been standardized in site design. e county would also like to
expand the types of infrastructure strategies that developers can
construct to meet the TDM requirement.

Over the 20-year life of the ordinance, the county believes that it has
experienced modest change by providing infrastructure improvements in
the county overall. Should the county redo the ordinance, it would ask for
more from developers.

Developer perspective: Although most developers opt in for the fee,
those interviewed who opted for a program that had ongoing TDM
marketing have seen positive impacts on their community because of
their TDM program. Anecdotally, they have seen a drop in parking and
traﬃc and see the program as an amenity. Unfortunately, without
standard monitoring and evaluation, this is hard to quantify.
CHAPTER 7

Conclusion

e 10 communities examined in chapter 6 represent a cross
section of jurisdictions—ranging in population from 30,000
to several million and in size from six square miles to more
than 2,000 square miles. eir respective densities, Walk
Scores, and availability of transit service are equally diverse.

Despite these diﬀerences, they all seek more dense and
mixed-use development. But many face a great challenge—
the perception that mixed-use higher densities bring more
people, more cars, and more congestion.

ese communities have all looked to TDM to combat that
perception through a variety of policies, and no two applied
identical solutions. However, as noted in chapter 5, when it
comes to creating TDM policy, common decision points
exist at which critical questions need to be addressed.

Our hope is that this book provides not only a framework
and best practices to address these questions, but also that it
will inspire developers and local government leaders to work
together to create and apply TDM policies and strategies that
will allow their communities to grow more sustainably, with
increased density and mixed uses—and reduced traﬃc
congestion.
