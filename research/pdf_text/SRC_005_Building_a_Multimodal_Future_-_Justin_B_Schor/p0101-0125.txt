SOAR ANALYSIS

Government perspective: e strength of the TDM ordinance is its
 exibility and ability to change over time. e TDM ordinance places

responsibility on the developer to mitigate its transportation impact.
TDM commitments are tied to the land and therefore continue as site
requirements aer ownership is transferred. Goals and standards for
monitoring allow the impact of the TDM program at the site to be
measured over time.

Some developers try to get out of their TDM responsibilities by making
triggers confusing and hard to track. For that reason, the county would
like to revisit its guidelines to provide more guidance and clarity on what
it expects as part of a site’s TDM development conditions. From an
administrative side, the county has had diﬃculty tracking project
monitoring, and some projects have fallen through the cracks.

Over time, the county would like to see developer attitudes toward TDM
shi from something that developers have to do to something they want
to do. e county would also like to start seeing developers go beyond
their requirements. To date, trips have been reduced and no one has had
to use the TDM remedy or penalty fund.

Developer perspective: Developers were highly supportive of the
standardized metrics that are part of Fairfax County’s TDM policy as well
as the fact that they are accountable to measure their performance against
them. ey expressed the importance of those measurements to see the
return on investment in a TDM program at their site. Developers
indicated those measurements help them feel con dent the money they
are spending is working. It also allows the public to see the bene t of
TDM and high-density development in reducing traﬃc in the future.

Developers felt the key strength of Fairfax County’s TDM policy is that it
oﬀers developers an incentive to achieve a standardized metric both in
the form of things they want (density) and things they want to avoid
( nancial penalties).

Although extra eﬀort may be required to determine which strategies
make most sense to help developers achieve the goals at their site,
developers appreciated the exibility of implementing what makes sense
for their tenants. Doing so allowed them to deliver transportation
amenities that not only create value for their tenants, but also improve
traﬃc conditions in Fairfax County; which makes their building more
appealing.
Among developers sentiment was unanimous that the language of TDM
conditions of development (called “proﬀers” in Fairfax County) could be
written in a much more concise, simple, and clear manner, so that anyone
can understand them. Complementing this desire to simplify the TDM
process was a desire to minimize the detail required in the TDM plan
documents that support the TDM development conditions, until such
time as the tenant demographics are more certain.

Some of the clarity desired by developers relates to knowing exactly when
their TDM commitments would end. is would allow them to reduce
lender concerns about having no cap to their potentially ongoing TDM-
related expenses.

Although developers are highly supportive of the exibility they have to
implement strategies that make sense for their tenants, some would have
bene tted from better promotion and awareness of Fairfax County’s
TDM strategy guidelines and best practices on how to successfully
achieve their goals. ose guidelines could be updated annually with new
successful TDM strategies that were implemented and reported on as
part of annual reports received by Fairfax County.

Some developers expressed interest in the opportunity to garner greater
economies of scale by working in coordination with other properties
through a transportation management association.

Developers would like a TDM policy that results in less traﬃc congestion
and a more pedestrian-friendly community that has multimodal
transportation choices and connectivity between places in the county.
is will create market conditions where people in the county choose to
drive a car only when they need to. Developers noted these conditions are
critical to sustainably attract and retain the businesses and residents
required to achieve full buildout.

HISTORY OF TDM ORDINANCE

e concept of TDM was introduced into city bylaws in 1987, through
Article XII of Chapter 135 of the Code of the Town of Lexington under
Traﬃc. is article establishes minimum criteria for requiring traﬃc
studies and mitigating traﬃc impacts caused by a proposed development.
In 1997, the planning board adopted a TDM policy, which is more
detailed than Article XII, providing a framework for developments aimed
at reducing the use of automobiles while improving mobility
opportunities through alternative transportation options.
In 2009, Lexington chose to increase the amount of development along
one of its central corridors, Hartwell Avenue. Realizing that increased
development would aﬀect transportation along that corridor, the town, in
conjunction with its general plan recommendation, adopted an overlay
district that makes the development process more streamlined and
predictable within that district.

  Developer involvement: Developers were brought into the planning
  process for the creation of the TDM policy and overlay district.
  Developers are also allowed to negotiate aspects of their PTDM plan
  with the town.

  Developer incentive: e 1997 TDM policy allows for a framework
  to mitigate transportation concerns such as traﬃc or parking when a
  developer chooses to apply for a special permit. e pursuit of a
  special permit provides the developer with development exibility.
  Developers in the overlay are given the opportunity to go through a
  more predictable and streamlined development process for projects
  that would normally require a special permit.

TRIGGERS

e town of Lexington has two mechanisms that could trigger a
developer to provide TDM: the 1997 TDM policy and the overlay district.

  Size of development (units/square footage): Compliance with the
  TDM policy is recommended for developments that propose to
  construct 10,000 square feet or more of commercial space or 25
  units or more of residential, as well as propose to increase density

  Applicable land use: e TDM policy or overlay district can apply
  to any use.

  Provided parking (number of parking spaces): TDM policy
  recommendations are not triggered by proposed parking. Projects
  electing to comply with overlay district regulations that propose to
  construct or expand their development with 24 parking spaces or
  more must submit a full PTDM plan. Projects electing to comply
  with overlay district regulations but that propose less than 24
  parking spaces must submit a small project PTDM plan.

  Location (where the site is located): If a proposed project lies
  within the overlay district, it may elect to comply with the overlay
  district regulations in exchange for a more streamlined and
  predictable development process.

  Traﬃc impact (number of vehicle trips): e TDM policy is
  recommended for developments where a change of use increases the

METRICS FOR SUCCESS

e Bayshore area has a geographically unique constraint that requires
strict compliance with metrics. Given that the Bayshore area has only
three entrances, congestion of these entrances could be detrimental to the
economic success of the area. A capacity analysis of the entrances
estimated that all three entrances combined could accommodate a total
of 18,900 vehicles during the morning peak (between 7 and 10 a.m.). is
trip maximum could be achieved if all future development capped mode
split at 45 percent SOV. For this reason, proposed TDM programs are
designed to achieve an SOV mode-split goal of 45 percent and a trip cap
assuming a 45 percent SOV mode share and 10 percent carpool mode
share (unless the carpool mode split is expected to be higher).

SOAR ANALYSIS

Government perspective: Mountain View staﬀ members believe the
policy has a clear objective for implementing the community’s vision for
TDM in North Bayshore. It has a clear expectation and process for how to
reduce single-occupancy vehicle trips. Because no building has been
completed at the time of this research, the jurisdiction is still not sure
how the policy will play out and whether developers will be successful.

Developer perspective: Local developers understand the need for the
policy and agree that it is very relevant to the conditions present in the
Bayshore area. ey appreciate that the ordinance is measurable and that
it focuses on the “right thing,” peak-hour trip reduction. Some developers
indicated they had provided many of the TDM strategies that are
required of them before implementation of the TDM policy.

Developers noted several things that would make the policy more
eﬀective. ey feel the policy potentially limits the type of tenants that
will lease the buildings they are developing. Large tenants with the
resources available to provide TDM will have no problem implementing
strategies that are required of them, but smaller rms may not have the
resources available to provide eﬀective TDM. A potential solution they
noted would be to have more exibility in the types of TDM strategies
pursued so that employers could decide for themselves how best to meet
the TDM goal. Although the developers appreciated the enhanced
multimodal investments and the opportunity to increase their
developments’ density, they did suggest that the plan could be more
reward-focused rather than penalty-focused with regard to TDM.

Developers also noted that because trip reduction is percentage-based
rather than trip-based, it disproportionally aﬀects smaller developments
in the Bayshore area, which may contribute the same penalty amount
despite having less total impact. Developers also note that the goals do
not encourage them to go beyond their trip-reduction requirements. Last,
the moratorium on new development should the trip cap not be met
actually hurts potential developers much more than existing property
owners who are responsible for generating the noncompliance. Penalties
are a drop in the bucket compared to the potential revenue that a
developer could be making should it be allowed to develop its land.

Despite the strictness of the TDM policies applied to development in
Mountain View, developers are not discouraged from developing there
again should the opportunity arise.

HISTORY OF TDM ORDINANCE

Recognizing the importance of growing sustainably, Seattle has had TDM
policies in its municipal code since the 1980s. Over time, these policies
have slowly been updated to re ect the changing urban and technological
landscape. e most recent of these updates was in October 2015. e
city’s municipal code has policies that require TDM strategies for trip
mitigation for both developments and large individual employers.

  Developer involvement: Developers were invited to participate in
  the development of TDM requirements through focus groups held
  during major municipal code updates. For the most part, developers
  understand that TDM is a conditional requirement and accept it.
  ey have mentioned that TDM introduces an element of
  uncertainty to the development process.

  Developer incentive: In Seattle Mixed (SM) zones and in the
  Northgate Overlay District, developers are granted higher FARs,

  exibility in land use, and setbacks as well as decreased parking
  ratios.

TRIGGERS

Seattle has two primary mechanisms that could trigger a developer to
provide TDM: the municipal code and special overlay districts.

  Size of development (square footage): Under the municipal code,
  all developments that propose over 100,000 square feet of
  commercial, oﬃce, or retail uses trigger TDM requirements. e
  Department of Planning and Development (DPD) may use its
  discretion to obligate any other development to provide TDM as a
  prerequisite for obtaining a land use permit.

  Applicable land use: Commercial, oﬃce, or retail uses can be
  required to provide TDM under the city’s municipal code. In
  addition, nonresidential components of SM zones, major
  educational institutions, and health institutions are required to
  provide TDM.

  Provided parking (number of parking spaces): e number of
  parking spaces a project seeks to provide does not trigger TDM
  under the municipal code.

  Location: Under the municipal code, TDM can be triggered in any
  part of the city. Seattle contains a special overlay district that has
  lower development triggers for providing TDM.
  Traﬃc impact (vehicle trips): Within the Northgate Overlay
  District, multifamily developments that are expected to generate 50
  or more trips in any one p.m. peak hour must provide TDM. In
  addition, any other substantial development that is expected to
  generate 25 or more employee or student vehicle trips in any one
  p.m. peak hour is required to provide TDM.

REQUIREMENTS

Seattle requires that projects triggering TDM obligations address them
during the following stages of development:

  Pre–site plan approval: To obtain a land use permit for the project,
  developments triggered to implement TDM must rst submit an
  acknowledgment of conditions to the city of Seattle. en, the DPD,
  in consultation with the Seattle Department of Transportation
  (SDOT) and the developer, will set a quantitative goal for the
  property. is goal must be met and maintained throughout the life
  of the project. To achieve the agreed-upon trip-reduction goal, the
  property is required to prepare a TMP outlining the measures that
  will be taken. Once the plan is nalized, it must be submitted to the
  DPD and SDOT for review and approval. Upon approval of the plan,
  the applicant is required to record the TMP with the King County
  Records and Elections Division as well as submit a copy of the
  recorded TMP to the DPD and SDOT.

  Post–site plan approval: No post–site plan approval TDM
  requirements.

  Preconstruction: No preconstruction TDM requirements.

  Preoccupancy: No preoccupancy TDM requirements.

  Postoccupancy: As tenants move into the building, the applicant or
  responsible party is required to distribute a copy of the TMP to all
  future tenants with the leasing documents and building
  management procedures. e applicant or responsible party ensures
  that all tenants are required to comply with TDM conditions.

STRATEGIES AND TDM PLAN ELEMENTS
In consultation with the city, the applicant is required to prepare a TMP
stating the goal and detailing the elements that will be implemented to
achieve the goal. e city requires the following 13 TDM strategies in all
TMPs:

  Appoint a building transportation coordinator (BTC);
  Require the BTC to attend at least one training per year;
  Produce and distribute up-to-date commuter information;
  Require all tenants to participate in the TMP;
  Conduct periodic surveys of TMP eﬀectiveness, as established by the
  DPD and SDOT;
  Submit regular reports about TMP elements as required by the city;
  Participate in a transportation management association, where
  available;
  Participate in transportation promotion and encouragement
  programs;
  Install a commuter information center in an appropriate and central
  location;
  Provide bicycle storage and amenities that meet city standards;
  Provide or require tenants to oﬀer transit pass subsidy to employees
  who work at the site;
  Provide information about ride-match opportunities; and
  Oﬀer a guaranteed ride home program.

METRICS FOR SUCCESS

e typical metric for TMPs is SOV mode split. e speci c target varies
on a case-by-case basis. TMPs for developments in SM zones are held to a
speci c 40 percent SOV mode-split goal.

ENFORCEMENT

Seattle deploys several enforcement techniques to ensure that TMP SOV
mode-split goals are eﬀectively implemented. ey occur both during the
entitlement process and postoccupancy.
  Staﬀ: e DPD and SDOT are both responsible for enforcing TDM
  under the municipal code.

  Enforcement mechanism: Seattle’s Environmental Policies and
  Procedures in Seattle Municipal Code (SMC) Chapter 25.05, which
  implement the State Environmental Protection Act, authorize the
  DPD to grant, condition, or deny land use permit applications that
  are subject to environmental review. In addition, the Seattle Land
  Use and Zoning Code (Title 23 SMC) contains requirements for
  certain projects to have a TMP. Because TDM is prompted by
  municipal code, it is enforceable by law.

  Penalties (ﬁnancial): e DPD may pursue enforcement for failure
  of compliance with a penalty of up to $250 per day. e money from
  the violation gets deposited in the city’s general fund.

  Penalties (delayed construction): Failure to implement a required
  TMP will result in delays in the entitlement process.

  Penalties (use of facilities): Developers who do not comply with
  municipal code are not allowed to continue through the entitlement
  process and will therefore not be allowed to use the facilities they are
  constructing.

MONITORING AND EVALUATION

Seattle requires a combination of surveys and typically annual reports to
monitor and evaluate performance toward the TMP’s SOV mode-split
goals.

  Survey: Typically, the city will require biennial surveys. ese
  surveys are expected to receive a response rate of at least 50 percent.
  SDOT is responsible for determining that the survey is properly
  conducted.

  Traﬃc counts: None.

  Parking use: None.

  Annual report: e applicant is required to submit a regular report
  in the timing and form stipulated by the city, typically annually.

SOAR ANALYSIS
Government perspective: Seattle staﬀ members see strengths in several
aspects of the ordinance. In particular, it allows the jurisdiction to
establish goals and strategies that can be negotiated and exible. ey
also like that the ordinance provides developers with a menu of strategies
to choose from.

e policy has gone through incremental change over time and needs to
be updated comprehensively. One of the things the staﬀ would like to see
is a comprehensive update that includes speci c goals for diﬀerent
geographic areas of the city. ey would also like to see TDM strategies
be more uid and change over time for a property. At the moment,
strategies get recorded with the property and remain static. Speci cally,
members recognize that they need a dedicated local funding source
through fees or nes to better manage monitoring and evaluation of the
TDM program. As of summer 2017, no staﬀ is dedicated to enforce the
policy’s monitoring and it is diﬃcult to keep in touch with property
contacts. e result is inconsistent monitoring of TDM properties, which
has made quantifying the eﬀectiveness of the policy diﬃcult, thus making
enforcement of penalties for noncompliance diﬃcult.

Developer perspective: Developers were highly critical of how the TDM
policy is implemented. Many thought the TDM policy is too focused on
measures rather than outcomes and the strategies lack exibility over
time, becoming antiquated. For example, many plans require buildings to
provide information racks despite the fact that most information now
gets distributed digitally or to provide carpool parking spaces despite
those spaces being ineﬀective and unused. City reviewers have authorized
some property owners to “ignore” these requirements. Unfortunately, the
only way to amend site-based TDM requirements is through the “master
use permit” process, which property owners are unwilling to go through
because it may generate additional requirements without providing
anything in return. Many of the physical measurements required, such as
bike parking, lockers, and showers, are now embedded in the code in
other locations. Furthermore, many requirements are provided regardless
of marketplace demand. Developers would prefer more exibility in the
implementation of strategies and would prefer to be held to the goal
rather than the implementation. ey suggested perhaps a “point system”
to allow them to pick and choose their preferred strategies.
