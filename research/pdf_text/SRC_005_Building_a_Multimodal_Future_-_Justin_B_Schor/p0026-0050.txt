CHAPTER 4

What Best Practices Should Be
Considered When Craing a
TDM Policy?

While chapter 3 provides an inventory of the types of TDM
policies that exist, this chapter provides a better
understanding of what works in practice from the
perspective of both the local jurisdiction and the property
owners/managers required to comply with that jurisdiction’s
TDM policy. is was achieved through a strengths,
opportunities, aspirations, and results (SOAR) analysis of
both perspectives for each of the 10 jurisdictions studied.
e perspectives of more than 40 professionals were
captured to produce best-practice recommendations
included in this chapter. ese recommendations also
discuss what should be avoided when developing a TDM
policy and why. Best practices in this chapter were used to
develop the steps for establishing a TDM policy, which are
highlighted in chapter 5. Detailed interview notes from the
SOAR interviews can be found in chapter 6.
BEST PRACTICE 1

Focus on Outcome Rather an Tactics

A common critique of TDM policies expressed by developers interviewed
was that jurisdictions overemphasize speci c TDM strategies as opposed
to outcomes. Prescriptive TDM policies, if not regularly updated, can
quickly become obsolete and require developers to implement strategies
that are no longer relevant or eﬀective. Furthermore, developers may not
fully buy into all strategies that are required of them, leading to
suboptimal implementation. Developers would prefer exibility to
implement strategies that they believe make sense for their site and
tenants. ey feel that if their developments meet the TDM goal, the
jurisdictions should not care how that goal is achieved.

Fairfax County, Virginia, focuses on achieving peak-hour trip reduction rather than speciﬁc TDM
tactics.

BEST PRACTICE 2

Establish Quantiﬁable Performance Goals

For outcome-based TDM policies to be eﬀective, they need a quanti able
metric against which performance can be measured. In addition to
determining progress toward compliance, performance goals enable
greater local support for projects because they provide communities with
clear assurances on transportation impact. From a long-term planning
perspective, performance goals provide jurisdictions with the ability to
mathematically quantify and control the permitted amount of incentive
zoning they are willing to provide to encourage developers to participate
in the TDM policy.

Mountain View, California, established a peak-period maximum for the Bayshore area of 18,900
vehicles trips during the morning peak.

BEST PRACTICE 3

Verify Quantiﬁable Performance Goals
with Monitoring and Evaluation

Quanti able performance goals are only eﬀective if they are measured.
Interviews with both developers and government employees revealed
they generally agree that monitoring and evaluation are a key piece of
ensuring TDM policy eﬀectiveness that deserves more attention.
Both developers and government employees generally agree that TDM
policies with monitoring and evaluation are much easier to justify and
defend because they provide a measurable way to correlate impact to the
policy. If the policy is tied to increased development opportunity, having
monitoring provides data that can assure citizens who have concerns with
traﬃc congestion or parking issues that the TDM policy is having the
desired eﬀect on the quality of life in that area.
Funding for monitoring and evaluation can either be done by the
property through a line item in the developer’s budget or through the
government. Speci c details for determining how to fund monitoring and
evaluation are included in step 6 of chapter 5.

Cambridge, Massachusetts, measures performance of large projects through annual surveys, traﬃc
counts, and vehicle and bike parking utilization rates.

BEST PRACTICE 4

Leverage Annual Reporting to Help TDM
Programs Evolve over Time

As noted, the most eﬀective TDM policies focus on outcome rather than
the speci c TDM strategies used. Not only does the tenant mix of a
building constantly change, but so does society and technology. ese
constant forces of change require that TDM strategies for the building
continually evolve over time. e most successful buildings are
continually doing market research through tenant surveys and
monitoring the use of TDM program elements to determine what will
work best at their site.
Jurisdictions that require annual TDM reports give properties extra
motivation to monitor and evaluate the performance of their TDM
program and ensure that it is delivering optimal services to their tenants
and results to the community. Changes presented in the annual report are
typically guided by site monitoring and evaluation.

Arlington, Virginia, uses annual letters to the county manager to understand TDM activities for the
site as well as acquire an ongoing record of those activities.

BEST PRACTICE 5

Invest Time Up Front to Establish
Eﬀective TDM Policy Administration

Although all communities explored in this research have a TDM policy,
some are more eﬀective at administering the policy than others. One of
the biggest challenges communities face in administering a TDM policy
is maintaining a contact person at all the buildings with TDM
requirements over a longer period of time. As buildings are purchased
and sold, ownership and property management contacts change, making
identi cation of the person managing TDM requirements for the site an
increasing challenge.
e rst step in overcoming this challenge is mandating that any site
required to have a TDM policy maintain a representative for the building
and inform the local jurisdiction when personnel change. e next step is
to ensure that the local jurisdiction does its part to proactively maintain
regular contact with those points of contact at least twice a year. e nal
step is to use a customer relationship management (CRM) tool to track
which developments have TDM requirements, who the site contact is,
how the site has performed over time, and when the next TDM
requirement is due. Using a CRM tool, the jurisdiction can identify and
follow up with individual sites as needed.

Seattle, Washington, buildings appoint a transportation coordinator who attends at least one training
per year.

BEST PRACTICE 6

Ensure Predictability with TDM in the
Development Process

A consistent nding among communities explored in this research is a
desire for consistency in the way that TDM policies are applied,
monitored, and enforced. When TDM policies do not clearly indicate
what is required of developments, it creates inconsistent requirements for
similar developments, makes it more diﬃcult for the local jurisdiction to
track compliance, and makes the entire development process more
unpredictable. TDM policies that are not written clearly and are diﬃcult
to understand are not eﬀectively implemented or enforced over time.
e following three key items should be considered when introducing
predictability in a TDM policy:
1. Provide details on how developers should meet the TDM policy. is
can be done by providing regularly updated supporting TDM guidelines
and an explanation about when and why to use certain TDM strategies.
2. Ensure that strategies included within the guidelines are “suggested”
strategies and not “required.” is will balance giving direction while
avoiding prescribed solutions that may not make sense for the tenant mix
of the site.
3. Provide best practice TDM plans to be used as samples for developers
in their jurisdiction.

Bloomington, Minnesota, staﬀ worked with the development community for three years to ensure
that predictable TDM requirements became a formal element of the city’s zoning ordinance in 2009.
