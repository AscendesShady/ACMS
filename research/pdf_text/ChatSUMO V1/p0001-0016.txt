1. Introduction

  Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) have emerged as a critical domain for addressing the growing challenges
of urbanization, traffic congestion, and safety (Qureshi and Abdullah, 2013). By integrating advanced sensing,
communication, and control technologies, ITS aims to enhance the efficiency and reliability of transportation
networks (Dimitrakopoulos and Demestichas, 2010). Within this ecosystem, microscopic traffic simulation serves as a
foundational tool for the evaluation and design of dynamic traffic management strategies (Zhang, Wang, Wang, Lin, Xu
and Chen, 2011). By enabling fine-grained modeling of traffic dynamics, control strategies, and behavioral interactions,
microscopic simulation supports applications ranging from infrastructure design to safety evaluation and autonomous
driving research.

  Despite their utility, constructing and modifying simulation scenarios in established platforms remains a labor-
intensive and expertise-driven process. Simulators such as VISSIM (Lownes and Machemehl, 2006), AIMSUN
(Barceló, Codina, Casas, Ferrer and García, 2002), and MITSIM (Yang and Koutsopoulos, 1996) offer powerful
capabilities but require users to master complex configuration files and proprietary scripting languages. Similarly,
while CARLA (Dosovitskiy, Ros, Codevilla, Lopez and Koltun, 2017) and SUMO provide open-source environments
for autonomous driving and urban mobility research, they demand significant technical proficiency to define network
geometry, demand patterns, and calibration parameters. This steep learning curve creates a barrier for non-expert users
and hinders rapid scenario exploration, particularly for large-scale studies requiring iterative modifications.

  The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), underpinned by the Transformer architecture (Vaswani,
Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser and Polosukhin, 2017), has revolutionized the landscape of human-
computer interaction. Frontier models such as GPT-4 (Achiam, Adler, Agarwal et al., 2023) and Llama 3 (Dubey,

  they typically operate as open-loop systems. They excel at the "one-shot" generation of a static scenario but often lack
  the mechanisms to iteratively refine that scenario based on the dynamic, emergent behavior observed during simulation
  execution.

  Recent advances in agent-oriented LLM research provide important insights into how language models can be
  integrated with external tools and execution environments. For example, reasoning–action frameworks such as ReAct
  (Yao, Zhao, Yu, Du, Shafran, Narasimhan and Cao, 2023) enable models to interleave intermediate reasoning with tool
  invocation, improving decision transparency during task execution. Tool-learning approaches, including Toolformer
  (Schick, Dwivedi-Yu, Dessì, Raileanu, Lomeli, Zettlemoyer, Cancedda and Scialom, 2023), further demonstrate that
  LLMs can autonomously select and invoke external APIs. In addition, multi-agent communication frameworks such as
  CAMEL (Li, Hammoud, Itani, Khizbullin and Ghanem, 2023) show how complex tasks may be decomposed through
  role-based interaction and cooperation.Despite these advances, existing approaches remain insufficient when directly
  applied to traffic simulation engineering. First, most agent frameworks are designed for stateless or weakly stateful
  tasks, and lack explicit mechanisms for maintaining and reasoning over persistent simulation states, intermediate
  artifacts, and execution histories. Second, tool usage in prior work is typically generic and loosely coupled, whereas
  traffic simulators require strict control over domain-specific workflows, file dependencies, and execution order. Third,
  existing agent paradigms rarely support closed-loop performance-driven refinement, in which simulation outputs are
  systematically evaluated and used to guide subsequent scenario modification. As a result, these methods are difficult to
  deploy in realistic simulation pipelines that demand reproducibility, extensibility, and fine-grained engineering control.

  To address these limitations, this paper introduces ChatSUMO-Agent, an agent-based framework that tightly
  couples an LLM with the SUMO simulation engine through a structured tool-control interface. Figure 1 illustrates
  the workflow of the proposed ChatSUMO-Agent during real-time interaction with users. Unlike prior approaches,
  ChatSUMO-Agent operates as a closed-loop decision-making system. The agent interprets high-level user goals, selects
  and executes domain-specific tools for network construction, route generation, and simulation execution, evaluates
  resulting performance metrics, and iteratively refines the scenario based on observed outcomes. By maintaining an
  explicit internal representation of simulation states and action histories, the agent is able to reason over past decisions
  and adapt subsequent actions accordingly. This design enables flexible interaction paradigms, allowing users to specify
  objectives in natural language while preserving transparency and reproducibility through structured intermediate
  artifacts. Furthermore, utilizing an agent-based architecture significantly enhances the extensibility of the system.
  Future functionalities, such as new traffic control strategies or sensor simulations, can be seamlessly integrated as
  additional tools for the agent to wield, without requiring fundamental restructuring of the core framework.

  The contribution of the paper is: (1) We propose ChatSUMO-Agent, a generalizable LLM-agent architecture
  for microscopic traffic simulation that supports planning, execution, and iterative refinement. By embedding traffic
  modeling heuristics within its tool invocation logic, the agent ensures that generated scenarios adhere to fundamental
  traffic principles while automating complex configuration tasks. This allows for a robust transition from open-loop text
  generation to closed-loop simulation engineering. (2) We demonstrate the effectiveness of ChatSUMO-Agent across a
  range of representative simulation tasks. Our experiments show that the framework achieves substantial reductions in
  manual effort while maintaining behavioral plausibility and alignment with user objectives. Through these evaluations,
  we validate the agent’s capability to autonomously handle end-to-end scenario construction and refinement.

2. Related Work

  The development of an AI agent for direct SUMO simulation generation lies at the intersection of Large Language
Models (LLMs), agentic AI, and intelligent transportation systems (ITS). This section reviews the literature across
these three domains.

2.1. LLMs in Transportation Simulation
  Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 2023) and Llama 3

(Dubey et al., 2024), have opened new possibilities for overcoming technical barriers in traffic simulation. Traditionally,
platforms like Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO) required expert knowledge in XML configuration, Python
scripting (TraCI), and network demand modeling (Li, Liu, Shen, Huang and Wang, 2024b).

  Early integrations of LLMs in this field focused on natural language interaction and data analysis. For instance,
TrafficGPT demonstrated that LLMs could act as a bridge between users and traffic foundation models, enabling
conversational traffic analysis and decision support (Zhang et al., 2024b). This approach was expanded by ChatSUMO,
which enabled end-to-end natural language control over simulation workflows (Li et al., 2024a).

  Recent research has shifted toward more sophisticated generation and control mechanisms. ChatSim introduced a
collaborative LLM-agent framework to decompose complex simulation demands into specialized editing tasks, such
as background rendering and view adjustment (Wei et al., 2024). Similarly, ChatScene leverages LLMs to generate
safety-critical scenarios by transforming textual descriptions into domain-specific languages (DSL) for simulators like
CARLA (Zhang et al., 2024a). To ensure physical feasibility and controllability, frameworks such as CTG++ employ
scene-level conditional diffusion models guided by LLM-generated loss functions (Zhong, Rempe, Chen et al., 2023).
Further enhancing controllability, Liu, Li, Wang et al. (2025b) proposed a hierarchical reasoning and refinement module
that utilizes Frenet-frame-based cost functions to improve the agent’s grasp of spatial relationships.

  2.2. AI Agent
  AI agents, often referred to as agentic AI, denote autonomous computational systems capable of pursuing complex

  and open-ended objectives under limited human supervision. Unlike conventional task-oriented models, agent-based
  systems emphasize goal-driven behavior, adaptive decision-making, and interaction with dynamic environments,
  frequently through multi-agent collaboration and on-the-fly task decomposition (Sapkota, Roumeliotis and Karkee,
  2026). Core properties commonly attributed to modern AI agents include autonomy, proactivity, and the ability to
  learn or adapt based on feedback (Hosseini and Seilani, 2025). These characteristics form the conceptual foundation of
  the proposed system, which aims to autonomously engineer simulation workflows rather than merely parameterizing
  predefined configurations.

  Recent research has explored mechanisms for improving agent adaptability and coordination beyond static role
  assignments. For instance, adaptive agent generation frameworks such as AutoAgents introduce mechanisms for
  dynamically creating and orchestrating specialized agents (e.g., planning or observation roles) in response to task
  requirements, enabling more flexible problem-solving compared to fixed multi-agent architectures (Chen, Dong, Shu
  et al., 2024). Complementary efforts in autonomous decision-making, exemplified by systems such as Auto-GPT,
  demonstrate how agents can decompose high-level objectives into executable sub-tasks and iteratively interact with
  external tools in online environments (Yang, Yue and He, 2023a). Such systems often incorporate auxiliary learning
  or feedback mechanisms to improve decision quality and robustness during long-horizon execution.

  Effective interaction with complex external systems further necessitates reliable and extensible tool-use capa-
  bilities. Recent work on self-instructed tool learning, such as GPT4Tools, shows that large language models can
  acquire tool-usage proficiency through self-generated instruction data and parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods,
  including Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), thereby reducing reliance on large-scale human-annotated demonstrations
  (Yang, Song, Li et al., 2023b). As agent autonomy increases, concerns related to governance, safety, and alignment
  become increasingly prominent. Prior studies and surveys emphasize the importance of managing risks specific to
  agentic workflows, including error propagation across multi-step plans and unintended consequences arising from
  autonomous code execution or tool invocation (Shavit, Agarwal, Adler et al., 2023).

  utilized an Agent-AI approach for the Discrete Network Design Problem (DNDP), demonstrating how agents can

  Miranda-Moreno and Korp (2025) introduced modular AI agents to conduct transportation surveys, significantly
  advancing engagement rates and cost efficiency compared to human-led methods.

  user intents and generates executable simulation scenarios through a goal-oriented workflow. In a complementary

3. Methodology

3.1. Overall Framework
  ChatSUMO-Agent is implemented as a closed-loop, agent-oriented execution framework that operationalizes traffic

simulation as a structured decision process rather than a direct text generation task (Figure 2). The system formalizes
simulation engineering as an iterative sequence of goal grounding, action execution, state observation, and adaptive
replanning. This formulation enables systematic interaction with the SUMO simulator while preserving explicit control
over intermediate artifacts, execution order, and feedback signals.

  At the core of the framework is an LLM-based planning module that functions as a state-aware controller. Instead of
producing simulation files directly, the planner operates over a structured internal state representation that encodes user
objectives, execution constraints, action histories, and observed simulation outcomes. Based on this state, the planner
performs hierarchical task decomposition and selects domain-specific actions under feasibility constraints. This design
allows planning decisions to be conditioned on both prior actions and real-time simulator feedback, supporting iterative
refinement and error-aware reasoning.

  All interactions with the simulation environment are mediated through a dedicated tool execution layer that
exposes a predefined set of domain-specific operations, including network construction, route generation, simulation
execution, and traffic control modification. These tools serve as grounded action primitives that translate high-level
planning decisions into executable simulator commands. A state and memory manager continuously aggregates tool

3.2. Agent State Representation
  To support closed-loop reasoning, traceability, and reproducibility, ChatSUMO-Agent maintains an explicit and

persistent agent state throughout the simulation workflow. Rather than treating intermediate results as ephemeral text
outputs, the system encodes all relevant information in a structured state object that is incrementally updated after each
execution step. At iteration 𝑡, the agent state 𝑆𝑡 can be conceptually represented as:

𝑆𝑡 = ⟨𝐺, 𝑡, 𝑡, 𝑡⟩,  (1)

where 𝐺 denotes the user-specified simulation goal, 𝑡 represents the set of concrete simulation artifacts, 𝑡 stores
the execution history, and 𝑡 captures auxiliary contextual and control information.

  Simulation artifacts 𝑡 are treated as first-class state variables and are explicitly materialized as files within a
persistent workspace. These artifacts include, but are not limited to, the network definition, route files, simulation
configuration files, and additional auxiliary inputs required by SUMO. By preserving artifacts as structured files
rather than implicit textual descriptions, the framework enables direct inspection, reuse across iterations, and external
validation independent of the agent’s internal reasoning process.

  The execution history 𝑡 is recorded as an ordered sequence of step-level records, each capturing the agent’s
selected action, the corresponding parameters, observed execution outcomes, and associated timestamps. When
available, each step may also store key performance indicators (KPIs), such as mean speed, delay, or throughput,
which summarize simulation outcomes relevant to the optimization objective. This fine-grained action–observation
trace allows the agent to reason over past decisions, maintain consistency across successive modifications, and avoid
redundant or contradictory operations during multi-step workflows.

  In addition to artifacts and history, the agent state includes contextual variables 𝑡 that encode task-specific
constraints, execution flags, and intermediate control signals, such as pending modification status or the most recent
modification target. The latest observed KPIs are cached within the state to support performance-aware replanning.
Together, these components form a state representation that tightly couples planning, execution, and evaluation,
enabling iterative refinement, error recovery, and reproducible simulation engineering.

3.3. LLM-Based Planning and Decision Logic
  In ChatSUMO-Agent, the large language model (LLM) functions as a state-aware planning and decision-making

controller that determines the next executable operation based on the current agent state. Given the state 𝑆𝑡, the planner
does not generate simulation artifacts directly. Instead, it produces a single, structured tool-invocation decision that
specifies (i) the selected action, (ii) the corresponding parameters, and (iii) a concise rationale. All planner outputs
are constrained to a predefined JSON schema, ensuring syntactic validity, bounded action space, and deterministic
downstream execution.

  The planning process is explicitly conditioned on a summarized representation of the agent state, which includes
the user-defined goal, the availability of existing simulation artifacts, and the complete execution history with observed
outcomes and performance indicators. This state summary is injected into the LLM prompt together with a system-
level specification that encodes domain rules, tool contracts, execution ordering constraints, and traffic-modeling
conventions. As a result, planning decisions are guided not only by high-level intent but also by concrete execution
feasibility, artifact readiness, and prior simulation results.

  Decision-making follows a state-driven progression that mirrors the lifecycle of traffic simulation engineering.
When no network artifact exists, the planner prioritizes network construction. When network artifacts are available but
demand or configuration files are missing, route generation is selected. Once a valid simulation setup is established,
the planner schedules simulation execution followed by performance analysis. If the user explicitly requests scenario
modification or if execution errors are detected, the planner instead selects a modification operation and defers
simulation execution until corrective actions are applied. This logic ensures that planning decisions remain consistent
with both user intent and simulator constraints.

  Parameter specification is performed jointly with action selection. The planner proposes minimal, action-specific
parameters that conform to tool schemas and domain constraints, such as deterministic random seeds for reproducibil-
ity, valid road identifiers derived from map data, and physically meaningful traffic control parameters. Planning is
therefore not a free-form text generation process, but a constrained decision step that produces grounded, executable
commands.

  To improve robustness, the planner incorporates multiple layers of validation and correction. Invalid or unsupported
actions are automatically mapped to safe alternatives based on the current artifact state, and extraneous parameters
are filtered to enforce schema compliance. In the event that the LLM fails to produce a valid decision, a rule-based
fallback planner guarantees forward progress by selecting a conservative next action. Together, these mechanisms
ensure reliable execution while preserving the flexibility of LLM-based reasoning.

  Crucially, planning in ChatSUMO-Agent is fully state-aware and performance-sensitive. Observed simulation
metrics, such as speed or delay, are stored in the agent state and implicitly influence subsequent decisions, enabling
adaptive behaviors such as iterative signal timing refinement, demand adjustment, or targeted network modification.
By embedding the LLM within a constrained, state-driven decision loop, the framework transforms language models
from passive generators into reliable planners suitable for closed-loop traffic simulation engineering.

  ChatSUMO-Agent bridges high-level planning and simulator execution through a provider-based Multi-Tool

Control Protocol (MCP), in which all interactions with SUMO are mediated by a finite set of domain-specific providers.
Each provider encapsulates a coherent class of simulation operations and exposes a collection of executable functions

  Simulation construction is handled by the SUMO Builder Provider, which is responsible for generating network-
level artifacts and assembling runnable simulation configurations. This provider supports both real-world network
extraction from OpenStreetMap data and synthetic network construction for controlled experiments, and produces
standardized SUMO configuration files that define simulation scope and reporting behavior. By centralizing network
and configuration generation within a dedicated provider, the framework ensures that subsequent simulation stages
operate on consistent and well-defined artifacts.

  Traffic demand specification is delegated to the Traffic Generator Provider, which implements route and flow
generation mechanisms used to populate the network with vehicular traffic. These functions enable both stochastic route
generation and deterministic origin–destination flow construction, allowing the agent to flexibly represent background
traffic or targeted demand patterns. Because demand generation is isolated within a single provider, modifications to
traffic intensity or routing logic can be applied independently of network structure.

  Execution of simulations is performed exclusively through the SUMO Runner Provider. This provider manages the
invocation of SUMO runs, controls execution parameters, and organizes simulation outputs and logs. By restricting
simulation execution to a single provider, MCP enforces strict ordering constraints and prevents uncontrolled or
redundant simulator calls, which is critical for reproducibility and long-horizon agent workflows.

  Scenario adaptation and in-place modification are implemented through the SUMO Modifier Provider. This
provider exposes functions that directly alter existing simulation artifacts, such as traffic signal logic, road availability,
or demand distributions. In particular, it supports both user-defined signal programs and automated signal optimization
procedures that generate and apply updated control plans based on observed traffic conditions. Because modifications
are applied to persistent artifacts stored in the agent workspace, their effects are explicitly tracked and can be
incrementally refined across iterations.

  Performance observation and feedback are handled by the Traffic Metrics Provider, which extracts structured
indicators from simulation outputs. Rather than returning raw logs, this provider computes task-relevant summaries
such as mean speed, travel time statistics, traffic density, and aggregate emissions. These metrics are normalized
into structured observations and injected into the agent state, enabling subsequent planning decisions to condition
on quantitative performance signals rather than unstructured text.

  Across all providers, MCP enforces schema-constrained execution and state-aware feedback. Each provider
function is invoked through a validated interface that constrains parameter types and permissible operations, while
execution outputs are systematically mapped back into the agent’s internal state. Because providers operate on shared
artifacts within a persistent workspace, complex workflows can be composed across multiple iterations, such as
repeated signal optimization, demand adjustment, or network stress testing. This provider-based abstraction allows
ChatSUMO-Agent to orchestrate heterogeneous simulation operations in a modular, extensible, and reproducible
manner, while preserving strict control over execution semantics.

3.5. Failure Recovery and Self-Healing

  Failure recovery is evaluated using a controlled failure injection protocol designed to assess the robustness of
closed-loop agent execution. For each simulation scenario, an explicit fault is deliberately introduced at a designated
time step 𝑡0, such as referencing an invalid network element or specifying an infeasible demand configuration. After
failure injection, ChatSUMO-Agent continues operating under the state–action–observation loop without external
intervention. Recovery is deemed successful if the agent restores the simulation to a runnable state and produces
valid, non-empty outputs—including KPI reports and simulator-generated artifacts—within a predefined maximum of
𝐾 recovery iterations.

  Formally, let 𝑡0 denote the step at which a failure is first observed. The recovery process can be expressed as a
sequence of replanning and execution steps:

𝑆𝑡0 → Plan𝑡0 → Execute𝑡0 → Fail𝑡0 → ⋯ → 𝑆𝑡∗ → Run𝑡∗ → Observe𝑡∗ ,  (2)

where 𝑡∗ represents the earliest time step at which execution succeeds and the system returns valid simulation outputs.
This formulation highlights that recovery is not treated as a special-case procedure, but as a continuation of the standard
closed-loop decision process.

steps are executed through MCP-managed tools, each corrective action is explicit, auditable, and reproducible. This
disciplined recovery strategy allows the agent to iteratively restore feasibility while preserving a complete trace of the
decision and execution process.

3.6. Implementation Details
  ChatSUMO-Agent is implemented using a modular Python-based architecture, with SUMO serving as the

microscopic simulation backend. The LLM communicates with the tool layer via structured prompts and JSON-
formatted action schemas. Simulation outputs are parsed programmatically to extract key indicators, which are
summarized and fed back to the agent as observations.To ensure reproducibility, all intermediate artifacts, tool
invocations, and simulation results are logged and preserved. This design allows experiments to be replayed, audited,
or extended without reliance on the LLM’s internal state alone, supporting transparent evaluation and systematic
comparison across scenarios.

4. Experiment Results

4.1. Experimental Setup
  The experimental evaluation assesses whether ChatSUMO-Agent can (i) autonomously generate valid and behav-

iorally plausible SUMO simulations from natural-language inputs, (ii) support closed-loop modification and analysis,
and (iii) significantly reduce human effort compared with conventional simulation workflows. The experiments
emphasize workflow effectiveness, time efficiency, and scenario fidelity rather than raw model accuracy.

  All experiments were conducted using SUMO as the microscopic simulation backend. ChatSUMO-Agent orches-
trates simulation tasks through a modular tool suite, including network generation, route construction, simulation
execution, and analysis. Both real-world map–based scenarios and synthetic modifications are considered. Performance
metrics are extracted directly from SUMO outputs, including average speed, travel time, traffic density, and emissions
indicators.

4.4. Failure Recovery and Robustness Evaluation
  This experiment evaluates the robustness of ChatSUMO-Agent under common failure modes encountered in urban

traffic simulation workflows. In practical simulation settings, invalid configurations such as missing network elements,
disconnected origin–destination pairs, or gridlock-inducing demand frequently lead to execution failures that require
manual diagnosis and correction. In contrast, ChatSUMO-Agent is designed to autonomously detect execution failures
through structured tool feedback, reason about likely causes, and iteratively apply corrective actions until a runnable
and analyzable simulation state is restored. We refer to this capability as failure recovery or self-healing.

  To assess robustness across problem scales, we evaluate ChatSUMO-Agent on small, medium, and large network
instances under three representative failure modes: EDGE_NF (referenced edge not found in the network), NO_PATH (no
feasible route between origin and destination), and GRIDLOCK (simulation deadlock caused by excessive or conflicting
demand). For each configuration, failures are intentionally injected, and the agent is allowed to iteratively modify the
simulation until a valid execution is achieved or a predefined iteration limit is reached.

Evaluation Metrics. Failure recovery performance is evaluated using three complementary metrics:

  • Recovery success rate, defined as the fraction of injected failures that are successfully resolved within a bounded
  number of iterations;

  • Recovery iterations, measured as the number of additional tool invocations required to reach the first successful
  simulation run after failure;

  • Time-to-recovery (TTR), defined as the elapsed wall-clock time between the first failed execution and the first
  successful simulation run.

  Table 2 summarizes recovery performance across failure modes and network scales. Overall, ChatSUMO-Agent
achieves high recovery success rates across all scenarios, exceeding 0.9 for EDGE_NF failures and remaining above
0.78 even for the most challenging GRIDLOCK cases in large networks. As expected, both recovery iterations and time-
to-recovery increase with network scale and failure complexity. Simple configuration errors such as missing edges
are typically resolved within a small number of iterations, whereas routing infeasibility and gridlock scenarios require
more extensive reasoning and validation, resulting in additional recovery steps and longer recovery times.

  Despite these challenges, ChatSUMO-Agent consistently restores a valid simulation state without human inter-
vention. Notably, the increase in time-to-recovery is primarily attributable to additional simulation and validation
steps rather than repeated human input. These results demonstrate that the proposed self-healing mechanism remains
effective across network scales and failure types, enabling robust and autonomous operation in realistic, failure-prone
traffic simulation environments.

  Across all injected failure cases, ChatSUMO-Agent restores a runnable and analyzable simulation state within a
small number of iterations. Notably, recovery gains arise from improved coordination and diagnosis rather than faster
simulation runtime. By externalizing failures as structured observations and enabling tool-mediated corrective actions,

4.5. Time-to-Insight Efficiency
  This experiment evaluates the efficiency benefits of ChatSUMO-Agent by comparing time-to-insight against a

conventional semi-manual ChatSUMO workflow across multiple urban networks. Time-to-insight is defined as the
total elapsed time required to progress from an initial user intent to actionable simulation results suitable for analysis.
Crucially, this metric accounts for both system execution time and human interaction time, thereby reflecting the
practical cost of simulation-driven experimentation rather than raw computational performance alone.

  Formally, time-to-insight is defined as

where 𝑁 denotes the number of interaction steps, 𝑇compute (𝑖) represents system execution time, including network
construction, demand generation, simulation execution, and analysis, and 𝑇human (𝑖) captures the time spent on manual
input, configuration, and intervention.

  Figure 5 compares time-to-insight for the baseline simulation workflow and for simulations involving edge removal
across three study areas. Under the conventional ChatSUMO workflow, the simulation process requires multiple
interaction turns to sequentially configure the network, specify traffic demand, execute simulations, and interpret
results. While individual computational stages remain relatively short, human interaction time dominates the overall
workflow, particularly in scenarios that require iterative modification such as edge removal. As a result, time-to-insight
increases substantially as task complexity grows.

  In contrast, ChatSUMO-Agent executes the entire workflow autonomously after receiving a single high-level
instruction. Network construction, demand specification, simulation execution, and analysis are planned and invoked
internally based on the agent’s evolving state representation, without intermediate human intervention. Across all study
areas, system computation time remains comparable to the conventional workflow, whereas human interaction time is
effectively eliminated, leading to a substantial reduction in total time-to-insight.

  Notably, the observed efficiency gains are consistent across both baseline simulations and modification-intensive
scenarios. This indicates that the improvement does not arise from faster simulation execution, but from the elimination
of manual coordination between steps. By operating as a closed-loop autonomous system capable of planning,
execution, and evaluation, ChatSUMO-Agent enables rapid iteration and scalable exploratory experimentation that
would be impractical under traditional semi-manual simulation workflows.
