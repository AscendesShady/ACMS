  ways of using interoperability for improving freight transport. By shifting
  freight trains from conventional to high speed lines in mountainous areas
  they will have to ascend less, they will have to travel shorter distances and
  waste less energy in overcoming the resistance of curves. As a result, they
  will save energy, take less time to cover the same distance and possibly
  allow the operator to offer services which might not be possible today,
  when freight trains circulate exclusively by conventional lines.

  However, there exists a need for quantifying the effects of all the po-
  tential measures which will derive from European policy, so that technical
  criteria are made available for designing lower-level policies. The task of
  correlating ﬁgures to this kind of actions is arguably difﬁcult. Any generic
  measure must, in the end, be deployed over a speciﬁc context whose par-
  ticularities might invalidate it partially or completely. On the other hand,
  any measure must be studied in generic terms so that it may be applicable
  to a wide range of scenarios. As a result, a study capable of offering repre-
  sentative conclusions to a variety of speciﬁc scenarios of application must
  combine a sufﬁcient level of generality with a certain degree of accuracy.

  We shall attempt here to achieve a compromise between both points.
  For this, we shall study the problem of freight transportation across the
  Guadarrama mountain range in the centre of Spain, NW of Madrid. This
  pass divides the Northern from the Southern Plains. We shall consider the
  real infrastructure but ideal trains, one representative of a light train and
  one of a dense one. There are four railway lines which cross the range,
  which we shall denote A, B, C and D, naming them from south to north.
  The two ﬁrst were built in the nineteenth century, line C is a high-speed
  line opened in 2007 and line D is a mid-twentieth century infrastructure.
  We shall simulate both trains, always the same trains, running on each
  line on both directions, travelling fully loaded and empty. From this sim-
  ulation we shall obtain consumption data with and without regenerative
  braking, which we shall analyze for obtaining further conclusions.

  In essence, the methodology that has been employed is an evolution of the
  disaggregated methodology described in [8]. Basically, it consists in calculating
  the integral of the motor force made by the locomotive along the longitudinal
  section of the line.

  There are a number of forces actuating on the train at a certain instant: motor
  force, gravity, rolling resistance, internal friction, etc. (see ﬁgure 4). The motor
  force must be equal to the rest of forces together to remain at constant speed.
  The basic equilibrium can be expressed:

  FM = Fg + FR + FC
  Where F M is the motor force, F g is the force of gravity, F C is the resistance
  due to curves. When accelerating, the expression becomes an inequality: F M ≥
  F g + F R + F C . In convenient units, the terms can be written:

  F g = M · g · is
  F R = M · (a + bV + cV 2)

  R

  Gravity is a function which depends on the mass of the train, M , and the
  inclination of the gradient, is, expressed as a ratio between ascended and trav-
  elled distance (normal units are mm/m or m/km). F R is expressed as a func-
  tion of three terms which stand for internally generated resistance, vehicle-track
  rolling resistance and aerodynamic resistance. In modern literature the formu-
  lation might change slightly: F R = M · a + BV + CV 2. F C represents the
  resistance put by curves, where K is a coefﬁcient which depends on the gauge
  of the line, and R is the radius of the curve. It shows that the resistance is lower
  the larger the radius. For counting the resistance put to the train by tunnels, an
  extra term should be added to the balance.

  In strict terms, the work done by the motor force when travelling from point 1
  to point 2 would be:

  2

  W M = F M · dl

  1

  Where dl stands for an inﬁnitely short stretch of the line. The result of this inte-
  gral, however, would be of little use, for it is the mathematical balance between
  the energy which is imported for climbing mountains and maintaining speed,
  and the energy which could be returned by the train when running downslope.
  This balance, however, does not happen in reality, because the excess of energy
  when travelling downslope is wasted, or only partially recovered with regener-
  ative braking.

  It must be distinguished whether F M is positive or negative at each instant.
  Positive means that the train is consuming energy, negative that it is braking.
  So, for calculating consumption along the line, the integral should only count
  F M whenever it is positive.

  Reciprocally, if the integral counted only negative values of, F M , the result
  would be the energy dissipated by braking. In fact, it is used for estimating
  how much energy might be returned to the network in the case of regenerative
  braking.

  and closed curves, heavy trains would have a limitation of approximately 84
  km/h, light trains of approximately 96 km/h loaded. Empty trains could reach
  approximately 100 km/h in every case.

  The maximum tractive effort and the maximum power of the locomotive
  also limit the maximum acceleration that the train may achieve when passing
  from a lower speed limit to a higher one. We have taken these limitations into
  account in the study. This makes that the train may not always reach the speed
  limit, because it may not have time to accelerate before having to break again.

  4.3. Assumptions

  The trafﬁc of freight trains in reality is very much conditioned by the parameters
  of line exploitation. As the passenger trains have priority over freight, these last
  may have to stop many times to give way, depending on the volume of trafﬁc
  and the frequency of passenger trains. Freight trains may also be stopped and

Transport Policy, Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated, 2011. ProQuest Ebook Central,

  recomposed on the way, so that the train arriving to a destination is different
  from the one which parted from a certain origin. These are only some of the
  circumstances which affect freight trains, and which make it very difﬁcult to
  simulate real trafﬁc.

  Thus, in order to have meaningful criteria, studies must abstract these cir-
  cumstantial aspects so as to isolate the desired effect as much as possible. In the
  case of this study, the basic simpliﬁcations are:

  Trains travel without stopping: The trains are assumed to run alone on the
  lines, subject only to their power limitations and those of the infrastruc-
  ture.

  The driving maximizes speed: The behaviour of drivers in reality may not ad-
  just to the maximum possible speed at every moment. It may also depend
  on the day or on other factors. Here, speed follows the criteria summa-
  rized in section 4.2..

  Therefore, to evaluate the real effect of running trains on high speed lines,
  many other aspects must be taken into account apart from the estimates of this
  study. Some of them will be commented in sections 5. and 6..

  The possibility of feeding energy back to the network while braking is one
  of the measures for reducing net consumption that are being currently studied
  for deploying them at a large scale. Before quantifying their possible effect,
  some aspects must be commented regarding to implementing them in practice.
  The beneﬁt of regenerative braking in direct current lines such as A and B is
  very limited, because the energy that a braking train returns must be consumed
  by another running simultaneously on the same line; it cannot be returned to
  the main network. Thus, there would exist the problem of synchronizing the
  excess of energy of some trains with the demand of others, and deviations from
  the schedule may alter the actual balance signiﬁcantly. Alternating current lines
  do not have this problem, for they do admit returning the braking energy to the
  general network. Assuming lines A and B could admit regeneration, the savings
  in energy consumption by circulating by line C would range from 10 to 35%.

  A typical value for the CO2 emission factor in Spain is 0.33 kg/kWh. The
  proportional savings in emissions follow those of energy consumption men-
  tioned above. Currently, the major part of freight transportation between Madrid
  and Valladolid follows line A, with no regeneration. Let us consider the case of
  less consumption for a loaded train, the dense train in down direction, 4399
  kWh. This would correspond to emitting approximately 1450 kg of CO 2. Cir-
  culating the train by line C would emit approximately 180 kg less. However,
  we should also consider the possibility of regenerating in line C while main-
  taining A as it is, because it would be technically more immediate (the main
  requirement would be to use locomotive Bombardier Traxx 140AC instead of

  5.2. Study of the Mountain Pass

Copyright © 2011. Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated. All rights reserved.  The lines considered for the study, A, B, C and D, include stretches at each
  side of the mountain pass which, although not ﬂat, could appear in regions with
  low or medium mountains, for which the measure studied here might be of less
  signiﬁcance. They have been included because it is expectable that they will
  necessarily exist for connecting high speed lines with conventional ones, given
  that conventional and high speed lines may become wide apart in mountainous
  regions, and nearer when apart.

  However, the effect of these stretches on the total consumption results might
  obscure the behaviour of the lines through the actual mountain pass. In order
  to explore this, we have considered the part of the lines included between the
  ﬁrst time they rise above 900 masl and the last time they descend below. The
  resulting sections are shown in ﬁgure 5, and they isolate the proper mountain
  pass from the rest of the lines. At ﬁrst sight, it can be observed that lines A and
  D were built minimizing gradients while B and C have steeper slopes. Hence,
  we may also observe that A and D are signiﬁcantly larger than B and C.

  These differences show on the results of table 4. We may observe that the
  consumption in lines B and C is considerably lower than in the other two, from
  which we may deduce that the main factor determining consumption is distance.
  This pattern holds between A and D; D being the largest makes it also the most
  consuming.

  Explaining the differences in consumption between B and C is not straight-
  forward. In many cases they fall between an interval of ±5%, which may easily
  fall within the margin of error of the estimation. There are some points of in-
  terest, however, that can be explained. For it, it is necessary to introduce the
  concept of equilibrium gradient .

  Apart from the motor force, there are basically two forces actuating on the
  train when travelling downslope. Pulling from the train downwards there is
  gravity, while pulling upward is aerodynamic resistance and inner friction. If
  the gradient is sufﬁcient, gravity will be greater than the other force, and the
  train will accelerate forward. If it is not, the train will decelerate. When the
  driver pretends to maintain the train at constant speed, he will have to brake in
  the ﬁrst case or to press the accelerator in the second. Given a certain speed
  target, there exists one gradient on which the driver would not need either to
  brake or to accelerate, so that the train would remain at constant speed by itself.
  This is the equilibrium gradient .

  Returning to lines B and C, if we examine the sections carefully, we shall
  ﬁnd that they differ in the evolution of the gradients. Line B has many long
  stretches with gradients above 15 mm/m, although they seldom pass 17 mm/m.
  On the other hand, line C has a few long stretches with gradients close to 20
  mm/m, and the rest near or below 10 mm/m. This means that line B is very
  steep all the way, while C is moderately steep most of the way, but steeper than
  B at some parts.

  Consumption by line B is lower than by C only in the cases of light or empty
  trains, higher in the case of loaded dense ones. The equilibrium gradients for

Transport Policy, Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated, 2011. ProQuest Ebook Central,
  180  Ignacio Lo´pez, Javier Rodr´ıguez, Alberto Garc´ıa et al.

  these last, due to their greater mass, will be low, while the gradients for the other
  trains will be higher; more, the lighter the train. In the case of the heavy trains,
  all the gradients in both lines is enough for the train to travel by gravity, having
  to break not to pass the speed limits. The equilibrium gradient for the lighter
  ones, however, falls between the ones of B and the softer ones of C. They will
  travel by gravity most of the time in B, while only in the very steep stretches of
  C, requiring energy in the rest. We see that the results in table 4 show exactly
  this.

  This study of the mountain pass shows that the fundamental factor for en-
  ergy consumption in mountainous areas is the distance travelled. Between two
  similarly long lines, it is difﬁcult to decide which would be the most econom-
  ical, because it would depend on the exact topology of the lines and on the
  precise composition of the trains. The differences might fall within the natural
  variability of train consumption due to driver, day of the week, season, etc.

Copyright © 2011. Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated. All rights reserved.  6. Conclusion

  In terms of the framework of European transport policy, the measure of using
  high-speed infrastructures for freight transport in mountainous regions must be
  valued according to its contribution to the three strategies for revitalising rail-
  ways:

  1. Integrating rail transport into the internal market

  2. Making optimum use of the infrastructure

  3. Modernisation of services

  The contribution of the measure to optimizing the use of the infrastructure
  is straightforward. Let us consider the case of this study, for instance. Approx-
  imately 130 freight trains travel weekly by line A, and their average travelling
  time between Madrid and Valladolid is above 5 hours, maily due to having to
  stop to give way to passenger trains. By shifting freight trafﬁc to line C, this
  capacity would be freed for conventional passenger trains. Now, turning to line
  C, we might observe that this stretch is one of the backbones of the Spanish high
  speed network, and that, consequently, the number of passenger trains will, ex-
  pectably, be very high. The two tracks coming out of Madrid divide in eight
  lines with 16 tracks further on, but the capacity for this measure is limited by

Transport Policy, Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated, 2011. ProQuest Ebook Central,
  Policies of Railway Interoperability  181

Copyright © 2011. Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated. All rights reserved.  the common stretch right out of Madrid, the one considered in this study. Thus,
  simultaneous trafﬁc of passengers and freight in this line could barely exist dur-
  ing the day.

  However, it would be possible for freight trains to travel by night. There
  is no passenger trafﬁc, and they could use the infrastructure virtually on their
  own. This would mean travelling a shorter distance than by line A but, most
  importantly, not having to stop.

  A ﬁrst consequence of the measure would be the simpliﬁcation of trafﬁc
  management on this bottleneck. One of the problems identiﬁed in the White
  Paper is that freight trains get delayed not only by a great number of planned
  stops for passenger trains, but also by stops which are overcome at short notice
  and which penalize reliability of service. A second effect of the measure would
  be to bring the average speed nearer to the values calculated in this study (which
  assumed no stops at all and ideal driving), approximately 90 km/h against the
  current 48 km/h.

  Further consequences derive from these, mainly regarding exploitation
  costs. They can be classiﬁed in two categories: those depending on travelling
  times and those on distance.

  Energy and maintenance costs depend basically on distance. On the other
  hand, the cost of the locomotives, the drivers and the wagons is proportional to
  the travelling time, because the shorter the time, the more available they become
  for other trips. As an example, [9] analyzes the mountain pass of Pajares in the
  north of Spain. The conventional line has an exploitation cost of 10.94 euro/km,
  while the cost of the high speed line is 12.48 euro/km. The difference in the
  length of the lines compensates for this, so that the total cost of the conventional
  line adds up to 909 euro and the high speed line to 620 euro, 31% less.

  Regarding how the measure would contribute to integrating rail transport
  into the internal market and how it would enable a modernisation of services,
  we must bear in mind that rail, although best suited in principle for certain
  types of transport, does not represent an attractive alternative to the customer
  in practice. The services that the customer needs are either unavailable or they
  lack reliability, punctuality or take longer times than they do by road or air.

  The White Paper and the subsequent documents mention the lack of reli-
  ability and punctuality in rail freight trafﬁc as one of the causes of mismatch
  between the market and the services offered by rail. In order to force their im-
  provement, a full scheme for managing delays is being developed, from detec-
  tion of delays and prevention of cascade delays to determining responsibilities.

  182  Ignacio Lo´pez, Javier Rodr´ıguez, Alberto Garc´ıa et al.

  Although the causes for delays in freight trafﬁc are complex, and not always
  liable to the rail operator, a signiﬁcant part is due to path allocation to passenger
  trains. If delays in freight trafﬁc are to become a priority, freight trafﬁc should
  be given a way to react to the nature and contingencies of passenger trafﬁc,
  such as line sharing. We may see that signiﬁcantly higher average speeds and
  less perturbances due to passenger trafﬁc could enable offering a wider range of
  services, for example transportation of perishable goods, and, more important
  still, improving existing ones.

  Returning to European policy, as it was mentioned in section 1., the three
  basic strategies of European policy just commented are reﬂected on a series of
  more speciﬁc targets by 2020:

  • For rail to increase its market share of (. . . ) goods trafﬁc from 8% to 15%,
  • A trebling of manpower productivity on the railways,
  • A 50% gain in energy efﬁciency,
  • A 50% reduction in emissions of pollutants,
  • An increase in infrastructure capacity commensurate with trafﬁc targets.

Copyright © 2011. Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated. All rights reserved.  In order to quantify the effect of shifting freight to high speed lines, a full de-
  ployment plan should be designed, specifying affected lines, volume of freight
  and trains shifted, regulating concurrence with passenger trafﬁc, etc. This study
  allows, however, to valuate the technical feasibility of the measure qualitatively.

  We have already seen how it could help modernising services and integrat-
  ing rail into the market, and brieﬂy commented on the potential gain in produc-
  tivity by reducing costs and optimizing management. The increase in infras-
  tructure capacity is straightforward, by releasing capacity in conventional lines
  and by using the capacity of the high speed ones, otherwise unused at night.

  The contribution to energy efﬁciency and reduction in pollutants, on the
  other side, goes further than the decrease in consumption at the pantograph
  which has been analyzed here. The conventional lines of the study are electri-
  ﬁed at 3kV direct current. This has two main consequences. First, direct current
  is an obstacle for feeding electricity back to the network by regenerative brak-
  ing. It makes necessary that a second train consumes the excess of energy of the
  braking one instantaneously, otherwise, the excess will have to be dissipated ei-
  ther with conventional brakes or with rheostats in the case of electric brakes, and

Transport Policy, Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated, 2011. ProQuest Ebook Central,
  Policies of Railway Interoperability  183

  thus, wasted. Further still, the low voltage of 3kV makes that high power loco-
  motives will require large currents. Thus, the losses from the substation through
  the catenary to the pantograph will increase accordingly. The situation wors-
  ens when considering lower voltage lines, also frequent in Europe, of 1.5kV.
  On the opposite side, high speed lines operate at 2x25kV alternating current,
  which eliminates the problem of regenerative braking and reduces the currents
  signiﬁcantly.

  It must be taken into account that, in spite of these advantages, if freight
  trains are to be commuted from conventional to high speed lines and back, this
  might require either changing locomotives or using dual-voltage ones. In the
  ﬁrst case, this would penalize the gain in travelling times, although if the process
  of changing is optimized, the overall effect could be minor.

  As concluding remarks, we may ﬁrst observe that the success in deploy-
  ing any policy depends critically on it matching reality, particularly in ﬁnancial
  terms. It emerges from the discussion above that sharing the high speed infras-
  tructure for freight trafﬁc might have a high possibility of success because it
  brings together three key points:

Copyright © 2011. Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated. All rights reserved.  • Potential reduction of costs for the operator
  • Creation of new services
  • Improvement of existing ones

  We have extensively commented on the adequacy of these points to Euro-
  pean Policy. However, and more important still, they are positive for rail oper-
  ators on the long term. With adequate strategies and business plans, they could
  derive in more proﬁtable services, which would constitute the ﬁnancial viability
  for the measure.

  As it was indicated earlier in this section, quantifying the full effect of
  freight shift to high speed lines requires evaluating a full policy, deﬁned in all
  its aspects. However, in the light of this study it can be said that the principle
  of such a policy, which is transferring trains from one line to another to cross
  mountains, is sound in terms of:

  • Complying with European policy, and making use of the improving levels
  of interoperability.

  • Optimizing the use of the network.

Transport Policy, Nova Science Publishers, Incorporated, 2011. ProQuest Ebook Central,
  184  Ignacio Lo´pez, Javier Rodr´ıguez, Alberto Garc´ıa et al.

  • Reducing consumption.

  • Reducing costs.

  • Reducing pollutant emissions.

  Designing a full policy based on this measure would require identifying
  paths and time slots which would allow shifting trafﬁc in advantageous condi-
  tions, as well as designing a speciﬁc priority scheme. It would depend critically
  on generating the adequate infrastructure and procedures (operational and reg-
  ulatory) for induced operations, such as possible locomotive changes. Above
  all, it would be necessary for it to be proﬁtable, so it should include a busi-
  ness plan in the case of new services, a set of speciﬁc objectives and indicators
  quantifying improvements in existing services and in energy consumption.
